Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for August 28, 2026.

The TL;DR: SK Biopharmaceuticals is paying Biohaven $400 million in near-term cash for opakalim, a Kv7.2-and-7.3 potassium-channel activator approaching a pivotal focal-epilepsy readout. The mechanism is compelling, but Xenon has already won phase 3 with a rival, so SK is buying speed and commercial fit before seeing Biohaven’s decisive data. Elsewhere in CNS, bankrupt BioXcel has selected Teva’s $57.5 million offer as the baseline bid for its dexmedetomidine agitation franchise. In RNA, BioNTech and Genentech stopped a personalized colorectal-cancer vaccine trial after more deaths occurred in the vaccine arm—an unsettling counterpoint to Moderna’s recent melanoma success—while AusperBio raised $120 million for a hepatitis B antisense drug reporting a 30% functional-cure rate. Flagship’s Mirai Bio is installing permanent leadership around data-driven lipid nanoparticles for delivery beyond the liver. Genentech is also paying DualityBio $45 million upfront for next-generation ADC payloads intended to work after topoisomerase-based ADC resistance. Across the stories, differentiated delivery and clinical context matter at least as much as the payload itself.

Let’s begin in epilepsy, where South Korea’s SK Biopharmaceuticals has licensed Biohaven’s opakalim and the broader Kv7 platform.

SK will pay $350 million upfront and another $50 million next year. Biohaven can receive up to $150 million in development and regulatory milestones, while SK is assuming up to $245 million in obligations owed to the platform’s original developer, Knopp Biosciences. Add everything together and the potential commitment reaches $795 million, plus royalties.

That structure is worth parsing. Four hundred million dollars is near-term, non-dilutive cash—real money that Biohaven receives without selling new shares. The remaining headline value depends on milestones and inherited obligations. This is not merely a biobucks mirage, but neither is SK handing Biohaven $795 million on Friday afternoon.

Opakalim, formerly BHV-7000, is a once-daily oral activator of Kv7.2 and Kv7.3 potassium channels. A phase 2/3 focal-epilepsy study should report later this year.

Kv7.2 and Kv7.3 are encoded by KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 and commonly assemble into heteromeric channels in neurons. They produce what electrophysiologists call the M-current: a slowly activating, non-inactivating potassium conductance that helps stabilize the membrane near resting potential and opposes repeated firing.

The intuitive version is that these channels act as an electrical brake. When a neuron begins depolarizing repeatedly, Kv7 channels allow potassium to leave the cell, making additional action potentials harder to generate. Activating them should reduce pathological network excitability without globally shutting down synaptic transmission.

That is the therapeutic attraction. The historical warning is ezogabine, or retigabine, an older Kv7 activator that worked in epilepsy but was withdrawn after commercial and safety problems, including retinal pigmentation and blue-gray skin discoloration. A modern Kv7 drug needs to preserve seizure control while avoiding those liabilities and minimizing dizziness, somnolence and cognitive slowing.

Biohaven argues that opakalim preferentially activates Kv7.2-and-7.3 heteromers and lacks GABA-receptor activity. That could support a cleaner tolerability profile, but selective pharmacology is only valuable if patients can feel the difference.

And the competitive timing is uncomfortable. Xenon Pharmaceuticals’ azetukalner has already succeeded in a phase 3 focal-seizure trial and is also being tested in generalized epilepsy. SK is therefore paying before opakalim’s pivotal readout for a drug that may enter the market behind another member of its class.

Why take that risk? Because SK already knows epilepsy commercialization. Its drug Xcopri, or cenobamate, was approved in 2019 for focal seizures. Rather than Biohaven building its own neurology sales force, payer operation and physician network, SK can potentially place opakalim into an existing franchise and target a U.S. launch in 2029.

The strategic logic is stronger than the clinical evidence available today. If opakalim delivers competitive seizure reductions with meaningfully better tolerability, SK has bought a natural companion to Xcopri. If the upcoming readout is mediocre, $400 million will look like an expensive act of impatience.

This also continues the ion-channel revival we have been tracking. Jazz recently paid $820 million upfront for Actio’s KCNT1 inhibitor in developmental epilepsy. Latigo raised heavily around Nav1.8 inhibition in pain. Lilly signed an opaque ion-channel antibody deal with OmniAb. After years when CNS investors preferred genetically fashionable targets or broad neuroinflammation stories, membrane excitability is emphatically back.

Staying in neuroscience, BioXcel Therapeutics has filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy and selected Teva as the stalking-horse bidder for its assets.

A stalking horse is the opening qualified buyer in a bankruptcy auction. Teva’s bid sets a floor, discouraging opportunists from offering something absurdly low, but another bidder can still arrive with a better proposal.

Teva is offering $57.5 million upfront and up to $67.5 million in milestones. It has already placed a $5.7 million good-faith deposit.

The centerpiece is BXCL501, a dissolving-film formulation of dexmedetomidine under FDA review for at-home treatment of acute agitation associated with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia. The FDA decision is expected November 14. Approval by that date would trigger the full $67.5 million milestone; later approval would reduce or restructure the payout.

Dexmedetomidine is an alpha-2 adrenergic-receptor agonist best known as a sedative used in monitored medical settings. By activating presynaptic alpha-2 receptors, particularly in noradrenergic systems, it reduces norepinephrine release and dampens autonomic arousal. The attraction in agitation is rapid calming without relying exclusively on antipsychotic dopamine blockade or benzodiazepine-like pharmacology.

The home setting changes the risk calculation. A therapy used under medical supervision can be monitored for excessive sedation, low blood pressure and slow heart rate. At home, labeling, patient selection and caregiver instructions must carry much more of that safety burden.

BioXcel’s approved related product, Igalmi, uses the same basic film approach for agitation in supervised healthcare settings. Expanding into at-home use could enlarge the market substantially. It also requires commercial execution that BioXcel, which recently warned it lacked enough money to operate through August, is no longer able to provide independently.

Teva has an established neuroscience business and can absorb manufacturing and commercialization more easily. For BioXcel employees, however, the distinction between preserving an asset and preserving a company is harsh. Bankruptcy may carry the molecule to an FDA decision. It rarely carries the original organization intact.

Now to personalized mRNA cancer vaccines, where BioNTech and Genentech have produced a sobering counterweight to last week’s Moderna euphoria.

The companies terminated a phase 2 study of autogene cevumeran, or BNT122, in patients with surgically removed stage 2 or 3 colorectal cancer. An independent data-monitoring board identified a numerical overall-survival imbalance. Genentech confirmed that more deaths occurred in the vaccine arm than in the observation arm.

We do not yet know the number of deaths, causes, timing, confidence intervals or whether the imbalance reflects treatment-related toxicity, cancer recurrence or chance. “Numerical imbalance” does not prove the vaccine caused the deaths. It does mean the independent monitors considered the pattern concerning enough that continuing the study was unjustified.

That is serious.

The comparison with Merck and Moderna’s recent phase 3 melanoma win is instructive. Moderna’s intismeran was added to Keytruda, an anti-PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor. BNT122 was being tested here as a standalone postsurgical treatment against observation.

Both products follow the personalized neoantigen-vaccine logic: sequence a patient’s tumor, identify mutations absent from normal tissue, predict which mutant peptides may bind that patient’s HLA molecules and manufacture an mRNA vaccine encoding a selected antigen set. The vaccine should prime or expand mutation-specific T cells capable of finding residual cancer cells.

But antigen presentation is not the same as productive antitumor immunity.

Melanoma is often highly mutated and relatively immunogenic. Colorectal cancer—particularly the common microsatellite-stable form—can be immunologically cold, with weak T-cell infiltration, suppressive myeloid cells, inhibitory stromal architecture and limited response to checkpoint blockade. Giving T cells a molecular wanted poster does not help much if they are poorly activated, cannot enter the tissue or are disabled upon arrival.

A separate BNT122 combination trial remains ongoing. That may be the more appropriate development path, and BioNTech emphasized “novel-novel combinations”—industry language for pairing two experimental approaches rather than simply adding an investigational drug to an established one.

Still, Citi analysts correctly called this a clear setback. BioNTech shares fell about 7.5%, in sharp contrast to Moderna’s 177% one-day rise after its melanoma announcement. The emotional whiplash is useful. Personalized mRNA cancer vaccines are not now generically “validated.” One specific vaccine-plus-checkpoint regimen succeeded in one immunologically favorable cancer. Another vaccine used alone in a colder tumor produced a concerning survival imbalance.

Tumor type, disease stage, immune context and combination partner are part of the therapy.

The more encouraging oligonucleotide news comes from AusperBio Therapeutics, which raised a $120 million Series C for chronic hepatitis B.

The Bay Area company was founded in 2019 and has now raised substantial capital: $37 million in a 2024 Series A, $186 million across a multipart Series B, and this new round led by RA Capital. The money is intended to carry AHB-137 through phase 3 and toward commercialization.

AHB-137 is an unconjugated antisense oligonucleotide designed to reduce hepatitis B viral proteins, inhibit viral DNA replication and potentially permit immune reactivation. In phase 2a, monotherapy produced what Ausper calls a 30% functional-cure rate at week 72, with no drug-related serious adverse events.

For hepatitis B, “functional cure” usually does not mean every trace of viral genetic material has vanished. HBV establishes covalently closed circular DNA, or cccDNA, in hepatocyte nuclei, and viral sequences can also integrate into the host genome. Sterilizing cure is therefore extraordinarily difficult.

Functional cure generally means sustained loss of hepatitis B surface antigen, often with or without development of protective antibodies, while viral DNA remains durably suppressed after treatment. That is clinically meaningful because surface antigen is not merely a diagnostic marker. Persistent high antigen exposure can help exhaust or tolerize HBV-specific immune responses.

An ASO can reduce viral RNA and protein production, lowering that antigenic fog. The hope is that antiviral suppression and reduced protein burden give the patient’s immune system enough room to regain control.

Thirty percent is an impressive early number, but we need the cohort size, baseline antigen levels, prior nucleoside therapy, durability after treatment withdrawal and exact cure definition. Chronic HBV studies are extremely sensitive to patient selection.

Competition is no longer theoretical. GSK’s bepirovirsen, licensed from Ionis, was approved in Japan this week. Ausper therefore needs more than proof that an oligonucleotide can work in hepatitis B. It needs a response rate, dosing scheme and eligible population that can compete with an emerging incumbent.

Ausper is also advancing AHB-171, a liver-targeted siRNA. That gives the company two RNA-silencing architectures: RNase-H-recruiting antisense and RNA-induced-silencing-complex-mediated siRNA. For oligonucleotide scientists, the comparison will be especially interesting because the constructs may differ in cellular uptake, transcript coverage, duration, dose and ability to suppress RNA arising from both cccDNA and integrated viral sequences.

The delivery question brings us to Flagship Pioneering’s Mirai Bio, which has made interim CEO Kate Torchilin permanent CEO and promoted Jagesh Shah to chief scientific officer.

Mirai’s pitch is data-driven lipid-nanoparticle design for nucleic acid delivery beyond the liver. That final phrase—beyond the liver—is where an enormous percentage of RNA-therapy ambition currently goes to become humble.

Systemically administered nanoparticles often accumulate in the liver because of blood flow, vascular anatomy, serum-protein adsorption and uptake by hepatocytes and liver-resident phagocytes. That is wonderful when the target is PCSK9, TMPRSS6 or a hepatic viral transcript. It is less useful when the desired cell is a neuron, muscle stem cell, lung epithelium or a specific immune subset.

Mirai says it combines LNP design, in vivo learning and integrated development to make extrahepatic delivery more predictable. The important words are “in vivo learning.” Particle behavior cannot be inferred reliably from ionizable-lipid structure alone. Protein corona formation, endosomal escape, innate sensing, tissue vasculature and cell-state-dependent uptake all intervene.

For job seekers, leadership expansion at a Flagship company can precede program prioritization and team growth, but it is not automatically a hiring boom. Watch for disclosed tissue targets, reproducible cell-type biodistribution and actual therapeutic programs. A large LNP library is useful. A nanoparticle that reaches the intended cell, escapes the endosome and expresses enough cargo without inflammatory toxicity is the product.

Genentech’s new ADC deal with China’s DualityBio tells a related delivery story using proteins rather than nanoparticles.

Genentech is paying $45 million upfront, with more than $1 billion in potential milestones, for ADCs carrying DualityBio’s Dupac payloads. These payloads are intended to kill tumors that have progressed after existing topoisomerase-inhibitor ADCs such as Enhertu or Trodelvy.

As ADCs move into first-line cancer treatment, resistance after ADC exposure becomes its own market. Tumors may downregulate antigen, alter internalization, increase drug efflux, improve DNA-damage repair or become less sensitive to the payload class. Swapping the antibody while retaining essentially the same topoisomerase poison may not solve payload resistance.

DualityBio has disclosed payload concepts including the mRNA-translation inhibitor DUP5 and ecteinascidin-derived compounds. Genentech will choose targets; DualityBio will build the ADCs and take them through phase 1a in China before Genentech assumes development.

That arrangement captures China’s expanding role with unusual clarity: Chinese discovery, Chinese early clinical speed and Western late-stage development capital. DualityBio has already partnered with BioNTech, GSK and BeOne. Hansoh separately noted this week that China now accounts for roughly 30% of investigational medicines. “Follower to frontrunner” is self-congratulatory corporate phrasing, but the licensing data increasingly support it.

Finally, Flagship’s ProFound Therapeutics will receive $20 million initially and up to $15 million more from the Gates Foundation to study preeclampsia using placental proteomics.

Preeclampsia involves hypertension during pregnancy and can include endothelial dysfunction, kidney injury, liver abnormalities and dangerous outcomes for both mother and fetus. The placenta is central, but the disease is not reducible to one circulating factor.

ProFound plans to compare placentas from affected and unaffected pregnancies, searching its so-called dark-proteome dataset for previously missed biomarkers and targets. It will then use agentic AI to propose functions before testing those predictions experimentally.

Placenta is unusually rich biological material, but there is a timing problem: it is most readily available after delivery, while useful biomarkers must identify disease before catastrophic progression. Targets found in delivered tissue will therefore need validation in longitudinal maternal blood samples and mechanistic models. Otherwise, the platform risks discovering molecular consequences of a diseased placenta rather than actionable causes.

Still, this is an area starved of investment. Only about 1% of global healthcare R&D goes toward female-specific conditions outside cancer. Twenty million dollars upfront for target discovery is meaningful, and preeclampsia is exactly the sort of common, biologically complex disease where unbiased tissue profiling may reveal something conventional candidate-driven research missed.

Today’s stories repeatedly return to the same principle. A Kv7 activator needs the correct neuronal conductance and a competitive tolerability profile. A personalized vaccine needs an immune environment capable of responding. An oligonucleotide needs productive hepatocyte uptake and sufficient antigen reduction. An ADC needs a payload that remains lethal after prior payload exposure. A placental protein signature needs to predict disease before delivery, not merely explain it afterward.

Delivery is not the packaging around the mechanism. Context is not decoration around the target. They are often the difference between an elegant molecule and a useful medicine.

And that’s the roundup for today.
