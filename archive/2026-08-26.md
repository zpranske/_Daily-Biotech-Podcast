Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for August 26, 2026.

The TL;DR: Regenxbio’s Hunter-syndrome gene therapy is back on clinical hold after enhanced MRI monitoring found small, asymptomatic spinal nodules or cystic masses in five patients treated three to six years ago. The findings may be benign and unrelated, but they follow a CNS tumor in a related program and have derailed a planned regulatory resubmission. In glioblastoma, nearly insolvent TME Pharma says two potential partners are evaluating its pegylated mirror-image RNA aptamer targeting CXCL12—and today’s source confusingly misidentifies that target as CCL2. Spruce Biosciences is making manufacturing progress toward filing an intracerebroventricular enzyme replacement for Sanfilippo syndrome type B. Capricor’s Duchenne cell-therapy decision has officially moved to November 22, without resolving its weak efficacy evidence. Outside neurology, Roche is paying $190 million upfront for Hanmi’s urocortin-2 obesity drug, while a mysterious Chinese inflammatory-disease asset is launching Sentivera. Across the stories, long-term safety, manufacturing and access to early-stage capital are becoming the binding constraints.

Let’s begin with Regenxbio, because this is both a neurological gene-therapy safety story and a caution about what intensive imaging can reveal years after treatment.

The FDA has placed RGX-121 on clinical hold. This is Regenxbio’s AAV-based gene therapy for mucopolysaccharidosis type 2, or Hunter syndrome, a rare X-linked lysosomal-storage disease caused by deficiency of iduronate-2-sulfatase.

Normally, iduronate-2-sulfatase helps lysosomes degrade glycosaminoglycans including heparan sulfate and dermatan sulfate. Without enough enzyme, partially degraded material accumulates across tissues. In neuronopathic Hunter syndrome, that includes the central nervous system, producing progressive cognitive and behavioral deterioration alongside systemic disease.

RGX-121 is delivered directly into cerebrospinal fluid, either intracisternally or intraventricularly, with the aim of getting a functional IDS gene into CNS cells and establishing sustained local enzyme production. Direct CSF administration is intended to bypass the blood-brain barrier, which circulating enzyme-replacement therapies cross poorly.

The immediate concern is that spine MRI scans found small nodules or cystic masses in five participants from the Campsiite trial. These patients received RGX-121 approximately three to six years ago. They have no reported symptoms from the findings, investigators classified them as nonserious, and radiologists currently believe they are probably benign. No corresponding brain nodules or masses were identified.

That is reassuring, but not exculpatory.

The scans were added because a patient receiving Regenxbio’s related RGX-111 gene therapy for MPS type 1 developed a central nervous system tumor. In January, the FDA placed both RGX-111 and RGX-121 on hold because the products, diseases and delivery strategies were similar enough that a possible shared risk had to be investigated.

There are at least three live explanations for the new spinal findings. They could be incidental abnormalities that also occur in untreated Hunter syndrome but have never been systematically documented because routine spine MRI is uncommon. They could reflect benign treatment-related tissue changes. Or, less comfortably, they could represent an AAV-associated proliferative process whose biology and clinical trajectory are not yet understood.

The data do not currently tell us which.

This is where advanced imaging creates its own interpretive problem. Once you look more intensively, you find abnormalities. But without a natural-history control group scanned using the same protocol, prevalence and causality become difficult to establish. Five findings are enough to demand investigation, not enough to establish mechanism.

The hold disrupts a regulatory path that had already swung wildly. The FDA rejected Regenxbio’s approval request in February and initially demanded more patients plus an untreated control arm. By June, the agency had relaxed those requirements and agreed to consider a resubmission using existing studies. Regenxbio had planned to refile this quarter. That is now off the near-term calendar.

For gene-therapy scientists, the point to retain is that long-lived expression creates long-lived uncertainty. A one-time treatment can produce benefits for years, but delayed abnormalities may also appear after the ordinary clinical-development window. Regulators increasingly want surveillance systems capable of distinguishing disease natural history from vector, transgene and administration-related effects.

Now to glioblastoma, and a story with unusually direct relevance to aptamer chemistry.

TME Pharma says it has narrowed its search for a NOX-A12 partner to two potential licensees. The Berlin company began the year with only 2 million euros, about $2.3 million, and cannot independently fund the next clinical study. So “two interested parties” is not a victory yet. It is evidence that the company may have an escape route.

First, an important correction. Today’s article calls NOX-A12 a CCL2 inhibitor. It is not. Olaptesed pegol, or NOX-A12, is a pegylated RNA aptamer that binds CXCL12, also called stromal-cell-derived factor 1. TME’s other aptamer, emapticap pegol or NOX-E36, targets CCL2. The names are similar; the chemokines are not interchangeable.

NOX-A12 belongs to a particularly elegant class called Spiegelmers—literally “mirror molecules.” Conventional biological RNA is built from D-ribose nucleotides. A Spiegelmer uses the mirror-image L configuration. Most endogenous nucleases evolved to recognize normal D-RNA, so L-RNA is highly resistant to enzymatic degradation while retaining the ability to fold into a target-binding three-dimensional structure.

The selection strategy is clever. Because ordinary proteins and L-RNA have opposite chirality, developers can select conventional D-RNA against a mirror-image version of the protein target, then synthesize the winning sequence as L-RNA. The resulting Spiegelmer should bind the natural protein. PEGylation then increases molecular size and circulation time.

In glioblastoma, CXCL12 signaling through CXCR4 and ACKR3, also known as CXCR7, helps organize the tumor microenvironment. It can recruit immunosuppressive myeloid cells, support vascular recovery after radiation and create spatial niches that protect malignant cells. Binding extracellular CXCL12 is intended to disrupt those signals rather than kill tumor cells directly.

TME tested NOX-A12 with radiation and Roche’s anti-VEGF antibody Avastin in phase 1/2. That combination makes mechanistic sense: radiation injures the tumor, Avastin suppresses VEGF-driven vascular support, and CXCL12 blockade may interfere with compensatory vascular and immune-cell recruitment.

But TME is now touting potential peak sales of $1 billion before it has a phase 2 partner. Treat that number as an aspiration generated by a cash-constrained seller. The more relevant near-term question is whether either suitor will provide enough upfront money to run a credible controlled study.

The company is also still seeking a partner for NOX-E36, its actual CCL2-binding Spiegelmer, after the Singapore Eye Research Institute withdrew from an ophthalmology collaboration. For an aptamer specialist, this is a revealing platform case: nuclease-resistant stereochemistry is genuinely differentiated, but exquisite chemistry does not finance clinical trials by itself.

Another neurodegenerative lysosomal program is moving in a more constructive direction. Spruce Biosciences says two FDA meetings support a fourth-quarter filing for tralesinidase alfa in Sanfilippo syndrome type B, or MPS IIIB.

MPS IIIB results from deficiency of alpha-N-acetylglucosaminidase, abbreviated NAGLU. That blocks one step in heparan-sulfate degradation, leading to lysosomal accumulation that is particularly destructive in the brain. Children can initially develop relatively normally, then experience behavioral dysregulation, sleep disturbance, language loss, cognitive decline and progressive neurological deterioration.

Tralesinidase alfa is an engineered NAGLU enzyme fused to an insulin-like-growth-factor-2-derived targeting element. That element engages the cation-independent mannose-6-phosphate receptor, helping cells internalize the enzyme and deliver it to lysosomes. Because a systemically administered enzyme would not adequately cross the blood-brain barrier, the therapy is delivered intracerebroventricularly.

The clinical package appears sufficiently mature that manufacturing has become the principal uncertainty. Spruce transferred production to a large biologics manufacturer, and the FDA has now found its analytical-comparability strategy reasonable. Comparability asks whether material produced by the new process is sufficiently similar in identity, purity, potency and structural characteristics to the material used in clinical studies.

That sounds administrative until one remembers that complex recombinant proteins can change with cell line, bioreactor conditions, purification and formulation. Glycosylation may affect receptor uptake, tissue distribution and stability. “Same amino-acid sequence” does not automatically mean “same medicine.”

Spruce is also evaluating larger bioreactors and a second supplier. Analysts consider the clinical evidence comparatively de-risked, but manufacturing risk remains difficult to price. If approved, this could become the first FDA-authorized therapy for MPS IIIB, a disorder affecting roughly one in 200,000 newborns.

Capricor, meanwhile, has received the expected three-month extension for FDA review of deramiocel. The new decision date is November 22.

We discussed the scientific problem last week, so the update is simple: more review time, not stronger evidence.

After an advisory committee voted nine to three against efficacy in Duchenne cardiomyopathy, Capricor refocused its proposed indication on upper-limb function and supplied another year of follow-up. The FDA accepted the amendment and extended its clock.

The phase 3 trial did not show a statistically significant and clinically meaningful benefit on the prespecified upper-limb analysis, according to the agency. Capricor produced an alternative statistically significant analysis, but the FDA said it was difficult to interpret. Longer open-label follow-up can suggest durability; it cannot recreate the randomized comparison that was missing or ambiguous.

November 22 is therefore a reprieve. Approval remains possible, especially in a devastating disease with limited options, but the filing is still an exercise in persuading the FDA that a fragile dataset contains a real functional effect.

Now to obesity, where Roche is paying Hanmi Pharm $190 million upfront for global rights outside South Korea to HM17321, a urocortin-2 analog. Another $2.3 billion is available in milestones.

Urocortin-2 is a peptide ligand that preferentially activates corticotropin-releasing-factor receptor 2. That system influences cardiovascular physiology, energy metabolism and skeletal muscle. Earlier UCN2 work concentrated partly on heart failure; Lilly discontinued a heart-failure program in 2019. Hanmi is now repositioning the biology around weight loss with preservation of lean mass.

That is a major competitive frontier. GLP-1-based drugs reduce fat impressively, but some of the lost weight is lean tissue. The exact clinical harm varies—some lean-mass reduction is expected whenever body weight falls—but older or frail patients cannot casually donate muscle to the scale.

Hanmi reports preclinical weight reduction with HM17321 alone and in combination with incretins. The candidate is now in phase 1. Roche also has emugrobart, a myostatin-pathway antibody being combined with Mounjaro, plus GLP-1, GIP and amylin programs.

The insider read is that Roche is building multiple ways to claim better body composition, not merely lower body weight. But $190 million upfront is still a substantial payment for phase 1 biology whose muscle-preservation claim remains largely preclinical.

The deal also fits the global asset-shopping thread. Hanmi is South Korean, while newly formed Sentivera is paying China’s Haisco Pharmaceutical $40 million upfront, almost $36 million in Sentivera equity and up to $1.46 billion in milestones for a small molecule targeting type 2 inflammatory disease.

We know almost nothing about the target. That matters. “Type 2 inflammation” spans asthma, eczema and other disorders involving cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13, eosinophils, mast cells and barrier-tissue responses. Dupixent has already demonstrated that this biology can support a vast franchise. It has also raised the differentiation bar.

Sentivera was created by Arch Venture Partners and Population Health Partners, the backers behind Metsera, only 12 days after Pfizer closed its $10 billion takeover of that obesity biotech. This is asset-centric company creation at high speed: assemble experienced investors, license one potentially scalable molecule, finance it aggressively and build the organization around it.

Haisco also receives a 17.5% stake, and its billionaire chair contributed $5 million to Sentivera’s $83 million financing. That is more integrated than a simple regional license. The Chinese originator retains Greater China rights while participating in the Western company’s upside.

Texas Congressman Nathaniel Moran offered a useful policy counterpoint today. Rather than focusing only on blocking Chinese deals, he argues that the United States should examine the inputs allowing China to move so quickly: early funding, trial infrastructure, active pharmaceutical ingredients and manufacturing capacity.

That connects directly to Massachusetts’ latest biotech report. The state raised $3.45 billion in venture capital during the first half of 2026, up 25%, and produced eight IPOs—the most since 2021. Yet the average seed round fell nearly 40%, from $7.6 million to $4.6 million, while Massachusetts lost approximately 3,600 biopharma R&D jobs during 2025.

For scientists entering industry, that apparent contradiction is the labor market. Capital is flowing into clinical companies with obvious milestones. The earliest research organizations are receiving smaller checks, and mature companies continue consolidating teams. Eight IPOs do not automatically restore 3,600 jobs.

If policymakers want to reduce dependence on foreign innovation, restricting licenses addresses only one end of the pipeline. The harder project is maintaining the academic research, seed financing, translational infrastructure and workforce that generate competitive assets five or ten years later.

Today’s stories make that chain unusually visible. A Spiegelmer aptamer can be chemically ingenious and still require a rescue partner. A CNS gene therapy can approach filing, then be stopped by MRI findings years after dosing. An enzyme replacement can have persuasive biology but remain hostage to manufacturing comparability. And a well-financed obesity program can cross continents because Roche believes someone else found a differentiated mechanism first.

Drug development is a long supply chain of ideas, molecules, assays, factories, trials and trust. Weakness at any one link eventually appears in the clinic—or on the balance sheet.

And that’s the roundup for today.
