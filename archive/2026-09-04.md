Across these stories, the recurring problem is not identifying a compelling target. It is delivering enough intervention, to the right cells, at the right developmental or disease stage—and then measuring something that matters.

Ultragenyx’s Angelman failure is where we need to begin.

The phase 3 Aspire study tested apazunersen in people with Angelman syndrome, a severe neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by profound speech impairment, motor and balance problems, intellectual disability, sleep disruption and often seizures.

Apazunersen missed the primary endpoint, change from baseline in the Bayley-4 cognitive raw score. It also missed the secondary endpoint based on the Multidomain Responder Index, a composite intended to capture functional improvement across several clinically relevant domains.

Ultragenyx disclosed no effect sizes, confidence intervals or patient-level breakdown. That is frustrating, but the corporate language was unusually direct. There was no attempt to celebrate an exploratory subgroup or introduce a newly assembled scale. CEO Emil Kakkis simply called the result disappointing and said the company is reconsidering the program.

Ultragenyx shares fell 43% after hours.

The biology here is worth walking through because Angelman syndrome presents one of genetic medicine’s most attractive rescue mechanisms. UBE3A encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase, a protein that helps attach ubiquitin to selected substrates and thereby regulates their trafficking, signaling or degradation. In neurons, UBE3A contributes to synaptic development and plasticity.

Most people inherit one UBE3A allele from each parent. In neurons, however, the paternal allele is normally silenced by a long antisense transcript called UBE3A-ATS. The maternal allele therefore supplies most neuronal UBE3A protein. If that maternal copy is deleted or dysfunctional, the paternal gene remains physically present but transcriptionally suppressed.

Apazunersen is an antisense oligonucleotide designed to inhibit the silencing transcript. Remove UBE3A-ATS, and the paternal allele should wake up.

It is molecularly beautiful. The phase 3 result reminds us that molecular correction and developmental rescue are not interchangeable.

If UBE3A is absent while neural circuits are forming, restoring it years later may improve ongoing synaptic function without reconstructing every circuit that developed abnormally. There may be age-dependent or cell-type-dependent therapeutic windows. The amount of UBE3A restored could also matter: partial unsilencing across some CNS regions may produce robust target engagement in cerebrospinal-fluid biomarkers while remaining insufficient in the neurons governing speech, motor planning or cognition.

Then there is measurement. The Bayley Scales were developed primarily to assess early childhood development. Raw scores can track acquisition of skills, but Angelman populations have wide differences in genotype, age and baseline function, and progress may occur in small, uneven steps. A multidomain responder index can capture broader change, but composites also create statistical and interpretive complexity. If a therapy makes sleep or attention better without producing measurable cognitive acquisition during the trial window, families may notice something real while the primary endpoint remains negative.

That possibility deserves investigation. It does not rescue a trial that missed both reported efficacy measures.

This matters beyond Ultragenyx. Ionis is developing ION582, and Oak Hill Bio recently began phase 3 testing of Roche-originated rugonersen. The field now has a difficult read-through: is this one molecule, dose or trial design failing, or is postnatal UBE3A reactivation less therapeutically powerful than preclinical models suggested?

Ultragenyx’s separate Aurora study spans additional genotypes and age groups, but analysts already view its prospects more skeptically. Even if Aurora somehow succeeds, commercialization could become difficult if benefit appears restricted to a narrowly defined subgroup.

The new programs target PRAME and MAGE-A4, tumor-associated antigens expressed across melanoma, lung, ovarian, head-and-neck and other cancers. Unlike a CAR, which usually recognizes a surface protein directly, an engineered T-cell receptor can recognize intracellular proteins after peptides are processed and displayed by HLA molecules. That opens a much larger antigen universe, but it also makes efficacy dependent on HLA type, antigen processing and stable peptide presentation.

TScan wants to engineer these TCR-T cells inside the patient using lentiviral delivery. Permanent genomic integration could allow engineered cells to proliferate and form memory populations, potentially creating long-term tumor surveillance without individualized ex vivo manufacturing.

That is ambitious. It is also a formidable selectivity problem. The vector must reach the intended T cells in vivo, express the correct receptor at a controlled level and avoid problematic integration or transduction of unintended cells. Solid tumors then add antigen heterogeneity, T-cell exclusion and a suppressive microenvironment.

This resembles ArsenalBio’s pivot earlier this week: another advanced cell-therapy company shrinking dramatically while promising that in vivo engineering will solve the cost and manufacturing problems of ex vivo therapy. AbbVie, by contrast, has generated convincing late-stage evidence with an off-the-shelf immune-cell redirector.

In the 393-patient phase 3 Cervino trial, the BCMA-targeted bispecific T-cell engager etentamig produced a 74% objective response rate in relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma, compared with 45.7% for investigator-selected standard therapies. Patients had received a median of three prior treatment lines and had already been exposed to a proteasome inhibitor, an immunomodulatory drug and an anti-CD38 antibody.

Etentamig reduced the risk of progression or death by 60%. At 12 months, overall survival was 87.9%, versus 72% in the control group, although the prespecified statistical boundary for overall survival had not yet been crossed.

BCMA is expressed on plasma cells, including malignant myeloma cells. Etentamig binds BCMA with one part of the molecule and CD3 on T cells with another, physically forcing an immune synapse. That makes it an off-the-shelf alternative to BCMA CAR-T, without leukapheresis and personalized manufacturing.

The particularly useful feature may be operational. Etentamig uses one step-up dose followed by monthly intravenous treatment. Severe infections remained common—27.7% versus 19.2% on standard therapy—but fatal infections were numerically lower, 1.5% versus 3.1%.

Do not call that infection profile benign. Deep plasma-cell and immune disruption has consequences. GSK is making another kind of conjugate bet, paying Hutchmed $110 million upfront for HMPL-A830, a preclinical KRAS-EGFR antibody conjugate. Milestones could raise the deal to $1.295 billion, but the upfront is the number to remember.

This is not a conventional ADC carrying a broadly cytotoxic payload. The construct is intended to deliver a KRAS inhibitor selectively to EGFR-expressing tumor cells while also blocking EGFR signaling.

That is a clever attempt to solve two problems simultaneously. KRAS inhibitors can face dose-limiting systemic exposure and adaptive signaling through receptor tyrosine kinases. EGFR activity is particularly relevant in colorectal cancer, where feedback reactivation helps explain why KRAS G12C inhibitors often need to be combined with an EGFR antibody.

By using EGFR as both a tumor address and a signaling target, HMPL-A830 could concentrate the KRAS inhibitor where it is needed while suppressing one major escape pathway. Colorectal, pancreatic and lung cancers are the initial intended indications.

Teva has better human evidence for its anti-IL-15 antibody, TEV ’408, in celiac disease.

In a 50-person phase 2a trial, participants received antibody or placebo, then began eating gluten daily for six weeks. TEV ’408 significantly reduced gluten-induced intestinal damage and met the primary endpoint, with no identified safety signal.

IL-15 is produced in the intestinal epithelial environment and helps activate cytotoxic intraepithelial lymphocytes. In celiac disease, gluten-derived peptides are deamidated by tissue transglutaminase and presented by HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 to CD4 T cells. IL-15 contributes another crucial signal, licensing cytotoxic cells to attack stressed enterocytes. The villi flatten, absorptive surface area disappears and patients can develop diarrhea, nutritional deficiencies or systemic symptoms.

Blocking IL-15 therefore does not teach the immune system to tolerate gluten. It interrupts part of the machinery converting gluten recognition into epithelial destruction.

That distinction matters commercially. Patients may still need a gluten-free diet, but an effective drug could protect against accidental exposure or perhaps support controlled dietary liberalization. Teva has not released histology numbers, symptom data or dose-response details, so it is too early to know how much protection was achieved. The company still needs dose-ranging work before phase 3.

Finally, Novartis has signed an option deal worth up to $3.2 billion for Alteogen’s ALT-B4 subcutaneous-delivery technology. No upfront was disclosed, and comparable Alteogen agreements involved only about $20 million upfront, so the giant number is almost certainly dominated by options and milestones.

ALT-B4 is a recombinant hyaluronidase. Hyaluronan forms a hydrated extracellular-matrix network beneath the skin that limits how quickly a large injected fluid volume can disperse. Temporarily degrading that barrier allows larger doses of biologics to be administered subcutaneously rather than through a prolonged intravenous infusion.

This is not glamorous discovery biology. It can still transform a product. Subcutaneous administration means shorter clinic visits, fewer infusion chairs and potentially at-home treatment. Merck has already used Alteogen’s technology for subcutaneous Keytruda, while Biogen, GSK and AstraZeneca have also signed deals.

For protein and glycobiology scientists, the hyaluronan connection is a nice reminder that extracellular matrix is part of drug delivery. You can engineer the perfect antibody and still lose commercially because the infusion takes hours and consumes specialized staff.

The shared lesson is unusually concrete. Target selection may begin the program. Delivery, timing, clinical measurement and operational usability determine whether anyone gets to finish it.
