Good morning. Here’s your Fierce Biotech update for October 23, 2026.

The TL;DR: Merck and Moderna have finally released the numbers behind their phase 3 melanoma win. Adding the personalized mRNA vaccine intismeran to Keytruda raised two-year recurrence-free survival from 70% to 82.2%, a strong result approaching optimistic expectations. Protection against distant metastasis was also significant, but weaker than the unusually impressive phase 2 signal. In multiple sclerosis, Roche’s brain-penetrant BTK inhibitor fenebrutinib is showing effects on MRI-defined chronic active lesions, although the reported reduction in disability progression is only numerical so far. Roche, meanwhile, has killed another allogeneic CAR-T and an Alzheimer’s antisense program. And proposed U.S. investment restrictions on Chinese biotech are broad on paper but deliberately preserve ordinary drug licensing—the cross-border engine behind many of this year’s largest deals.

Let’s begin with the melanoma vaccine, because this is the detailed dataset everyone wanted after August’s topline announcement.

In the phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial, 82.2% of patients receiving Moderna’s intismeran autogene plus Merck’s Keytruda were alive without melanoma recurrence at two years. The corresponding figure with Keytruda alone was 70%.

The hazard ratio was 0.58. In plain language, the combination reduced the instantaneous risk of recurrence or death by 42% over the observed follow-up period. A hazard ratio is not quite the same thing as saying 42% fewer people experienced recurrence by one specific date, but here the hazard ratio and the 12.2-percentage-point separation in two-year recurrence-free survival tell a consistent story.

This is a strong phase 3 result.

Intismeran is a personalized mRNA cancer vaccine. After a patient’s melanoma is surgically removed, investigators sequence the tumor and compare it with normal DNA to identify somatic mutations—changes present in the cancer but absent from healthy tissue. Computational algorithms then predict which mutation-derived peptides, or neoantigens, are most likely to bind that patient’s HLA molecules and become visible to T cells.

Moderna manufactures an individualized mRNA construct encoding a selected set of those neoantigens. Cells take up the mRNA, translate the encoded sequences and present fragments through antigen-presentation pathways. The goal is to expand T cells capable of recognizing microscopic residual melanoma cells carrying those mutations.

Keytruda blocks PD-1, an inhibitory receptor on activated T cells. The vaccine supplies molecular wanted posters; Keytruda makes it harder for the tumor to switch off the responding officers.

That combination is important. BioNTech’s recent fixed-antigen HPV vaccine failed to improve progression-free survival when added to Keytruda in head-and-neck cancer, and BioNTech and Genentech stopped a personalized colorectal-cancer vaccine trial after a concerning survival imbalance. The current evidence does not validate mRNA cancer vaccination everywhere. It validates one personalized vaccine-checkpoint combination in resected, high-risk melanoma, a cancer that is comparatively immunogenic and often carries many mutations.

The secondary endpoint was less spectacular. At two years, distant metastasis-free survival was 88.5% with intismeran and Keytruda, versus 80.2% with Keytruda alone. The hazard ratio was 0.62, representing a 38% reduction in distant metastasis or death.

That is clinically meaningful. It is also weaker than phase 2, where the initial risk reduction was 65% and the five-year analysis showed 59%. Early trials with fewer patients often overestimate effect sizes through statistical variation, patient selection or unusually favorable baseline balance. Phase 3 commonly brings the number back toward Earth.

Distant metastasis may be more consequential than local recurrence because metastatic disease is more directly connected to mortality and systemic treatment. So the softer secondary result should temper the victory lap, not cancel it. The total package still looks persuasive: substantial recurrence prevention, meaningful protection from distant spread and a mechanism supported by vaccine-induced T-cell responses.

The next questions are durability, overall survival and manufacturing. Every patient requires tumor sequencing, antigen selection, individualized RNA production, quality control and delivery while the postoperative treatment window remains open. Moderna recently rehired a manufacturing executive who helped scale its COVID operations. This dataset explains why. A personalized vaccine is partly an immunology product and partly an extremely elaborate logistics company.

Now to multiple sclerosis, where Roche is making the case that fenebrutinib does more than suppress relapses.

Fenebrutinib is an oral inhibitor of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase, or BTK. BTK is best known for transmitting signals downstream of the B-cell receptor. Inhibiting it can reduce B-cell activation without necessarily eliminating B cells outright, unlike anti-CD20 antibodies such as Roche’s Ocrevus.

BTK is also active in myeloid-lineage cells, including macrophages and microglia. That creates the possibility of influencing inflammatory biology inside the central nervous system—provided the drug reaches sufficient brain exposure.

At an MS meeting, Roche reported that fenebrutinib reduced the volume of slowly expanding lesions by as much as 30% and reduced accumulation of paramagnetic rim lesions by 62% in relapsing MS.

These MRI features are attempts to visualize chronic active inflammation. A conventional enhancing lesion reflects relatively acute blood-brain-barrier disruption. A paramagnetic rim lesion contains iron-associated, activated myeloid cells around the edge of an older lesion. Slowly expanding lesions enlarge gradually, suggesting persistent tissue injury at their margins even when a patient is not having an obvious relapse.

For a microscopy-minded listener, think of this as spatial pathology translated into clinical MRI: the lesion center contains established damage, while the biologically active frontier remains at the edge. It is not cellular-resolution imaging, but the rim acts as a mesoscopic signature of compartmentalized inflammation.

Roche calls this the first prospective phase 3 demonstration that a therapy affects chronic active lesions. That is intriguing, particularly in progressive MS, where disability can worsen independently of overt relapses.

But the patient-level result is less definitive. Pooling two relapsing-MS trials, fenebrutinib produced a 16% numerical reduction in 12-week confirmed disability progression. “Numerical” usually signals that the analysis did not establish statistical significance. The drug also reduced total brain-volume loss by 23%, another supportive measure rather than a direct account of how well patients walk, use their hands or function independently.

Fenebrutinib could become a blockbuster if it combines oral dosing, relapse control and a convincing effect on progression. Whether physicians move it ahead of anti-CD20 therapy will depend on disability data, liver safety, infections and comparative efficacy—not just attractive lesion images. The FDA has accepted filings in relapsing and primary progressive MS, with a potential launch next year.

Staying with excitability and CNS pharmacology, Rapport Therapeutics has succeeded in bipolar mania—but by less than it previously said would be enough.

RAP-219 improved Young Mania Rating Scale scores by 3.1 points over placebo after three weeks. The result was statistically significant and supported by secondary measures. Rapport plans phase 3 trials next year.

The awkward part is that management and analysts had identified four points as the clinically meaningful threshold before seeing the data. Existing treatments have produced placebo-adjusted improvements of roughly four to seven points. After obtaining 3.1, the company now says the result clearly meets clinical meaningfulness.

That is goalpost mobility, even if the drug truly has activity.

RAP-219 targets TARP-gamma-8, an auxiliary protein associated with AMPA-type glutamate receptors. AMPA receptors mediate much of the brain’s rapid excitatory synaptic transmission. TARPs regulate receptor trafficking, channel behavior and synaptic localization. Gamma-8 is enriched in forebrain regions including the hippocampus and cortex.

By binding the receptor through this auxiliary subunit, RAP-219 is intended to reduce pathological excitatory signaling more selectively than a global AMPA-receptor blocker. That could theoretically dampen seizure networks or manic hyperexcitability without broadly suppressing every AMPA synapse and producing intolerable sedation, motor impairment or cognitive slowing.

The epilepsy application is mechanistically straightforward: reduce excessive synchronous firing. Bipolar mania is more complicated. Mania involves distributed changes in arousal, reward, sleep, impulsivity and cognition; it is not simply a cortical seizure stretched over several weeks.

A statistically successful 3.1-point effect establishes clinical proof of concept. It does not yet establish parity with effective antipsychotics or mood stabilizers. Phase 3 must reproduce the result, establish a favorable tolerability profile and show whether the drug can prevent relapse during maintenance treatment. A slightly weaker acute effect could still be commercially useful if patients tolerate chronic dosing much better. But Rapport should be judged against the threshold it stated before the envelope was opened.

Our most relevant chemical-biology financing story comes from Lycia Therapeutics, which is seeking nearly $228 million in an IPO for extracellular protein degraders.

Lycia’s lead candidate, LCA-0061, is a catalytic lysosomal-targeting chimera, or cataLYTAC, designed to remove IgE for food allergy.

A LYTAC is a bifunctional molecule. One portion binds the extracellular protein to be eliminated. Another recruits a cell-surface receptor that naturally internalizes cargo and sends it toward lysosomes. The target is therefore physically rerouted from extracellular space into the cell’s degradative compartment.

This differs from PROTACs, which usually act inside cells and recruit an E3 ubiquitin ligase to send a cytosolic protein to the proteasome. It also differs from an ordinary neutralizing antibody. Xolair binds free IgE and prevents it from loading onto the high-affinity Fc-epsilon receptor on mast cells and basophils. Lycia wants to actively remove total and free IgE.

In food allergy, IgE bound to mast cells primes those cells for rapid activation when allergen crosslinks adjacent IgE-receptor complexes. Histamine, lipid mediators and proteases are released, potentially causing hives, bronchoconstriction, gastrointestinal symptoms or anaphylaxis.

Lycia reports rapid, deep and sustained IgE reduction in phase 1, without dose-limiting toxicity so far. Numbers remain essential. Deeper IgE depletion could improve protection and overcome the dosing limitations Xolair faces in patients with very high baseline IgE. It could also interfere more substantially with antiparasitic immunity and other normal functions.

The company wants $80 million for phase 2 development of LCA-0061 and another $70 million for a follow-on IgE degrader with different dosing characteristics. It is also developing LCA-0321 to selectively degrade TSH-receptor autoantibodies in Graves’ disease.

That Graves program is conceptually even more precise: eliminate the pathogenic antibody rather than suppressing all B cells or reducing broad IgG classes. The technical challenge is capturing a polyclonal population of disease-driving antibodies without binding useful immunoglobulins or perturbing the receptor itself. If Lycia can do that reproducibly, extracellular degradation becomes more than an elegant disposal trick. It becomes programmable immunology.

Finally, Treasury’s proposed China-investment rules are sweeping in vocabulary but narrower in their practical target.

The rules classify an enormous range of pharmaceutical modalities as sensitive technologies, including small molecules, antibodies, vaccines, cell and gene therapies and delivery systems. Certain U.S. equity investments, convertible debt, joint ventures, corporate expansions and limited-partner investments involving China could be prohibited.

Ordinary licensing is largely spared. A U.S. or European company can still pay a Chinese biotech upfront cash, milestones and royalties for ex-China rights to a drug. Fee-for-service R&D collaborations also generally remain possible.

This preserves the transaction model behind Roche’s Hansoh ADC partnership, Novo’s Hengrui obesity license and many other deals we have tracked. Treasury is drawing a line between buying a drug and financing or jointly owning the foreign company or foundational technology.

The practical complications will appear around NewCos, equity stakes and joint ventures. If a Chinese company licenses an asset into a newly created Western biotech and retains substantial ownership, the transaction may require much more careful legal analysis. Business-development teams can continue shopping for molecules. Venture funds and company builders cannot assume that every equity structure remains permissible.

The industry has received a measured reprieve, not a return to geopolitical innocence.
