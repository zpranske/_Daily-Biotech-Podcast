Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for September 2, 2026.

The TL;DR: The autoimmune CAR-T boom has encountered its most serious safety warning yet. Novartis paused a broad CD19 CAR-T program after three patients died from immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, an overwhelming inflammatory reaction related to HLH. Bristol Myers Squibb has also paused enrollment with a similar therapy after inflammatory events. That arrives just as ArsenalBio is laying off 99 people and abandoning its ex vivo solid-tumor CAR-T programs to join the already crowded in vivo CAR-T race. Elsewhere, Takeda’s newly approved orexin agonist is being called the “GLP-1 of neuroscience,” which is an eye-catching comparison built on fascinating arousal biology but very little evidence outside narcolepsy. Lilly is buying Merida Biosciences for up to $2.875 billion to selectively eliminate pathogenic autoantibodies, Roche has licensed another Chinese trispecific B-cell engager, and RNA therapeutics face two revealing tests in transthyretin cardiomyopathy and seasonal influenza.

Let’s start with the safety signal in autoimmune CAR-T, because this could reset expectations across one of biotech’s hottest fields.

Novartis has temporarily stopped screening, randomization and treatment across multiple studies of rapcabtagene autoleucel, or rap-cel, after three deaths from immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, abbreviated IEC-HS.

Rap-cel is an autologous CD19-directed CAR-T. Investigators collect a patient’s T cells, engineer them to recognize CD19 and return them to the patient so they can eliminate B cells. Novartis’ T-Charge manufacturing platform minimizes extended culture outside the body, potentially preserving a less exhausted, more proliferative T-cell population.

That potency may be useful. It can also become dangerous.

IEC-HS is related to hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, or HLH: a state in which activated immune cells and macrophages create a self-amplifying inflammatory storm. Patients can develop extremely high ferritin, cytopenias, liver dysfunction, coagulopathy, fever and multiorgan failure. It overlaps with cytokine-release syndrome but can emerge with a somewhat different timing and inflammatory profile. The phrase “immune effector cell-associated” tells you that this is not ordinary background autoimmunity. It is a complication of intensely activated therapeutic immune cells.

The paused Novartis studies span lupus, lupus nephritis, systemic sclerosis, vasculitis, inflammatory myopathies, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s disease, myasthenia gravis and both relapsing and progressive multiple sclerosis. Its oncology program is continuing.

That distinction is important. In aggressive leukemia or lymphoma, physicians and patients may accept substantial acute risk because the untreated disease can kill quickly. In rheumatoid arthritis or multiple sclerosis, especially before all established therapies have failed, three treatment-related inflammatory deaths radically change the calculation.

Bristol Myers Squibb has separately paused enrollment in autoimmune trials of another autologous CD19 CAR-T, zola-cel, after what it called transient and reversible inflammatory events. Those events are not equivalent to the Novartis deaths, but the timing will make regulators examine the entire class more carefully: conditioning chemotherapy, CAR expansion kinetics, B-cell depletion depth, baseline inflammatory state and whether autoimmune patients respond differently from oncology patients.

This does not erase the striking remissions reported after deep B-cell depletion. The biological hypothesis remains compelling: remove autoreactive B cells thoroughly enough, allow the repertoire to regenerate, and perhaps reset the disease rather than continuously suppressing it. But “immune reset” is now colliding with the reality that the reset mechanism can overwhelm the patient.

Keep that in mind as Roche licenses SIM0660 from China’s Simcere Zaiming. Roche is paying $75 million upfront, with the total package reaching as much as $1.53 billion including milestones. That means the upfront is real but the billion-plus headline is overwhelmingly contingent biobucks.

SIM0660 is a preclinical trispecific antibody engaging CD79a, CD19 and CD3. CD19 and CD79a provide two addresses on the B-cell lineage, while CD3 recruits T cells to kill those B cells. Dual B-cell recognition may increase coverage after prior CD19- or CD20-directed treatment and reduce escape through loss of one marker.

Simcere says the construct is designed to limit cytokine release. Every T-cell engager would like to say that; human data get the deciding vote. The therapeutic tension is intrinsic. You need enough immune synapse formation and T-cell activation to eliminate B cells deeply, but not so much systemic activation that inflammatory toxicity becomes unacceptable.

Roche has already discontinued a CD19-by-CD3 lupus bispecific because the overall profile was inadequate. Boulevard Bio recently licensed a CD19-by-BCMA-by-CD3 trispecific, and multiple companies are pursuing in vivo CAR-T for similar diseases. So SIM0660 enters a crowded experiment comparing three architectures: off-the-shelf antibodies, patient-specific ex vivo cell therapy and nanoparticles that manufacture CAR-T cells inside the patient.

The Novartis deaths raise the value of controllability. An antibody can usually be stopped and allowed to clear. An expanding cellular population is harder to call back.

That brings us to ArsenalBio, where the strategic whiplash is severe.

The company is laying off 99 employees, representing most of its remaining workforce, stopping development of its ex vivo clinical programs and seeking new homes for existing assets. ArsenalBio had already cut its staff roughly in half last year, leaving 127 employees. It once raised $325 million in a single financing, with Bristol Myers Squibb and Regeneron among its backers, to engineer sophisticated CAR-T therapies for solid tumors.

Solid tumors remain brutal territory for CAR-T. Target antigens are heterogeneous and often shared with healthy tissue. Engineered cells must leave the bloodstream, penetrate abnormal vasculature and dense extracellular matrix, survive a hypoxic and metabolically hostile microenvironment, and continue functioning amid suppressive myeloid cells and inhibitory cytokines. Blood cancers are comparatively accessible: malignant B cells circulate or occupy lymphoid tissues and display lineage markers such as CD19 or BCMA.

ArsenalBio is now pivoting to in vivo CAR-T, where genetic cargo is delivered directly to T cells inside the patient rather than through bespoke manufacturing. The attraction is obvious: no leukapheresis, less centralized manufacturing and potentially an off-the-shelf product. But Lilly, Johnson & Johnson, AbbVie, Bristol Myers, AstraZeneca and Kite are already spending heavily there.

A pivot does not mean ArsenalBio has solved in vivo delivery. It means its ex vivo programs no longer justified the organization required to support them. For job seekers, 99 layoffs across functions are a reminder that platform knowledge may survive while the platform team does not. In vivo delivery specialists may eventually be recruited. Most of the current company is leaving first.

Now for a more optimistic CNS story: orexin agonism.

Takeda won FDA approval in August for oveporexton, sold as Orzeyful, in narcolepsy type 1. Some researchers are now describing orexin drugs as the “GLP-1s of neuroscience”—a phrase that is scientifically suggestive, commercially irresistible and currently much too confident.

Narcolepsy type 1 is associated with the loss of hypothalamic neurons that make orexin, also called hypocretin. These neurons do not merely generate generic wakefulness. They integrate circadian state, sleep pressure, metabolism, emotion and environmental salience, then stabilize arousal by recruiting downstream noradrenergic, histaminergic, serotonergic and dopaminergic systems.

Without sufficient orexin signaling, the boundary between sleep and wakefulness becomes unstable. Patients can experience overwhelming daytime sleepiness and cataplexy, the sudden loss of muscle tone often triggered by emotion. REM-sleep-like features intrude into wakefulness.

Oveporexton is an oral agonist of orexin receptor 2. Rather than broadly stimulating the brain with amphetamine-like catecholamine release, it attempts to replace the missing organizing signal. That is unusually satisfying disease biology: a defined neuronal population disappears, a neuropeptide signal is lost, and receptor agonism restores part of the circuit output.

The broader claims involve idiopathic hypersomnia, fatigue, cognition, attention and perhaps mood. Plausible, yes. Proven, no.

Orexin neurons act as integrators, not a simple brightness knob for consciousness. Increasing signaling in someone with intact orexin biology could improve vigilance, or it could produce insomnia, autonomic activation and poorly timed arousal. Cognitive performance also follows an inverted-U in many neuromodulatory systems: too little arousal is bad, but too much is not intellectual transcendence. It is often anxiety and impaired flexibility.

The GLP-1 comparison is best treated as a statement about market ambition. Narcolepsy provides clean mechanistic validation. Whether orexin agonists become broad medicines for modern exhaustion will require controlled trials that distinguish genuine functional improvement from simply keeping people awake longer.

Lilly’s acquisition of Merida Biosciences is another attempt to move from broad suppression toward mechanistic precision. The offer is worth up to $2.875 billion, although Lilly has not disclosed how much is upfront. Without that split, we cannot tell whether this is a multibillion-dollar commitment or a smaller payment surrounded by milestones.

Merida is developing therapies intended to eliminate specific pathogenic autoantibodies while preserving the rest of the antibody repertoire. Its lead asset, MER511, entered phase 1 in Graves’ disease in December.

In Graves’ disease, antibodies bind the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor and behave as agonists. Instead of merely blocking normal physiology, they imitate the receptor’s ligand and drive excess thyroid-hormone production. Patients can develop weight loss, tachycardia, tremor and heat intolerance; related autoimmune activity in orbital fibroblasts can contribute to thyroid eye disease.

Current drugs suppress thyroid-hormone synthesis or manage downstream physiology. FcRn inhibitors can lower circulating IgG more broadly. Merida’s ambition is narrower: bind and route the disease-driving antibodies toward elimination while sparing useful antibodies.

That selectivity is chemically beautiful and biologically demanding. Autoantibodies are polyclonal, and patients may recognize different epitopes with different affinities. The therapy must capture enough pathogenic species without becoming an immunogenic complex or interfering with normal TSH-receptor signaling. Lilly says early data show robust reductions in thyroid-stimulating antibodies. Actual patient numbers and clinical effects will matter much more than the adjective “robust.”

Merida also has programs for IgE-mediated allergy and membranous nephropathy. If its clearance architecture works across antibody classes and specificities, Lilly may have bought a genuine platform. For now, it has bought an early clinical Graves’ program plus that possibility.

In RNA medicine, AstraZeneca and Ionis’ phase 3 failure with Wainua has opened an argument about transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy.

The liver produces transthyretin, or TTR, as a tetramer. When that complex destabilizes, monomers can misfold and assemble into amyloid deposits in the heart. Stabilizers such as tafamidis bind the tetramer and make dissociation less likely. Silencers attack upstream production: Wainua is an antisense oligonucleotide, while Alnylam’s Amvuttra and next-generation nucresiran use RNA interference to reduce hepatic TTR synthesis.

Combining a stabilizer with a silencer sounds almost self-evidently better: make less TTR and stabilize what remains. But Wainua failed to show sufficient benefit in a trial where more than half of patients—and over 80% by the end—were also receiving stabilizers.

AstraZeneca now sounds skeptical that layering the mechanisms adds value. Alnylam argues that its molecules and trial designs are different, pointing to Amvuttra’s positive Helios-B study and suggesting nucresiran may perform better in earlier-stage patients. Wainua did show a favorable signal in stage 1 disease.

The fair interpretation is that TTR silencing has not been invalidated. Combination benefit has become much harder to assume. As tafamidis use rises, future trials must show what a silencer contributes on top of an increasingly effective baseline. Earlier patients may have more salvageable myocardium, but subgroup optimism needs prospective confirmation.

GSK, meanwhile, is taking an mRNA flu vaccine into phase 3 this month after a 971-person phase 2 study.

Its vaccine encodes both hemagglutinin and neuraminidase. Most conventional flu vaccines emphasize hemagglutinin, the viral surface protein that mediates entry into host cells. Neuraminidase helps newly produced viral particles escape infected cells. Antibodies against NA may therefore reduce viral spread and potentially broaden protection when the selected HA strains are imperfectly matched.

GSK says its mRNA candidates produced higher immune responses than standard or high-dose vaccines in younger and older adults, with acceptable safety. Unfortunately, its press release omitted the numbers. Higher antibody titers are encouraging; they are not the same as fewer laboratory-confirmed influenza cases, hospitalizations or deaths.

Moderna already won approval for the first mRNA flu vaccine, mFlusiva, which encodes three HA proteins. GSK has missed the first-to-market prize but may still differentiate through the HA-plus-NA design. Phase 3 must prove that the extra antigen produces better protection, not merely a busier immunogenicity table.

Finally, Alumis lost 54% of its value after envudeucitinib failed every primary and secondary efficacy endpoint in a 408-patient phase 2b lupus trial.

The oral drug inhibits TYK2, a JAK-family kinase transmitting signals from cytokine receptors including pathways associated with type 1 interferons. Alumis is advancing toward phase 3 because patients with a high interferon gene signature appeared to respond better than interferon-low patients. Around 60% of the study was signature-high, and the top dose produced a 52.6% response rate in that group versus 24.4% in signature-low patients.

That is hypothesis-generating, not rescue by proclamation. We need the placebo-adjusted effect within the prespecified interferon-high subgroup, confidence intervals and consistency across endpoints. A biomarker aligned with the mechanism is attractive, but lupus is heterogeneous enough that many subgroup stories can be biologically plausible after a trial fails.

A phase 3 restricted to interferon-high patients could be legitimate precision medicine. It could also become a very expensive replication test for a favorable slice of a negative study. Alumis at least has a phase 3 psoriasis win, so envudeucitinib is not pharmacologically inert. The unresolved question is whether interferon-high lupus is a prospectively treatable population or simply where this dataset looked least disappointing.

Today’s stories are all about precision colliding with systems biology. Orexin agonism elegantly replaces a missing arousal signal. Merida wants to remove only the antibodies causing disease. RNA silencers reduce one amyloid precursor, and TYK2 inhibition targets an interferon-enriched lupus subgroup.

Then the immune system reminds everyone that it is a network, not a wiring diagram. Deep B-cell depletion can produce remission—and lethal inflammation. Specific mechanisms remain valuable. Their consequences are rarely confined to the box we drew around the target.
