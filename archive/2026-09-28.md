Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for September 28, 2026.

The TL;DR: Adicet Bio has produced an encouraging autoimmune CAR-T result at exactly the moment the field needs one. A year after receiving its off-the-shelf gamma-delta CAR-T therapy, 12 of 22 evaluable lupus patients were in remission, including eight of 16 with lupus nephritis—and there were no cases of the lethal HLH-like syndrome that recently stopped Novartis’ program. It is a small phase 1 study, but the combination of remission, withdrawal of chronic immunosuppression and apparently manageable inflammatory toxicity is genuinely notable. In oncology, the FDA has rejected the accelerated-approval case for Merck and Daiichi Sankyo’s B7-H3 ADC in small-cell lung cancer, further damaging a $4 billion partnership already hit by clinical and manufacturing setbacks. Meanwhile, a lawsuit alleging conflicts and trade-secret misuse is exposing the governance risks behind the fashionable China-to-West “NewCo” model. And several leadership appointments—from Travere to Nuclera—show companies bringing in executives who specialize in commercialization and scale rather than simply generating more science.

We’ll start with Adicet Bio, because this is the first important counterweight to the recent autoimmune CAR-T safety crisis.

Adicet’s therapy, prula-cel, put 12 of 22 evaluable lupus patients into remission one year after treatment. Among the 16 patients who had lupus nephritis—the kidney-damaging form of the disease—eight achieved renal remission.

Every evaluated patient, including those who did not reach full remission, stopped taking their standard chronic immunosuppressants. All but one substantially reduced steroid use.

That is clinically legible. Lupus treatment often requires combinations of corticosteroids, antimalarials, conventional immunosuppressants and targeted biologics. Those drugs can control disease, but they generally do not erase the autoreactive immune system. Patients remain exposed to infections, metabolic toxicity, bone loss and organ damage while waiting to see whether the next flare breaks through.

Prula-cel is attempting something much more ambitious: one treatment, deep B-cell depletion, then repopulation with a healthier B-cell repertoire.

The therapy targets CD19, which is expressed across much of the B-cell lineage. B cells contribute to lupus in several ways. They can differentiate into antibody-secreting cells, present antigen to T cells and produce inflammatory cytokines. In lupus, loss of immune tolerance permits antibodies against nuclear material—DNA, RNA-associated proteins and other intracellular antigens—to form immune complexes. Those complexes deposit in tissues, activate complement and drive inflammation.

In lupus nephritis, the kidney’s glomeruli become a particularly consequential target. Immune-complex deposition and complement activation injure the filtration apparatus, leading to protein in the urine and eventually loss of renal function.

The CAR-T strategy is therefore not simply “remove the antibodies.” It tries to eliminate enough of the B-cell compartment that the pathological network collapses and rebuilds from less autoreactive precursors.

Adicet says the returning cells look like naïve B cells rather than the disease-associated populations present before treatment. That is what the company means by an “immune reset.” It is encouraging biological evidence, although naïve phenotype alone does not prove permanent tolerance. Those cells still have to encounter antigens, enter germinal-center reactions and mature over time. The important question is whether autoreactive clones and pathogenic antibodies reappear two, three or five years later.

Prula-cel’s architecture also differs from the autoimmune CAR-T programs that recently ran into trouble. It is an allogeneic, off-the-shelf product made from donor cells, rather than an autologous product manufactured separately from each patient. And it uses gamma-delta T cells rather than the more familiar alpha-beta T cells.

Most circulating T cells carry alpha-beta T-cell receptors that recognize peptide antigens presented by MHC molecules. Gamma-delta T cells use a different receptor architecture and can recognize stressed, infected or transformed cells through less conventional signals. They also tend to have distinctive tissue-homing and innate-like properties.

Adicet argues that gamma-delta cells naturally reside in tissues, making them attractive for autoimmune diseases that play out inside organs rather than solely in circulating blood. Lupus nephritis is a good test of that claim because deleting blood B cells is useful, but reaching disease-associated immune populations in the kidney and lymphoid tissues may be more important.

Off-the-shelf manufacturing could also turn CAR-T from a bespoke procedure into a more standardized medicine. An autologous product requires leukapheresis, transport to a manufacturing facility, genetic engineering, expansion, quality testing and return shipment. An allogeneic batch can theoretically be produced in advance and used for many patients.

“Off the shelf,” however, does not mean “simple.” Donor-derived cells may be rejected, may fail to persist or may create unwanted immune interactions. Gamma-delta cells could reduce some graft-versus-host concerns because they do not rely on conventional MHC recognition in the same way as alpha-beta cells, but persistence and repeatability still need to be demonstrated.

Safety is why this result will receive disproportionate attention. Adicet reported no immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, or IEC-HS. That is the HLH-like hyperinflammatory condition that caused three deaths in Novartis’ autoimmune CAR-T program. Bristol Myers Squibb also recently paused enrollment with its autologous CD19 CAR-T after inflammatory events.

Adicet additionally saw no ICANS—the neurotoxicity syndrome associated with immune effector therapies—and almost all cytokine-release syndrome was grade 1.

That is excellent so far. It is also 22 evaluable patients. A toxicity occurring in one of every 50 or 100 patients would probably remain invisible. Differences in conditioning chemotherapy, disease severity, cell dose and expansion kinetics could all affect safety. We should not conclude that gamma-delta CAR-T has solved the inflammatory problem from one small study.

Still, the field badly needed evidence that deep immune reset might be achievable without recreating oncology-level toxicity in patients with chronic autoimmune disease. Prula-cel has provided that evidence strongly enough to justify a larger test.

Now to Merck and Daiichi Sankyo, where a very expensive ADC partnership has suffered another delay.

The companies have withdrawn their accelerated-approval application for ifinatamab deruxtecan, or I-DXd, in previously treated extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer. The FDA concluded that the phase 2 dataset did not provide enough evidence for accelerated approval.

The study enrolled 187 patients whose cancer had progressed after platinum chemotherapy. The partners called the efficacy promising, but “promising” and “approvable” are different regulatory categories.

I-DXd targets B7-H3, a surface protein expressed at high levels across several tumors, including small-cell lung cancer. The antibody binds B7-H3 and delivers a deruxtecan topoisomerase-I-inhibitor payload. After internalization and linker cleavage, that payload damages DNA during replication and can also diffuse into nearby tumor cells—the useful but potentially toxic phenomenon known as bystander killing.

The unmet need is real. Small-cell lung cancer grows rapidly, spreads early and frequently develops resistance after an initial response to platinum chemotherapy. Amgen already has accelerated approval for Imdelltra, a DLL3-by-CD3 T-cell engager, in this post-platinum setting.

But the competitive and regulatory standards have risen. An uncontrolled phase 2 response rate must be sufficiently large, durable and convincing to justify approval before randomized phase 3 confirmation. Evidently, the FDA did not think I-DXd crossed that threshold.

This does not kill the drug. Enrollment in the phase 3 IDeate-Lung02 trial is nearly complete, and Merck and Daiichi can return with randomized evidence. I-DXd is also in phase 3 for prostate and esophageal cancers.

It does postpone any near-term return from Merck’s 2023 deal, which included a remarkable $4 billion upfront payment for three Daiichi ADCs.

That portfolio has not behaved kindly. The FDA first rejected the HER3-targeted patritumab deruxtecan application over manufacturing deficiencies. Merck and Daiichi later withdrew another submission after the drug failed to improve overall survival. Now I-DXd cannot secure accelerated approval from its phase 2 small-cell dataset. The third partnered asset, a CDH6-directed ADC in ovarian cancer, remains in development.

The $4 billion was not paid for three interesting constructs. It was paid to create a commercial ADC franchise. So far, the franchise remains a collection of late-stage expenses and delayed possibilities.

This also sharpens the race against GSK’s B7-H3 ADC, risvutatug rezetecan. GSK recently reported 18.5-month median overall survival in a Chinese phase 3 small-cell lung-cancer trial, compared with 10.3 months on topotecan. Cross-trial comparisons remain dangerous, and pulmonary toxicity may differentiate the competing ADCs. But randomized phase 3 survival data are simply a stronger currency than a “promising” single-arm phase 2 package.

The next story is not about a molecule failing. It is about the business machinery used to move Chinese molecules west.

Lepu Medical Technology has sued business-development consultant Su Zhang and her firm, CoDevCo, alleging conflicts of interest, double-dealing and misuse of trade secrets. These are allegations, not established findings, and Zhang’s complete response is not included in today’s source. That legal caution matters.

According to Lepu, it hired CoDevCo as its exclusive business-development representative to find Western partners for GLP-1 and metabolic assets. Lepu alleges that Zhang was simultaneously acting as chief business officer for BrightGene Pharmaceutical, a direct competitor, without adequate disclosure, consent or safeguards.

Lepu wants its $500,000 retainer returned, seeks the disgorgement of benefits allegedly obtained during the conflict and is asking the court to protect its trade secrets. The dispute emerged after CoDevCo pursued transaction-based success fees in Singapore arbitration.

Why does this merit attention beyond the pleasure of unusually spicy business-development litigation? Because Zhang is associated with the rise of the China “NewCo” model.

In this model, a Chinese biotech licenses one or more assets into a newly created Western company. Investors capitalize that NewCo, install a development team and often give the Chinese originator both cash and equity. The originator therefore receives immediate value while preserving upside if the Western company succeeds.

Zhang previously helped Hengrui license a GLP-1 portfolio to a newly formed company called Hercules. Hercules became Kailera Therapeutics and completed a record-breaking $625 million Nasdaq IPO this April, only about two years after the original deal.

That success made the structure fashionable. For Western investors, it provides access to assets that may have been discovered and advanced faster or more cheaply in China. For Chinese licensors, it creates a pathway into U.S. capital markets without building a complete Western development and commercial organization.

But the model creates extraordinary information asymmetry. A consultant may see detailed preclinical packages, clinical plans, manufacturing data, valuation expectations and competing bids from several companies operating in similar therapeutic areas. In GLP-1 development, where everyone is comparing half-life, weight loss, lean-mass effects, tolerability and manufacturing economics, that information can be commercially decisive.

The legal principle is familiar from academia even if the setting is not: disclose conflicts before they influence decisions. A consultant representing several overlapping parties needs explicit consent, information barriers and carefully defined responsibilities. Otherwise, “global asset sourcing” begins to look like one person standing in several negotiating rooms while carrying everyone’s confidential notebooks.

This lawsuit will not end the NewCo boom. It may make investors and licensors considerably more demanding about governance, exclusivity and who is allowed to know what.

Finally, several leadership moves tell us where companies believe their next bottleneck lies.

Travere Therapeutics CEO Eric Dube is stepping down after overseeing the launch of Filspari in IgA nephropathy. Bradley Campbell takes over December 1 after leading Amicus Therapeutics through its $4.8 billion sale to BioMarin.

Travere used to be called Retrophin, the company founded by Martin Shkreli. Dube helped distance the organization from that history, rebrand it and turn Filspari into an approved rare-kidney-disease product. Campbell brings a different résumé: long-term rare-disease commercialization, a second Amicus product launch and a successful acquisition.

That looks like Travere preparing for its next stage as a commercial rare-disease company rather than replacing a failed research leader.

Lift Therapeutics has recruited former Cue Biopharma CEO Daniel Passeri to advance an allogeneic neutrophil-based cancer therapy toward the clinic in 2027. Neutrophils are abundant, rapidly tissue-migrating innate immune cells, which makes them intriguing carriers or effectors in solid tumors. It also makes them difficult products: short lifespan, activation sensitivity and manufacturing consistency are not trivial problems.

And Nuclera has appointed Carl Raimond as CEO while co-founder Michael Chen moves into a scientific and business-development role. Raimond previously led Olink Proteomics through its $3 billion acquisition by Thermo Fisher.

That appointment is particularly relevant to sequencing-oriented scientists. Olink’s proximity-extension assays use pairs of antibodies attached to DNA oligonucleotides. When both antibodies bind the same protein, their oligos come close enough to create an amplifiable DNA reporter, converting protein detection into a nucleic-acid measurement that can be quantified by PCR or sequencing.

Nuclera operates in benchtop protein-expression and screening technology, helping researchers move from a protein sequence toward experimentally usable protein more quickly. Bringing in someone who scaled Olink suggests the problem is no longer proving that the platform works. It is building instruments, consumables, customer support and repeatable adoption across pharmaceutical and academic laboratories.

That is a recurring career signal. Founder-scientists are often best positioned to invent the platform and establish credibility. Once the company needs to manufacture hardware, sell consumables and support hundreds of external users, the board begins looking for someone who has already scaled a life-science-tools business.

Today’s stories cover very different technologies, but they converge on trust and execution. Adicet must show that a small, clean autoimmune CAR-T study remains clean when hundreds of patients are treated. Merck and Daiichi must generate the randomized evidence that a promising ADC response could not substitute for. China-to-West NewCos need governance strong enough to protect confidential science. And Nuclera must turn an interesting protein platform into something laboratories can use reliably every day.

The molecule matters. So do the cells, the trial, the manufacturing process—and the people allowed inside the data room.
