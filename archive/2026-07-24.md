The TL;DR: Psychedelic dealmaking continues after Lilly’s $2.8 billion AtaiBeckley acquisition, but today’s $3.3 million upfront deal between Jupiter Neurosciences and PharmAla is a much smaller, much more speculative bet on a non-racemic MDMA formulation for PTSD. Another CNS company, Neuphoria, has found an exit through a reverse merger with cancer-vaccine developer Scancell after failures in social anxiety and Alzheimer’s disease. Roche has dropped a CD19-by-CD3 T-cell engager for lupus but remains interested in deeper immune-cell reset using cell therapy. Scribe Therapeutics, which we discussed earlier this week, has now priced an upsized $129 million IPO at the top of its range—a welcome sign for the biotech financing market. And two dermatology setbacks, involving Sanofi’s OX40-ligand antibody and Celldex’s mast-cell-depleting anti-KIT antibody, show why mechanistic activity is not the same as clinically meaningful differentiation.

We’ll begin in neuropsychiatry, where Jupiter Neurosciences is paying PharmAla Biotech $3.3 million upfront for U.S. rights to ALA-002, a patented, non-racemic MDMA formulation.

The total deal can reach $100 million, but most of that is contingent biobucks: money PharmAla receives only if development, regulatory and commercial milestones are achieved. For perspective, another $3.3 million is due when ALA-002 enters phase 3, and $20 million would come with FDA approval. This is therefore a modest upfront wager, not anything resembling Lilly’s multibillion-dollar commitment to short-duration psychedelic medicine.

“Non-racemic” is the scientifically interesting phrase. Standard MDMA is usually a racemic mixture containing two mirror-image enantiomers. Those enantiomers can differ in transporter activity, receptor pharmacology, metabolism and subjective effects. Developing one enantiomer, or an unequal mixture, could in principle tune the balance among serotonin release, catecholamine signaling, prosocial effects and cardiovascular liabilities. But “non-racemic” is not itself evidence of a better drug. Jupiter still needs to show what is actually different in humans.

The simplistic explanation is that MDMA raises serotonin and dopamine, turns down amygdala hyperreactivity and thereby treats PTSD. Reality is messier and more interesting. MDMA reverses monoamine-transporter flux, strongly increasing extracellular serotonin while also affecting norepinephrine and dopamine. It may reduce fear responses, increase feelings of social safety and make traumatic memories more tolerable to revisit during therapy. That combination could support fear-memory reconsolidation or extinction learning.

This is not just a neurotransmitter-replacement story. The drug creates a temporary brain state in which psychotherapy may work differently.

That also creates the development problem. Patients and therapists can usually tell who received active MDMA, so blinding is fragile. Outcomes can depend on therapist behavior, expectancy, treatment setting and how adverse events are managed. The FDA rejected Lykos Therapeutics’ MDMA application in 2024 partly amid concerns about trial conduct and the therapy component surrounding the drug.

PharmAla’s material is already being used in U.S. government-sponsored Veterans Affairs and Defense Health Agency studies, which gives the company some practical credibility as a supplier. It does not validate ALA-002 as a differentiated product.

Jupiter itself is an unusually speculative home for the asset. Its lead program, jotrol, uses a formulation intended to deliver resveratrol to the brain and is in phase 2 for Parkinson’s disease. Resveratrol has accumulated a vast literature involving oxidative stress, inflammation and sirtuin biology, along with a far less impressive record of becoming a convincing medicine. Jupiter now has two independent CNS shots, but neither is low risk.

Staying with neuroscience—but moving from a new bet to an exit strategy—Neuphoria Therapeutics is merging with London-based Scancell Holdings.

Neuphoria ended development of soclenicant after the alpha-7 nicotinic-receptor negative allosteric modulator failed phase 3 in social anxiety disorder last October. Then Merck terminated a phase 2 Alzheimer’s study of MK-1167, another alpha-7 nicotinic-receptor modulator partnered with Neuphoria.

The alpha-7 receptor is a homopentameric nicotinic acetylcholine receptor with high calcium permeability and roles in attention, cognition, neurotransmitter release and inflammatory signaling. It is attractive precisely because it touches so many potentially useful processes. That pleiotropy has not made it easy to translate. Receptor desensitization, disease-stage dependence and circuit-specific effects can all separate clean pharmacology from meaningful cognitive or psychiatric benefit.

Scancell is using a reverse merger to obtain Neuphoria’s Nasdaq listing. In ordinary English, rather than conducting a conventional IPO from scratch, privately or overseas-listed Scancell is effectively moving into Neuphoria’s publicly traded corporate shell. Neuphoria’s shareholders receive stock in the combined company instead of being left with a slowly liquidating failed CNS biotech.

Scancell brings oncology assets, particularly iSCIB1+, a DNA-based immunotherapy for advanced melanoma. In phase 2, the combination of iSCIB1+ with Bristol Myers Squibb’s checkpoint inhibitors Opdivo and Yervoy produced 77% progression-free survival at 22 months. That is intriguing, but the planned global randomized phase 3 will have to separate vaccine contribution from a powerful checkpoint backbone.

The transaction comes with $89 million in debt and equity financing and is expected to fund Scancell through 2029. For employees, the blunt lesson is that a public company can survive even when its original scientific thesis does not. The ticker, cash and corporate structure can themselves become assets. The neuroscience organization usually does not survive in recognizable form.

Roche, meanwhile, has terminated RG6382, a CD19-by-CD3 bispecific T-cell engager being tested in systemic lupus erythematosus.

The concept resembles an off-the-shelf version of immune-cell redirection. One arm binds CD19 on B cells; the other binds CD3 on T cells, physically recruiting cytotoxic T cells to eliminate the B-cell compartment. In lupus, that could remove autoreactive B cells and antibody-producing precursors more deeply than conventional B-cell-directed antibodies.

Roche said the molecule lacked the characteristics required to continue, without identifying a dramatic safety event or single failed endpoint. That phrasing often means the total package—depletion depth, exposure, efficacy and tolerability—did not support further investment.

Importantly, Roche has not abandoned immune reset. Its $1.5 billion acquisition of Poseida brought a BCMA-by-CD19 CAR-T platform that could potentially eliminate both B cells and plasma-cell-lineage populations. CAR-T is more operationally burdensome than a bispecific antibody, but it may deliver a deeper, more durable purge of pathogenic immune memory. The autoimmune cell-therapy thesis increasingly rests on that depth: not merely suppress the inflammatory pathway, but erase enough of the miseducated immune repertoire that the system reconstitutes in a healthier state.

That is a high bar, and it carries risks from cytokine release, infection and prolonged cytopenias. But early academic CAR-T results in severe autoimmune disease have been striking enough that large pharma is not walking away because one T-cell engager failed.

Now, an update to Scribe Therapeutics. Earlier this week, the gene-editing company was targeting roughly $96 million in IPO proceeds. It has done considerably better.

Scribe is selling 8.6 million shares at $15 each, the top of its proposed range, for $128.7 million in gross proceeds. Underwriters can add another $19.3 million. The shares begin trading under the ticker SCTX.

This is meaningful beyond Scribe. An upsized offering priced at the top of the range suggests public investors are again willing to finance selected preclinical and early-clinical platform companies—not indiscriminately, but selectively.

Scribe’s lead program, STX-1150, is designed to silence PCSK9 in the liver, potentially producing very long-lasting LDL lowering after one treatment. The phase 1 trial in Australia should report topline data in the first half of 2027. Sanofi is also buying about $7.5 million of stock alongside the IPO.

There is a catch: Merck has just won approval for Lipfendra, the first oral PCSK9 inhibitor. Scribe is therefore offering extreme durability in a target class that is becoming easier to treat through conventional pharmacology. A one-time genetic intervention must be extraordinarily safe to beat a pill patients can simply stop taking.

Two dermatology stories now rhyme in an instructive way.

Sanofi has abandoned approval plans for amlitelimab, the anti-OX40-ligand antibody obtained through its $1.1 billion acquisition of Kymab. Amlitelimab hit primary endpoints in phase 3 atopic-dermatitis studies, but its efficacy did not represent a meaningful improvement over existing care—especially Sanofi and Regeneron’s own Dupixent.

OX40 is a costimulatory receptor expressed on activated T cells, while OX40 ligand appears on antigen-presenting cells. Blocking that interaction should dampen pathogenic T-cell activation and memory. The mechanism worked enough to produce biological and clinical activity. It simply did not clear today’s commercial bar.

That distinction matters. “Positive phase 3” no longer guarantees a viable product in a crowded category. If physicians already have Dupixent and newer targeted options, another injected biologic needs better efficacy, better safety, easier dosing or a clearly defined responder population.

Celldex encountered an even more direct translational failure. Its anti-KIT antibody barzolvolimab produced profound systemic mast-cell depletion in prurigo nodularis—and did not improve itch at week 12 or succeed on secondary endpoints.

KIT signaling is essential for mast-cell survival, so the pharmacodynamic result is exactly what the antibody was designed to do. Yet removing mast cells did not help. The most uncomfortable interpretation is also the simplest: mast cells may not be a central pathogenic driver of prurigo nodularis.

That finding slightly clouds Celldex’s upcoming atopic-dermatitis readout, although the diseases are not interchangeable. Barzolvolimab’s stronger case remains chronic spontaneous and cold urticaria, where mast-cell degranulation is much more directly connected to wheals and symptoms.

This is a beautiful example of why target engagement can falsify a disease hypothesis. If the cells disappear and the phenotype remains, delivery is no longer the convenient excuse.

Finally, Lilly’s triple agonist retatrutide produced 20.8% weight loss in patients with type 2 diabetes and obesity and 22.6% in people with severe obesity plus established cardiovascular disease. Those are enormous effects in absolute terms, though below the roughly 28% seen in earlier phase 3 populations.

Retatrutide activates GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors. GLP-1 and GIP influence insulin secretion and appetite; glucagon can increase energy expenditure and lipid mobilization, although it may also raise glucose without the balancing effects of the other agonism.

The cardiovascular analysis was inconclusive: 44 major events occurred across retatrutide groups versus 52 on placebo, but event rates were lower than expected, leaving the study underpowered to prove protection. Meanwhile, 13.5% of patients on 12 milligrams discontinued because of adverse events in the cardiovascular-disease study.

So retatrutide remains a formidable weight-loss drug. But investors increasingly want more than kilograms. They want demonstrated reductions in heart attacks, strokes and deaths, with tolerability that keeps patients taking the medicine. Twenty-two-point-six percent weight loss is spectacular. It does not automatically answer those other questions.

Today’s common thread is unusually clean: biological activity is only the beginning. Depleting mast cells, blocking OX40 ligand, redirecting T cells, editing PCSK9 or driving extraordinary weight loss can all prove that a mechanism is pharmacologically real. Industry value appears only when that mechanism improves outcomes enough to beat the alternatives—and does so with a product patients, physicians and healthcare systems will actually choose.
