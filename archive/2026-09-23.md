The TL;DR: Today’s most important result is a rare, unambiguously positive ALS trial. Otsuka and Ionis say ulefnersen slowed functional decline and improved survival-related outcomes in FUS-mutant ALS, an exceptionally aggressive genetic subtype that accounts for only about 0.6% of cases. GSK, meanwhile, is openly describing a strategy of balancing Chinese drug discovery with Western development—and China now wants at least 25% of the world’s first-in-class pipeline by 2030. We also have two informative autoimmune wins: mast-cell depletion worked convincingly in chronic hives after failing in prurigo nodularis, and CD40-ligand blockade succeeded in Sjögren’s disease.

Let’s start with FUS-ALS, because Otsuka and Ionis have produced something the broader ALS field does not get nearly often enough: a phase 3 win on an endpoint combining function and survival.

The Fusion trial enrolled 89 people whose ALS was caused by mutations in FUS, or fused in sarcoma. The primary analysis included 73 patients and assessed functional impairment, time to rescue treatment and survival without permanent ventilation through week 72. Patients receiving the antisense drug ulefnersen performed significantly better than patients on placebo.

The drug also beat placebo on secondary measures, including a neurodegeneration biomarker and a composite of time to death, permanent ventilation, rescue or withdrawal because the disease had progressed. Most adverse events were described as mild or moderate.

We need the numerical effect sizes before deciding how transformative this is. “Significant” could describe a modest slowing or a dramatic one, and the press release does not tell us which. But hitting function, survival-related outcomes and a biomarker in the same direction is considerably more persuasive than rescuing a failed functional trial with neurofilament alone.

FUS is an RNA- and DNA-binding protein involved in transcription, RNA processing, transport and stress responses. Pathogenic FUS variants can disrupt its normal localization and promote toxic accumulation, particularly in motor neurons. Ulefnersen is an RNA-targeted antisense oligonucleotide designed to reduce production of the mutant and normal FUS protein.

That last point matters. The therapy is not repairing the mutation. It is lowering the transcript, betting that reducing toxic FUS outweighs the consequences of reducing some normal FUS function. Dose selection therefore has to find a biological window: enough knockdown to protect motor neurons, but not so much that loss of ordinary RNA-processing functions creates a new problem.

FUS-ALS represents only about 0.6% of ALS. It is disproportionately important in juvenile and pediatric disease and can progress horrifyingly quickly, sometimes causing respiratory failure and death within one or two years of symptom onset. That aggressiveness may actually help reveal a treatment effect over a feasible trial period, although it makes every enrollment and placebo decision emotionally difficult.

Commercially, this is tiny. Guggenheim analysts forecast only $14 million in Ionis royalties by 2030 and $16 million by 2036. Otsuka obtained worldwide rights for just $10 million upfront in 2024.

Scientifically, it matters much more. Tofersen already established that lowering SOD1 can change biomarker biology in genetically defined ALS, although its initial functional evidence was complicated. Ulefnersen appears to have gone further by succeeding directly on a function-and-survival endpoint. ALS increasingly looks less like one drug market and more like a collection of molecularly distinct diseases sharing motor-neuron degeneration as a final phenotype.

That does not solve sporadic ALS. It does support a development model: identify a causal toxic protein, intervene early with a sequence-specific oligonucleotide, and enrich the trial for patients whose biology actually contains the target.

The practical challenge is diagnosis. In rapidly progressive FUS disease, months spent cycling through orthopedic evaluations or more common neurological explanations can consume a meaningful fraction of the therapeutic window. Genetic testing and referral to an ALS specialist become part of the treatment architecture.

Lexeo Therapeutics is making a smaller but strategically interesting expansion in another inherited neurodegenerative disorder: Friedreich’s ataxia.

Lexeo will acquire Mantle Therapeutics for $8.3 million upfront and up to $13 million in milestones. That is a modest asset purchase, not a lavish acquisition. It gives Lexeo four additional Friedreich’s ataxia programs led by LX3010, an oral combination therapy already tested in 11 patients.

Lexeo says those patients improved by six points on the modified Friedreich Ataxia Rating Scale, or mFARS, which ranges from zero to 93. Eleven patients are nowhere near enough to establish efficacy, especially without a clean randomized comparison. But a six-point movement is large enough to justify closer inspection.

Friedreich’s ataxia is usually caused by expanded GAA repeats in the FXN gene. The expansion promotes transcriptional repression, leaving cells deficient in frataxin. Frataxin supports mitochondrial iron-sulfur-cluster biology, so its loss disrupts energy metabolism, iron handling and oxidative homeostasis. Neurons involved in proprioception and coordination are especially vulnerable, but this is a multisystem disease: roughly 80% of patients develop cardiomyopathy.

Lexeo’s existing lead program, LX2006, is an AAV gene therapy intended to supply frataxin and is already in pivotal development for Friedreich’s ataxia cardiomyopathy. The Mantle acquisition broadens the portfolio beyond one viral-vector product and adds three preclinical programs that also address frataxin biology.

That diversification is sensible. AAV may provide durable expression, but it is difficult to redose and carries long-term manufacturing and safety burdens. An oral treatment could be adjusted or stopped and may address tissues differently. Lexeo says it will choose one Mantle program to advance, with prioritization early next year and an IND planned for 2027.

For job seekers, do not interpret “four acquired assets” as four newly funded development teams. Lexeo laid off 15% of its staff last year to protect lead cardiac programs. It has cash through 2028 and says it will advance one acquired candidate. This is option-building under discipline, not broad discovery expansion.

The day’s other major oligonucleotide result comes from Roche and Ionis in IgA nephropathy.

Their monthly subcutaneous antisense drug sefaxersen significantly reduced proteinuria compared with placebo at week 37 in the phase 3 Imagination trial. The measure was 24-hour urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, or UPCR. The actual percentage reduction was not disclosed, which is a rather important omission.

IgA nephropathy begins with aberrant immunoglobulin biology. Many patients produce IgA1 with under-galactosylated hinge-region O-glycans. Autoantibodies recognize those abnormal glycoforms, immune complexes form, and those complexes deposit in the glomerular mesangium. Complement activation then amplifies tissue injury, allowing protein to leak across the filtration barrier into urine.

Sefaxersen targets liver production of complement factor B, a core component of the alternative complement pathway. Factor B helps form the alternative-pathway C3 convertase, which acts as an amplification engine. Even when disease begins with immune-complex recognition, alternative-pathway activity can substantially intensify the final renal injury.

The trial will remain blinded through week 105 to assess kidney function directly. That distinction matters. Less proteinuria is encouraging and is associated with slower long-term decline, but preserving estimated glomerular filtration rate is what tells us the kidney is actually surviving longer.

Roche hopes the week-37 result can support accelerated approval. Its major competitor is Novartis’ Fabhalta, an oral factor B inhibitor taken twice daily. Sefaxersen is injected only once a month and did not show the cholesterol increase observed with Fabhalta in earlier studies.

So the commercial contest is not simply ASO versus small molecule. It is monthly injection versus twice-daily pills, long-duration silencing versus rapidly reversible inhibition, and potentially different safety profiles. Roche forecasts peak sales of one to two billion euros, although analyst estimates have been closer to 400 million euros by 2030.

Roche paid Ionis $55 million upfront for IgA-nephropathy rights in 2022, after an earlier $75 million option deal. GSK had previously held the program and walked away in 2017. Drug development occasionally rewards the company willing to retrieve a mechanism from someone else’s discard pile.

Staying with kidney disease, Vertex reported a more preliminary signal for inaxaplin in APOL1-mediated kidney disease.

In 22 patients with modest proteinuria, the APOL1 channel inhibitor reduced urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio by 42.7% at week 13. UPCR fell 44.7%. Those are encouraging numbers.

The 17-patient cohort with both APOL1-mediated disease and type 2 diabetes was less impressive: UACR declined 17.3%, while UPCR fell 25.4%. These are tiny, uncontrolled groups, so baseline variation can move percentages substantially.

Risk variants in APOL1 are concentrated among people with relatively recent African ancestry. Under inflammatory conditions, pathogenic APOL1 proteins can form damaging membrane-associated channels and stress kidney cells, particularly podocytes. Inaxaplin is intended to inhibit that channel function directly.

Vertex’s pivotal Amplitude study, enrolling patients with severe proteinuria and without competing kidney-disease causes, reports an interim analysis early next year. That is the dataset to care about. Today’s result suggests the addressable population might eventually expand by roughly 100,000 patients, but diabetes introduces enough additional renal pathology that APOL1 inhibition may no longer control the whole phenotype.

Now to sequencing and target discovery. Envisagenics has signed a multiyear oncology collaboration with Boehringer Ingelheim carrying up to $1 billion in potential value.

No upfront payment was disclosed, so the billion dollars should be treated as milestone-heavy biobucks. But the platform is genuinely relevant to transcriptomics.

Envisagenics’ SpliceCore system analyzes more than 14 million alternative-splicing events, searching for isoforms that appear selectively in tumors and generate unique protein regions absent from normal tissues. Boehringer will validate selected targets and may use them for ADCs, T-cell engagers or multispecific antibodies.

Alternative splicing lets one gene produce several RNA and protein products by choosing different exon combinations. Cancer cells often distort that process through mutations, altered splicing-factor activity or transcriptional stress. A tumor-specific exon junction could create a novel extracellular epitope—a molecular address that is cleaner than targeting a protein also expressed in healthy tissue.

That is the exciting version. The skeptical version is that detecting a tumor-enriched RNA isoform does not prove that enough stable protein reaches the cell surface. Short-read sequencing can also struggle to reconstruct full-length isoforms unambiguously, and low-level expression in an essential normal tissue can become very important once a potent T-cell engager arrives.

The collaboration therefore lives or dies in validation: long-read or orthogonal transcript confirmation, proteomics, cell-surface localization, normal-tissue atlases and functional killing assays. Envisagenics traces its technology to the Columbia laboratory where the spinal-muscular-atrophy drug Spinraza originated, giving the company real splicing pedigree. The Boehringer deal should also help fund its own CNS candidate into the clinic.

Two autoimmune readouts then provided a useful lesson in disease context.

Celldex’s anti-KIT antibody barzolvolimab won both phase 3 trials in chronic spontaneous urticaria, totaling 1,939 patients. Among patients whose hives had resisted Xolair, complete responses at week 12 reached 55.3% and 41.7% in the two studies with the 150-milligram regimen, versus 9.3% and 15.1% on placebo. The 300-milligram regimen produced complete-response rates of 44.3% and 46.4%.

KIT signaling is essential for mast-cell survival. Barzolvolimab therefore depletes or suppresses mast cells upstream of histamine release and other inflammatory outputs. Only two patients experienced anaphylaxis, although full safety data and the consequences of sustained mast-cell suppression still need scrutiny.

This is especially informative because the same drug recently failed completely in prurigo nodularis despite “profound” mast-cell depletion. In chronic hives, mast cells appear close to the disease bottleneck. In prurigo nodularis, sensory-neuron sensitization, skin-barrier pathology and other immune pathways evidently continued without them.

Amgen also won phase 3 in Sjögren’s disease with dazodalibep, a fusion protein that blocks CD40 ligand. In 651 patients with moderate-to-severe systemic disease, treatment significantly improved the ESSDAI disease-activity score after 48 weeks.

CD40 ligand on activated T cells communicates with CD40 on B cells and antigen-presenting cells, supporting B-cell activation, antibody responses and inflammatory coordination. Blocking that interaction is a plausible way to interrupt the autoimmune conversation attacking salivary and lacrimal glands.

The FDA will probably want the second phase 3 study, which enrolls patients with milder systemic activity, to succeed as well. No drug is specifically approved in the United States to modify Sjögren’s disease, but Novartis’ ianalumab has already produced two phase 3 wins. Amgen may have validated the pathway without winning the race.

Finally, GSK has supplied unusually candid language for the geopolitical thread running through today’s news. The company says it is balancing “the West and the East” after Chinese partnerships opened its eyes to the speed, scale and quality of drug development there.

Its early Hansoh deals cost a combined $270 million upfront for ex-China rights to B7-H3 and B7-H4 ADCs. The B7-H3 drug ris-rez has now produced 18.5-month median survival in small-cell lung cancer. That is industrial policy, not a prediction guaranteed to come true. Yet Western licensing behavior suggests the goal is no longer absurd. The United States is also considering largely exempting ordinary pharmaceutical investments from proposed restrictions on Chinese biotech deals, provided the work is unrelated to pathogens or technologies with obvious weaponization potential.

For scientists entering industry, the practical implication is that “external innovation” increasingly means global asset evaluation, assay transfer and cross-border development. Chinese biotechs are not merely producing inexpensive followers. They are generating ADCs, antibodies and clinical datasets that Western pharma increasingly considers too valuable to ignore.

Today’s stories span several scales of precision. Ulefnersen targets one toxic RNA in a tiny genetic form of ALS. Sefaxersen suppresses one complement amplifier across a much larger kidney market. Envisagenics is searching millions of splice events for tumor-specific addresses. And mast-cell depletion succeeds or fails depending on whether mast cells actually control the disease being treated.

The technology can be exquisitely specific. The disease still gets the final vote.
