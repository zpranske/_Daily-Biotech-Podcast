Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for August 17, 2026.

The TL;DR: MapLight Therapeutics is pausing its entire preclinical pipeline to concentrate roughly half a billion dollars of available capital on its mixed-but-positive M1/M4 muscarinic agonist for schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s psychosis. That is the clearest neuroscience signal today—and a reminder that even well-financed biotechs are narrowing around assets with human data. Capricor, meanwhile, may get another chance at FDA approval for its Duchenne cell therapy by shifting the proposed indication from cardiomyopathy toward upper-limb function, although advisory-committee members called the evidence “fragile.” Redx Pharma is effectively taking over failed-program biotech Skye Bioscience’s public listing, with $125 million supporting a gut-restricted ROCK inhibitor for fibrotic Crohn’s disease. BioCryst is closing internal discovery and becoming an external-innovation rare-disease company. And former Legend CEO Ying Huang has resurfaced at young ADC and T-cell-engager startup K2 Therapeutics. Across all of these stories, companies are conserving discovery dollars and putting operators, capital and public listings behind fewer clinical bets.

Let’s start with MapLight, because this is a genuinely consequential update to the mixed schizophrenia result we discussed last month.

MapLight is going all-in on ML-007C-MA, its M1 and M4 muscarinic-receptor agonist, and pausing further investment in every preclinical and discovery-stage program. That includes ML-055, a next-generation M1/M4 agonist that MapLight had described as more potent, potentially suitable for once-daily dosing and possibly adaptable into a long-acting injectable. Also paused are a GPR52 positive allosteric modulator for hyperactivity and an M4 antagonist for Parkinson’s disease.

Those were not decorative pipeline boxes. ML-055, in particular, was supposed to address one of the problems exposed by the phase 2 Zephyr study: the twice-daily ML-007C-MA regimen worked, while the once-daily regimen did not.

In that 307-patient schizophrenia trial, twice-daily dosing improved the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, or PANSS, by a placebo-adjusted 4.5 points after five weeks. The primary endpoint was met. But a 4.5-point effect is modest, and the failure of once-daily dosing made the package less convincing. MapLight lost more than 60% of its market value after the readout.

The drug’s mechanism is still worth caring about. Most antipsychotics strongly inhibit dopamine D2 receptors. That can reduce positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, but it can also produce movement disorders, elevated prolactin and other liabilities. Muscarinic M1 and M4 agonism offers a different route. M1 receptors influence cortical excitability, cognition and synaptic plasticity; M4 receptors can modulate striatal and mesolimbic dopamine signaling indirectly.

Bristol Myers Squibb’s Cobenfy validated this approach when it became the first major new mechanistic class in schizophrenia in decades. Cobenfy combines the M1/M4-preferring agonist xanomeline with trospium, a peripherally restricted muscarinic antagonist intended to reduce gastrointestinal and other outside-the-brain cholinergic effects.

Validation is useful, but it changes the exam question. MapLight no longer needs to show that muscarinic pharmacology can reduce psychosis. It needs to show that ML-007C-MA can compete with Cobenfy on efficacy, cognition, tolerability, dosing convenience or some clinically recognizable combination of those features.

The company does have resources. A recent private placement raised $150 million, meaning selected investors bought newly issued shares outside a conventional public offering. Add that to the $351.3 million MapLight held at the end of June, and the company has approximately half a billion dollars available. Management says that should finance the schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s-disease-psychosis programs through the end of 2028.

MapLight plans to meet with the FDA and begin a phase 3 schizophrenia study with topline data expected in 2028. The phase 2 Vista trial in Alzheimer’s psychosis is already enrolling and should report in the second half of 2027.

Alzheimer’s psychosis could be an important second opportunity, but it is also a delicate population. Older patients with dementia are particularly vulnerable to orthostatic hypotension, constipation, urinary retention, falls and confusion. A muscarinic drug that produces central benefit without excessive peripheral cholinergic effects could be valuable. Conversely, an effect that looks merely modest in younger schizophrenia patients may become hard to use if tolerability is not excellent.

For the job market, MapLight’s decision is unusually legible. Clinical development, biomarker work, safety, regulatory science and phase 3 operations remain funded. Early discovery has been explicitly deprioritized. Half a billion dollars in the bank does not automatically mean broad laboratory hiring. It can mean an extremely well-funded two-program company.

That same concentration is visible at Capricor Therapeutics, although there the issue is regulatory survival rather than pipeline strategy.

Earlier this month, an FDA advisory committee voted nine to three against the efficacy of Capricor’s donor-derived cell therapy deramiocel for cardiomyopathy associated with Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Capricor now says the FDA is willing to review an amendment that would refocus the proposed indication on upper-limb function.

That distinction matters because the phase 3 Hope-3 trial’s primary endpoint concerned upper-limb performance. Cardiac function was secondary. Capricor had nevertheless sought an indication centered on cardiomyopathy, where the advisory committee found the evidence particularly weak.

The company plans to add 24-month results from an open-label extension and further analyses of existing data. “Open-label” means everyone knows patients are receiving the therapy, so longer follow-up may help establish durability but does not repair the absence of a blinded control over that extension period.

Deramiocel consists of donor-derived cardiosphere cells infused every three months. The plausible mechanism is paracrine. These cells are not expected to lodge throughout Duchenne muscle, become replacement myofibers and restore dystrophin. Instead, they may secrete extracellular vesicles, cytokines and trophic factors that modulate inflammation, fibrosis and tissue stress.

That broader mechanism could conceivably affect skeletal muscle as well as heart. But the evidentiary standard remains the same: did treated patients preserve usable arm and hand function better than a credible control group?

Advisory-committee members were not dismissive, but they were hardly persuaded. Neurologist Steven Pavlakis said, “There might be something here,” before calling the evidence “very fragile.” Biostatistician Christopher Coffey said he was not convinced about upper-limb benefit, although he found it more plausible than improvement in left-ventricular ejection fraction. Another panelist speculated that a larger, longer trial using different endpoints might reveal an effect.

That is encouragement to run a better experiment, not a clean endorsement of approval.

Capricor’s stock nevertheless rose 71%, from $4.21 to $7.19, because investors had begun pricing in a straightforward rejection. The FDA’s willingness to accept an amendment preserves a path. It does not indicate that the agency has accepted Capricor’s interpretation.

The current August 22 decision date will probably be extended once the amendment arrives. A PDUFA date, to decode the regulatory jargon, is the target date by which the FDA expects to act on an application. Extending it gives reviewers time to examine the new submission; it is neither approval nor rejection.

Now to one of biotech’s more revealing corporate marriages. Skye Bioscience and Redx Pharma are combining to create Fibrx Therapeutics, a new Nasdaq-listed company backed by $125 million.

The transaction resembles a reverse merger. Skye already supplies the public corporate vehicle, while Redx supplies the leadership and the programs that investors actually want to finance. Redx CEO Lisa Anson will run Fibrx, and the combined company will focus on Redx’s fibrosis portfolio.

Skye arrives with baggage. It ended its ophthalmology work after a phase 2 glaucoma failure in 2024 and experienced another clinical setback last year. Redx has its own failed transaction history: in 2023, it planned to merge with the depleted oncology biotech Jounce Therapeutics, only for Concentra Biosciences to appear and acquire Jounce instead.

This time, the structure comes with actual financing. The $125 million package is assembled through several private investments, subscription agreements and an equity line involving firms including Abingworth, Redmile, British Business Bank, NEXTBio Capital and 5AM Ventures. Fibrx expects that money to provide runway into 2029.

The lead drug, RXC008, is a gastrointestinally restricted pan-ROCK inhibitor entering phase 2 for fibrostenotic Crohn’s disease. Topline data are expected in the second half of 2028.

ROCK stands for Rho-associated coiled-coil-containing protein kinase. Downstream of Rho-family GTPases, ROCK regulates actomyosin contraction, cytoskeletal organization, cell migration and mechanotransduction. In fibrosis, ROCK signaling can help maintain activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, promote extracellular-matrix deposition and reinforce the contractile, stiff-tissue state that makes fibrosis self-sustaining.

Fibrostenotic Crohn’s disease is not simply inflamed bowel. Repeated injury and repair can deposit collagen and other matrix components in the intestinal wall, narrowing the lumen into a stricture. Anti-inflammatory biologics may control immune activity without reversing a mature mechanical obstruction. Patients can eventually require bowel resection or strictureplasty, a surgical widening of the narrowed segment.

RXC008 is designed to remain largely within the gastrointestinal tract. That restriction could be important because systemic ROCK inhibition can affect vascular tone and other normal processes. The therapeutic ambition is to hit fibrotic tissue in the bowel while limiting exposure elsewhere.

Redx says preclinical studies suggest actual reversal of gastrointestinal fibrosis, and phase 1 showed favorable tolerability. Reversing fibrosis in an animal model, however, is considerably easier than reopening a mature human intestinal stricture. Phase 2 will need convincing measures of bowel-wall remodeling, obstruction symptoms and ideally reduced need for surgery—not only changes in inflammatory biomarkers.

The corporate structure also offers a useful lesson. Sometimes the most valuable assets in a struggling public biotech are its Nasdaq listing, cash position and capacity to issue shares. The surviving science may come from the nominally private partner.

BioCryst Pharmaceuticals is making a related but less dramatic transition. It has shut down internal discovery, including its Birmingham, Alabama, Discovery Center of Excellence, and says future pipeline additions will come exclusively through licensing or acquisitions.

CEO Charlie Gayer insists BioCryst is not abandoning science and R&D. That is true in the narrow sense: evaluating external drugs, running trials and developing them are all R&D. But closing a discovery center means the company is abandoning one specific kind of R&D—the internal generation of new molecules and targets.

BioCryst can fund this strategy with Orladeyo, its oral plasma-kallikrein inhibitor for hereditary angioedema, which generated $158.2 million in the second quarter. Hereditary angioedema usually involves deficient or dysfunctional C1 inhibitor, permitting excessive plasma kallikrein activity and overproduction of bradykinin. Bradykinin makes blood vessels leaky, producing unpredictable swelling that can affect limbs, the gastrointestinal tract, face and, dangerously, the airway.

Orladeyo’s commercial success gives BioCryst the option to become a rare-disease asset buyer. The company wants to add two programs within 18 months. It already acquired Astria Therapeutics and its long-acting HAE candidate navenibart, then sold European rights for $70 million upfront. A phase 3 readout is expected in the third quarter of 2027.

BioCryst also retains BCX17725, a KLK5 inhibitor in phase 1 for Netherton syndrome. KLK5 is a skin protease whose dysregulation contributes to barrier disruption, inflammation and excessive shedding in this severe genetic disease. Initial clinical data are expected by year-end.

The insider interpretation is fairly blunt. A profitable commercial company has concluded that buying externally validated programs offers a better probability-adjusted return than maintaining its own discovery engine. That may be rational for shareholders. For bench scientists, it removes a whole category of internal career path.

Finally, former Legend Biotech CEO Ying Huang has reappeared only weeks after his sudden departure. He will now lead K2 Therapeutics, a Singapore- and Boston-based startup founded in 2024 with a $50 million seed round from MPM BioImpact.

K2 says it has eight preclinical and clinical programs spanning antibody-drug conjugates and T-cell engagers. Huang brings unusual experience across finance and operations: he was previously a biotech equity-research analyst, then Legend’s CFO and CEO, and helped shepherd the BCMA CAR-T therapy Carvykti to market.

For a young multimodality oncology company, that résumé signals aspirations beyond clever constructs. K2 wants someone who understands capital markets, partnerships, manufacturing and commercialization. Eight programs on $50 million also sounds ambitious; expect prioritization.

A smaller leadership move worth watching comes from Tanai Therapeutics, a 2023 spinout of Belgium’s VIB and Ghent University. Chief scientific officer Josua Jordi is becoming CEO as the company develops obesity small molecules that target energy expenditure rather than relying solely on appetite suppression. Jordi previously co-founded EraCal, helped license an oral obesity compound to Novo Nordisk and later led metabolism discovery at Roche.

Tanai remains early, but its positioning reflects the new obesity bar. Another generic appetite suppressant will struggle. A small molecule that safely increases energy expenditure could complement incretin therapies—provided it avoids the cardiovascular and thermoregulatory problems that have haunted this idea historically.

Taken together, today’s companies are not abandoning innovation so much as sorting it ruthlessly by distance from human evidence. MapLight is freezing discovery around one mixed phase 2 signal. Capricor is trying to rescue one fragile application. Fibrx is using a public shell and fresh financing to move a gut-restricted antifibrotic into phase 2. BioCryst would rather buy programs than discover them.

For scientists entering industry, cash remains abundant in selected places. But follow the allocation, not merely the financing headline. A company can hold $500 million and still pause the laboratory program you hoped to join.
