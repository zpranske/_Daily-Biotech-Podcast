BioVie’s Parkinson’s readout deserves the closest scrutiny, partly because the biology is plausible and partly because the presentation is so uncomfortable.

The phase 2 study tested bezisterim, an orally available small molecule intended to reduce neuroinflammation and improve metabolic signaling. BioVie has discussed the drug in terms of modulating inflammation associated with insulin resistance and potentially reducing downstream neuronal stress. The broad Parkinson’s hypothesis is familiar: activated microglia, inflammatory cytokines, mitochondrial dysfunction and altered protein homeostasis may help drive degeneration beyond the initial alpha-synuclein pathology.

The trial’s stated primary endpoint was change in Part Three of the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale, or MDS-UPDRS. Part Three is the clinician-scored motor examination: tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, gait, posture and related signs.

BioVie’s press release did not clearly report the overall result on that primary endpoint.

Instead, the company centered its announcement on something called EPNIC-15—the Early Parkinson’s Neuro-Inflammatory Composite 15. This combines 15 selected subdomains from several existing scales, including motor findings, daily-life symptoms and sleep. BioVie says patients receiving bezisterim generally improved on this composite while placebo patients worsened, particularly among people with elevated baseline platelet counts, which it is using as a marker of inflammation.

There are several problems here. First, EPNIC-15 appears to be a new, company-constructed measure with little independent validation. Second, choosing a subset of items after learning from prior studies can produce a scale that is unusually well matched to the observed data. That does not make it fraudulent or biologically meaningless. It does make it exploratory.

And the available primary-endpoint graph is not comforting. At lower platelet counts, placebo numerically outperformed bezisterim on the motor scale. At higher counts, the drug looked better, but the 95% confidence interval still crossed zero. In other words, even the inflammation-enriched result did not clearly exclude no treatment effect.

Investors cut BioVie’s stock by 50%, to about one dollar, while the company simultaneously filed to sell more shares. That reaction is not merely Wall Street being allergic to scientific nuance. The financing creates urgency: BioVie needs capital, and a positive narrative around a novel composite helps it raise that capital.

A platelet-defined inflammatory subgroup might ultimately be real. Platelets participate in immune signaling and can rise with systemic inflammation. But platelet count is also influenced by infection, iron status, medications and many other conditions. Before this becomes a precision-Parkinson’s strategy, BioVie needs a prospectively defined trial showing that platelet-high patients benefit on a validated clinical endpoint. Build the hypothesis now. Test it cleanly next time.

Vistagen’s fasedienol story has a similar statistical shape, although a very different mechanism.

Fasedienol is an intranasal neuroactive steroid being developed for social anxiety disorder. Vistagen describes it as acting through nasal chemosensory pathways that influence limbic and autonomic circuits, rather than requiring conventional systemic exposure like an SSRI or benzodiazepine. The intended product is situational: take the spray before a stressful social event, such as public speaking, and reduce the acute fear response without sedation or dependence.

In the new 61-patient phase 2 trial, participants completed a public-speaking challenge after receiving one dose, two doses or placebo. Neither fasedienol regimen significantly beat placebo on the primary subjective-units-of-distress scale.

Numerically, two doses reduced distress by 15.4 points, one dose by 14.7 and placebo by 6.8. That looks suggestive, but the trial was small, and Vistagen says it was not powered to establish statistical significance. An underpowered study can generate a useful signal. It cannot retroactively become confirmatory because the numerical ordering looks attractive.

The company again highlighted patients with very severe baseline social anxiety. In that subgroup, distress fell by 17.5 points with two doses and 15.3 with one dose, versus only 1.6 with placebo. Still, neither comparison crossed the conventional p-value threshold of 0.05.

This follows two phase 3 disappointments, including one in June, and a roughly 20% layoff after the earlier miss. Vistagen did produce a spectacular phase 3 win in 2023, which once sent its shares up more than tenfold. The central problem is reproducibility: why does the drug look dramatic in one study, fail in another and then appear to survive mainly in successively narrower subgroups?

Social-anxiety trials are vulnerable to variable placebo effects, expectancy and the artificiality of a laboratory speaking challenge. Severe patients could genuinely have more room to improve and a lower placebo response. Or the subgroup could simply be where random variation favors the drug. The only persuasive answer is a preregistered, adequately powered trial limited to that population. FDA flexibility is possible when unmet need is high, but unmet need does not turn repeated post hoc subgroup analyses into replicated efficacy.

Lilly, meanwhile, has removed the Gaucher-disease type 1 study of its GBA1 gene therapy LY3884961 from the pipeline. The company says there was no safety signal; the program simply failed to meet its internal bar.

GBA1 encodes glucocerebrosidase, a lysosomal enzyme that helps break down glucosylceramide. Biallelic loss-of-function mutations cause Gaucher disease by allowing lipid-rich material to accumulate in macrophages and other cells. Variants in the same gene are also among the strongest genetic risk factors for Parkinson’s disease, probably through a feedback loop involving impaired lysosomal function, altered lipid composition and alpha-synuclein accumulation.

Lilly will continue the Propel trial in Parkinson’s disease. That distinction is interesting. Gaucher disease offers a relatively direct enzyme-replacement logic, whereas Parkinson’s involves a more probabilistic disease-modification hypothesis. Yet systemic Gaucher manifestations and CNS Parkinson’s biology require different biodistribution, dose and therapeutic thresholds. A vector that fails to create enough enzyme in the relevant peripheral tissues could still potentially affect neuronal or glial lysosomal biology after direct CNS delivery—or vice versa.

In the retina, Tarsus Pharmaceuticals is paying $450 million—$270 million in cash and $180 million in stock—to acquire Alkeus and its Stargardt-disease candidate gildeuretinol.

Stargardt disease is commonly caused by mutations in ABCA4, which disrupt clearance of vitamin-A-derived compounds from photoreceptor outer segments. Toxic bisretinoids then accumulate in retinal pigment epithelial cells as lipofuscin. The RPE becomes dysfunctional, photoreceptors lose metabolic support and central vision progressively deteriorates.

Gildeuretinol is a modified oral form of vitamin A designed to slow formation of those toxic dimers while preserving vitamin A’s essential role in the visual cycle. That balancing act is crucial. Suppress retinaldehyde chemistry too aggressively and you may reduce toxic waste while also compromising the chromophore regeneration needed for phototransduction.

The drug previously failed a phase 3 study in geographic atrophy, but phase 2 Stargardt data were encouraging enough for Tarsus to make a substantial bet. The current phase 3 study reads out in 2029. This will be an imaging-intensive race. Fundus autofluorescence can quantify lipofuscin-associated signal and map areas of RPE loss. The challenge is showing not merely a prettier biomarker trajectory, but a slower loss of usable vision.
