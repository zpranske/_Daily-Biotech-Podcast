Let’s begin with Gate Bioscience, because it combines a genuinely interesting protein-control mechanism, a neurological application and an almost comically uninformative business-development announcement.

Eli Lilly has expanded its collaboration with Gate by adding one undisclosed target, plus an option for another. The new agreement carries up to $870 million in possible payments. That comes on top of last year’s collaboration, which had another $856 million in potential value.

Before anyone mentally records this as a $1.7 billion transaction, stop. The upfront payment is undisclosed, and most of the advertised value is biobucks: development, regulatory and commercial milestones that may never be paid. Lilly has increased its commitment, but we cannot tell whether Gate received $10 million, $100 million or something else today.

Scientifically, Gate’s “molecular gates” are small molecules designed to interfere selectively with proteins passing through SEC61, the translocon channel in the endoplasmic-reticulum membrane.

Secreted proteins and many membrane proteins begin translation on cytosolic ribosomes. A signal sequence on the emerging polypeptide recruits machinery that guides the ribosome toward SEC61. The growing protein is then threaded into or through the ER membrane, where it can fold, receive glycans, form disulfide bonds and proceed through the Golgi toward secretion or cell-surface display.

Gate wants to place a highly selective bouncer at that doorway. Its compounds recognize sequence features in a particular nascent protein and prevent that protein from completing translocation, ideally causing it to be degraded before it reaches its functional destination.

That is conceptually different from a conventional inhibitor, which binds an already formed protein, and from a proteolysis-targeting chimera, which recruits an existing cytosolic protein to an E3 ligase. Gate is intervening during protein biogenesis.

The company’s most advanced internal program targets prion disease. The normal prion protein, PrP, enters the secretory pathway and ultimately becomes a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored cell-surface protein. In prion disease, misfolded PrP templates further misfolding and aggregation, producing a self-propagating neurodegenerative process.

Reducing the supply of normal PrP could deprive that process of substrate. Human genetics support the general direction: lower PrP expression is expected to be protective, and several groups are pursuing antisense approaches against PRNP RNA.

Gate’s challenge is to show that blocking ER entry actually lowers PrP safely in relevant neurons without creating problematic mislocalized intermediates or broadly disrupting other secretory proteins. SEC61 is not an obscure accessory. It is central cellular infrastructure. The entire value proposition depends on sequence-selective obstruction rather than generalized traffic congestion at the ER.

Lilly’s expansion suggests it has seen encouraging internal data. It does not validate Gate’s platform clinically. For scientists, this is a company to watch across chemical biology, cotranslational quality control, proteostasis and neurodegeneration. The immediate hiring signal is harder to read because neither the collaboration targets nor the upfront research funding were disclosed.

Now to the day’s largest clinical and market-moving result.

Vaxcyte’s VAX-31 met every prespecified primary immunogenicity endpoint in the phase 3 Opus-1 trial comparing it with Pfizer’s Prevnar 20 and Merck’s Capvaxive in adults. Investors sent Vaxcyte shares up 57% before the market opened, from $56.48 to $88.50.

VAX-31 covers 31 pneumococcal serotypes. It was noninferior on all 20 serotypes included in Prevnar 20 and on 17 of the 19 serotypes shared with Capvaxive.

The two misses against Capvaxive were serotypes 3 and 12F. Under this trial’s more stringent definition, the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the immune-response ratio had to exceed 0.667. VAX-31 did not clear that bar for those two serotypes, although it did exceed the older threshold of 0.5.

That is a good result, not a flawless one.

Streptococcus pneumoniae is surrounded by a polysaccharide capsule, and variation in that capsule defines the many serotypes. Antibodies against one capsule may offer little protection against another, which is why pneumococcal vaccines resemble increasingly ambitious molecular collections rather than one universal antigen.

Conjugate vaccines chemically attach capsular polysaccharides to carrier proteins. That converts a response that would otherwise be largely T-cell-independent into one that recruits helper T cells, supports class switching and generates better immune memory. It is particularly important in young children, but conjugate architecture also improves the quality of adult responses.

Adding more serotypes is not trivial. Each component must be manufactured consistently and contribute useful immunity without excessive antigenic interference. A 31-valent vaccine is therefore a substantial formulation and analytical accomplishment, not simply Prevnar with eleven extra ingredients shaken into the vial.

Vaxcyte estimates that VAX-31 could provide incremental coverage of 13% to 36% of invasive pneumococcal disease and 19% to 31% of pneumococcal pneumonia compared with current adult conjugate vaccines. It also preserves historically important serotypes, including serotype 4, which disproportionately affects some underserved populations.

The commercial argument is appealing: combine the useful coverage choices currently split between Pfizer and Merck into one product. But regulators will examine serotypes 3 and 12F closely, particularly because the Capvaxive-specific serotypes account for a meaningful share of current adult disease.

Vaxcyte expects to file in the first half of 2028. The company still has to complete the regulatory package and demonstrate reproducible commercial manufacturing across 31 conjugated components. The 57% share-price jump says investors now view that path as much more credible.

Xaira Therapeutics has also emerged with new platform information, although almost no clinical evidence.

Xaira launched in 2024 with an astonishing $1 billion and a founding roster that included protein-design pioneer David Baker. After two years of relative silence, the company has unveiled X-Design, its generative antibody-design system.

Xaira says X-Design can create antibodies “from scratch” against targets that resisted conventional discovery. Its first disclosed candidate, XA-1, is an oncology antibody reportedly designed in seven weeks. XA-4 targets a difficult G-protein-coupled receptor. The precise targets remain secret, as do the indications, preclinical results and clinical timelines.

Seven weeks is impressive for sequence design. It is not seven weeks to a drug.

An antibody still needs the correct epitope and binding geometry, useful functional activity, acceptable specificity, favorable pharmacokinetics, low aggregation, limited immunogenicity and manufacturability at high concentration. GPCRs are especially difficult because they are conformationally dynamic membrane proteins with limited extracellular surface available for antibody binding.

Xaira’s broader vision combines X-Design with X-Cell for target discovery and X-Patient for identifying responsive patient populations. In theory, that creates a “full-stack” drug company: discover the disease biology, design the molecule and select the patient.

That integration is attractive because many programs fail at the interfaces. A beautiful antibody against the wrong target is still wrong. A valid target without a tractable molecule remains a paper. A useful drug tested in an unselected population may appear inactive.

The danger is that “full stack” becomes a polished description for three expensive models whose errors compound. Xaira now needs experimentally validated structures, functional assays, animal pharmacology and eventually human data. The $1 billion financing bought it time to build. It did not prepay validation.

For scientists moving from academia, Xaira should be relevant to structural biology, protein engineering, high-throughput screening, single-cell profiling and computational-experimental integration. The most valuable hires will probably be people willing to tell a model when its gorgeous sequence makes an awful protein.

In oncology, Genmab’s folate-receptor-alpha ADC rinatabart sesutecan, or Rina-S, produced a 45.9% objective response rate and median progression-free survival of 9.5 months in 109 patients with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer.

These were heavily treated patients. Fifty-three percent had received three or four prior lines of therapy, every patient had received bevacizumab and a taxane, almost half had received a PARP inhibitor, and one-third had already received AbbVie’s competing folate-receptor-alpha ADC Elahere.

Rina-S combines an antibody against folate receptor alpha with a cytotoxic topoisomerase-inhibitor payload. Folate receptor alpha is overexpressed in many ovarian tumors but has more restricted accessibility in healthy tissue, making it a useful delivery address.

The 9.5-month progression-free-survival result looks excellent beside Elahere’s 5.6 months in a separate phase 3 study. TD Cowen analysts called Rina-S “clearly superior.”

That adjective is premature. Separate trials can differ in baseline risk, receptor expression, imaging schedules and prior therapy. Genmab’s study was also not a randomized head-to-head comparison.

The safety profile is nevertheless intriguing. Genmab reported no signal for ocular toxicity, peripheral neuropathy, interstitial lung disease or stomatitis. Elahere carries a boxed warning for ocular toxicity, including keratopathy. Rina-S instead produced more fatigue, nausea and vomiting.

Activity in patients previously treated with Elahere and in tumors with low or nominally absent folate-receptor expression may indicate useful bystander killing, where released payload diffuses into adjacent cells. It could also reflect limitations in receptor testing. Genmab’s ongoing phase 3 trial against physician-selected treatment will determine whether the durability and safety advantages survive randomization.
