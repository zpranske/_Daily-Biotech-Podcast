The TL;DR: Axiom Biosciences is reporting zero deaths over two years among nine newborns treated with a Wharton’s-jelly-derived mesenchymal stromal cell therapy after severe brain injury. That is intriguing, but the tiny, apparently uncontrolled phase 1 study cannot establish efficacy, especially across two biologically different conditions. Axiom now plans the unusual move of listing first in Hong Kong and later on Nasdaq. Elsewhere in neuroscience, Latigo is preparing an IPO to challenge Vertex in non-opioid pain with Nav1.8 inhibitors, while Lilly’s $2.8 billion AtaiBeckley purchase is already lifting sentiment across psychedelics. Novartis has killed one radioligand candidate without retreating from radiopharma, Kolon’s elaborate cell-based osteoarthritis therapy failed phase 3, and Bristol Myers Squibb is building an enormous Nvidia-powered AI system. Across all of these stories, ambitious platforms are meeting the less glamorous demands of controls, endpoints, manufacturability and capital.

Let’s start with Axiom Biosciences, because the combination of neonatal brain injury, stem cells and a genuinely strange financing strategy deserves careful unpacking.

The San Diego company says nine newborns treated with its lead therapy had a zero-percent mortality rate over two years. The infants had either severe intraventricular hemorrhage, meaning bleeding into the brain’s ventricular system, or hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, in which reduced oxygen and blood flow injure the newborn brain. Axiom contrasted its result with a claimed first-year mortality rate of 46% for these conditions.

The therapy, co-developed with South Korea’s Medinno, consists of mesenchymal stromal cells derived from Wharton’s jelly, the connective tissue surrounding the blood vessels in the umbilical cord.

You will often hear these called mesenchymal “stem” cells, but stromal cell is usually the more disciplined term. Their proposed therapeutic value generally does not depend on rebuilding the brain by becoming replacement neurons and wiring themselves into circuits. That would be an extraordinary claim. The more plausible model is paracrine: the cells secrete cytokines, extracellular vesicles and trophic factors that could modulate inflammation, vascular injury, cell survival and endogenous repair.

That biology is at least conceivable in neonatal injury, where secondary damage evolves after the initial insult. Microglial activation, blood-brain barrier disruption, excitotoxicity and oxidative stress can expand the lesion over time. A transient immunomodulatory intervention might therefore preserve vulnerable oligodendrocyte precursors or neurons without needing to reconstruct an entire circuit.

Still, nine patients are nine patients. The study apparently combined two conditions that have different severity distributions and prognoses, and the comparison is against an external historical mortality estimate rather than a randomized concurrent control group. Patient selection, supportive care and baseline disease severity could easily dominate the outcome. Zero deaths is worth following. It is not yet proof that the cells prevented deaths, and we also need neurodevelopmental outcomes—motor function, cognition, hearing, vision and seizure burden—not survival alone.

Axiom now intends to become what it says would be the first U.S. biotech primarily listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, with a Nasdaq co-listing later. A listing simply means selling publicly traded shares on that exchange. Hong Kong allows pre-revenue biotechs to list once a core product has passed specified clinical milestones, including initial phase 1 development. Axiom’s Asian investors and Korean development relationship make this less random than it sounds.

The proposed IPO is not expected until 2027. So this is a financing plan, not money in the bank. But it illustrates how companies are shopping globally for public investors rather than treating Nasdaq as the only door.

That leads naturally to Latigo Biotherapeutics, which is also preparing an IPO, this time for a much more familiar neuroscience proposition: block peripheral pain signaling without opioids.

Latigo’s lead molecule, LTG-001, inhibits Nav1.8, a voltage-gated sodium channel enriched in peripheral sensory neurons, particularly nociceptors. Sodium channels generate the inward current that initiates and propagates action potentials. Nav1.8 is appealing because its expression is concentrated largely outside the brain, offering a route to dampen pain transmission without directly engaging opioid receptors or broadly sedating the central nervous system.

Vertex validated the target when suzetrigine, sold as Journavx, became the first approved Nav1.8 inhibitor. Latigo is therefore not proving that Nav1.8 can be drugged. It must prove that LTG-001 is materially better.

The company says Journavx has limitations involving efficacy, onset and contraindications. Conveniently, those are exactly the areas Latigo wants investors to believe it can improve. LTG-001 is headed toward phase 3 in patients undergoing bunion surgery, plus another late-stage study spanning several surgical and nonsurgical settings. Data are planned for the second half of 2027.

Bunionectomy is a classic acute-pain trial model because the procedure is standardized and predictably painful. It is also a somewhat artificial proving ground. A positive result there does not guarantee broad utility in back pain, neuropathic pain or chronic musculoskeletal disease, where sensory mechanisms are more heterogeneous and central sensitization may already be established.

Latigo also has LTG-321 in phase 2 for osteoarthritis and a preclinical molecule, LTG-418, that could potentially support topical or locally delivered formulations. The company was incubated by Westlake Village BioPartners, using science originating at Baltimore’s Lieber Institute for Brain Development, and recruited former Amgen neuroscience employees after Amgen reduced that work in 2019. It has already raised $285 million across Series A and B rounds and employs 59 people. For job-market purposes, that is a clinical-stage neuroscience company moving from venture-backed expansion toward the operational grind of phase 3.

Pain development has no patience for merely elegant electrophysiology. The drug has to outperform placebo and existing analgesics by enough that surgeons, payers and patients notice.

This is particularly salient because Kolon TissueGene has just provided another reminder of what pain endpoints can do to a sophisticated therapy. Its cell-based gene therapy TG-C failed both co-primary endpoints in a 531-patient phase 3 knee osteoarthritis trial.

TG-C combines allogeneic human chondrocytes with an irradiated human embryonic-kidney-derived cell line engineered to express TGF-beta-1. TGF-beta signaling can promote extracellular-matrix production and influence cartilage biology, although it can also drive fibrosis and other context-dependent effects. The intended product is therefore part cell therapy, part local cytokine-delivery system.

One year after a single intra-articular injection, TG-C was statistically no better than placebo on either knee pain or function. It also missed every key secondary endpoint.

Kolon still has a nearly identical phase 3 trial reporting in October, so the company will wait before formally deciding the program’s fate. But highly similar trials usually do not produce radically different truths. They can produce different p-values, certainly, but investors will now assume the first failure is the default and require unusually compelling data to change their minds.

There is historical baggage as well. South Korea approved TG-C in 2017, then revoked the authorization two years later after the identity of one cell component was found to be different from what had originally been represented. Even before this phase 3 miss, product characterization and trust were central issues.

Its lead program, STX-1150, is designed to silence PCSK9 in vivo. PCSK9 normally promotes degradation of the LDL receptor. Reduce PCSK9, and hepatocytes retain more LDL receptors on their surface, pulling more LDL cholesterol out of circulation. This target is already clinically validated by antibodies such as Repatha and by Novartis’ RNA-interference drug Leqvio.

The competitive question is duration. Leqvio is dosed every six months. Scribe’s CEO has suggested STX-1150 might eventually need a booster only once every decade or two.

That would be remarkable—and it raises the safety bar accordingly. A six-month therapy can eventually wear off. A durable genetic intervention must be delivered to the correct cells, avoid consequential off-target activity and remain tolerable even if biology changes over the next fifteen years. The isotope is not magic; target density, internalization, dosimetry and tumor heterogeneity still rule.
