Today’s thread is the distance between biological plausibility and evidence strong enough to support irreversible treatment.

Let’s begin with Huntington’s disease, because the FDA has accepted uniQure’s application for AMT-130—now formally called ifezuntirgene inilparvovec—and granted priority review.

A decision is expected by May 1, 2027. The FDA also tentatively plans to convene an advisory committee, meaning outside experts may publicly debate whether the evidence supports approval.

This is genuinely new. We discussed the program in September, when its four-year update sent uniQure shares down 41%. At 36 months, the high-dose group had appeared to experience 75% slower progression on the composite Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale, or cUHDRS. At 48 months, that estimate fell to 44% and was no longer statistically significant.

The FDA has nevertheless accepted a filing based principally on the earlier, 36-month analysis. Acceptance does not mean the agency has decided the drug works. It means the application is sufficiently complete to undergo substantive review.

AMT-130 is an AAV-delivered microRNA therapy infused directly into the brain through small openings in the skull. The microRNA is designed to reduce huntingtin messenger RNA, lowering both mutant and normal huntingtin protein.

Huntington’s disease is caused by an expanded CAG repeat in the HTT gene. That creates an abnormally long polyglutamine tract in huntingtin protein, promoting misfolding, altered protein interactions and progressive dysfunction of vulnerable neurons. Striatal medium spiny neurons are especially affected, but cortical and other networks also deteriorate.

The therapy is therefore a one-time, invasive attempt to lower the supply of the toxic protein. It is not allele-selective. That means dose selection must balance two competing effects: enough knockdown to reduce mutant huntingtin toxicity, without removing so much normal huntingtin that its ordinary cellular functions become a problem.

The regulatory difficulty is that uniQure’s treated cohort is small and was not randomized against a concurrent four-year placebo group. The company compared patients with an external natural-history dataset. By year four, a large amount of comparator data was missing, and the remaining external controls appeared to be progressing more slowly. Change the control group, and the estimated treatment effect changes with it.

One functional measure has been more stable. Total Functional Capacity, or TFC, suggested approximately 61% slower decline at four years. TFC asks whether a person can work, manage finances, perform household tasks and live independently. It is less mechanistically rich than a multidomain composite, but much easier to explain around a kitchen table.

UniQure has now aligned with the FDA on a 200-patient confirmatory study. Patients will be randomized two-to-one to gene therapy or standard care, with TFC assessed at 35 months. Screening should begin before the end of this year, ensuring that the trial is underway before the approval decision.

That design also tells us what regulators remain worried about. A 200-patient randomized study would not be necessary if the existing efficacy evidence were considered conclusive.

The advisory committee, if held, will probably focus on three questions. Is the 36-month result credible despite the unstable external control? Does the more consistent TFC signal provide enough confidence? And how much uncertainty is acceptable for an irreversible neurosurgical gene therapy in a fatal disease with no proven disease-modifying treatment?

My read is that acceptance keeps approval very much alive, but it does not rehabilitate the 75% slowing claim. The company now has a review, not vindication. If approval comes, it will likely arrive with substantial postmarketing obligations and intense scrutiny of the confirmatory trial.

Our second Huntington-adjacent story is really about brain delivery more broadly.

Novartis has ended its collaboration with BioArctic only 14 months after signing it. Novartis paid $30 million upfront and had offered up to $772 million in milestones to combine an undisclosed neurodegeneration antibody with BioArctic’s BrainTransporter platform.

Those $772 million were biobucks: contingent payments tied to future success. BioArctic keeps the $30 million. Almost all of the larger headline disappears.

BrainTransporter uses the transferrin receptor, or TfR, as a route across brain endothelium. The blood-brain barrier is formed by tightly joined endothelial cells supported by pericytes, astrocytic endfeet and specialized transport systems. Large antibodies barely diffuse through it.

A brain shuttle attaches therapeutic cargo to a binder that engages a receptor on endothelial cells. The receptor-cargo complex enters the cell and, if the engineering works, crosses to the brain-facing side through transcytosis.

TfR is attractive because brain endothelial cells use it to acquire iron-bearing transferrin. But affinity and valency are critical. An antibody that binds too tightly, or clusters the receptor too strongly, can become trapped in endosomes and routed to lysosomes instead of released into brain tissue. A successful shuttle must bind well enough to enter, but not so avidly that it refuses to leave.

BioArctic says Novartis’ decision was unrelated to BrainTransporter itself. That may be true. The undisclosed antibody target could have failed internal validation, lost strategic priority or become commercially unattractive. Without knowing the target, we cannot distinguish delivery failure from portfolio triage.

The broader Novartis behavior supports the portfolio explanation. In September, it paid $125 million to acquire Sironax’s brain-delivery platform after evaluating it for more than a year. So this is not a retreat from brain shuttles. It is Novartis pruning one target-platform combination while keeping another architecture. BioArctic still has BrainTransporter partnerships with Lilly and Bristol Myers Squibb.

For scientists, the important experimental question is not whether an antibody appears somewhere in a brain homogenate. It is quantitative, cell-resolved pharmacology: how much intact cargo leaves the endothelium, how evenly it distributes and whether it engages the intended neuronal or glial target without excessive peripheral exposure.

Now to Teva, where a phase 2 multiple-system-atrophy study has stopped early for futility.

An independent monitoring committee concluded that the 350-patient trial of emrusolmin had a low probability of meeting its primary efficacy endpoint if it continued. No safety concern prompted the recommendation. Teva will inspect the full data before formally deciding the program’s fate, but futility stops of this kind rarely end in resurrection.

Multiple system atrophy, or MSA, combines autonomic failure with parkinsonism, cerebellar dysfunction or both. Patients may experience severe blood-pressure instability, urinary dysfunction, impaired movement, balance problems and progressive loss of independence.

Like Parkinson’s disease, MSA is an alpha-synucleinopathy. The cellular geography differs, however. Parkinson’s is classically associated with alpha-synuclein-rich Lewy pathology in neurons. MSA is distinguished by glial cytoplasmic inclusions, particularly in oligodendrocytes.

Emrusolmin, derived from Modag’s anle138b program, was designed to interfere with pathological protein oligomers, including aggregated alpha-synuclein species. Oligomers are assemblies intermediate between individual soluble proteins and mature fibrils. They may be especially toxic because they interact with membranes, disrupt trafficking and propagate abnormal conformations.

The therapeutic proposition is appealing: bind or destabilize toxic assemblies before they spread and damage additional cells. The difficulty is knowing which alpha-synuclein species drives human disease, whether sufficient drug reaches it and whether treatment begins before too much circuitry is already lost.

This futility result does not prove alpha-synuclein is irrelevant. It does weaken the argument that this particular small-molecule oligomer modulator can alter established MSA over the tested period.

For Teva, the program had once been a pillar of its attempt to become more than a generics company. The surviving innovation strategy now rests more heavily on its long-acting olanzapine formulation and anti-TL1A antibody. For neurodegeneration scientists, this is another reminder that strong pathological association is not the same as a druggable rate-limiting step.

The next CNS setback is more provisional.

The FDA has partially held Bright Minds Biosciences’ planned phase 2 trial of BMB-101 in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. Ongoing studies are unaffected, but the U.S. pediatric trial cannot proceed as proposed.

BMB-101 activates the serotonin 5-HT2C receptor. This is a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in neural circuits controlling excitability, appetite and other functions. Activating it can reduce seizure susceptibility, probably through distributed effects on inhibitory and excitatory networks rather than one simple ion-channel brake.

The class has clinical precedent. Fintepla, or fenfluramine, is approved for severe developmental epilepsies and has activity involving serotonergic systems, including 5-HT2C. Bright Minds is trying to obtain cleaner receptor selectivity, particularly against 5-HT2A and 5-HT2B. That distinction is relevant because 5-HT2A drives psychedelic effects, while sustained 5-HT2B activation has been associated with cardiac-valve pathology.

Bright Minds previously reported a 73.1% average reduction in absence seizures among 11 treated patients. That is encouraging and extremely small.

Juvenile animals can metabolize drugs differently from adults, and developing organs may have vulnerabilities that ordinary adult toxicology misses. Bright Minds raised $175 million in January, so it can probably fund additional studies. The 49% stock decline reflects time and uncertainty: nobody yet knows whether resolving the hold requires a limited analysis, a full juvenile study or a redesign that delays the program substantially.

Tiziana Life Sciences has a more positive—but highly qualified—multiple-sclerosis result.

This earns a larger, longer study. It does not establish disease modification, and Tiziana’s suggested expansion into Alzheimer’s, ALS and MSA is running ahead of the controlled evidence.
