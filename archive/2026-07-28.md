The TL;DR: MapLight Therapeutics lost more than 60% of its value after a schizophrenia trial produced one positive dose and one failed dose. Its M1/M4 muscarinic agonist did reduce symptoms, but by a modest placebo-adjusted 4.5 points on the PANSS scale—less than Bristol Myers Squibb’s Cobenfy achieved in its pivotal studies. In genetic neurology, Oak Hill Bio is using a SPAC merger to raise $175 million and advance an Angelman-syndrome antisense drug that reactivates the normally silent paternal copy of UBE3A. Apnimed is seeking up to $160 million in an IPO ahead of a potential first-in-class sleep-apnea pill. We also have a $2.2 billion autoimmune acquisition by argenx, a $118 million corneal-regeneration financing with unusually strong imaging relevance, and a useful contrast in ADC development: Hansoh and GSK scored another phase 3 win, while AstraZeneca killed two internally developed conjugates. Finally, synapse biologist Michael Ehlers is joining AI drug developer Formation Bio as chief scientist.

Let’s begin with MapLight, because this is the sort of trial that is scientifically positive, financially disastrous and strategically unresolved—all at once.

The phase 2 Zephyr study enrolled 307 patients hospitalized with acute worsening of schizophrenia. MapLight tested two dosing regimens of ML-007C-MA, an M1 and M4 muscarinic-receptor agonist. The twice-daily regimen met the primary endpoint. After five weeks, it improved Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, or PANSS, scores by 4.5 points relative to placebo.

The once-daily regimen failed.

Investors responded with the subtlety of a falling piano. MapLight shares dropped from $36.56 to roughly $13.76, wiping out more than 60% of the company’s market value that morning.

Why such a severe reaction when one arm technically succeeded? First, 4.5 points is a modest effect, and analysts noted that it was smaller than the placebo-adjusted improvements reported for Cobenfy, Bristol Myers Squibb’s now-approved schizophrenia therapy. Cobenfy combines xanomeline, which stimulates central M1 and M4 receptors, with the peripherally restricted antimuscarinic trospium to reduce cholinergic adverse effects outside the brain.

This muscarinic approach is important because it does not directly block dopamine D2 receptors, unlike most antipsychotics. M1 receptors influence cortical cognition and synaptic plasticity, while M4 signaling can indirectly restrain dopaminergic activity in relevant circuits. In principle, that could reduce psychosis without producing the movement disorders, prolactin elevation and other liabilities associated with strong D2 antagonism.

But once Cobenfy validated the class, MapLight’s job changed. It no longer needs merely to show that muscarinic agonism works. It needs to show why physicians would choose its product instead.

MapLight pointed to a six-point improvement among patients who completed all five weeks, but that is not the cleanest analysis. Patients who tolerate and remain on therapy can differ systematically from those who drop out. The randomized intention-to-treat result—the 4.5-point figure—is the one to keep in your head.

There was, however, a genuinely interesting cognitive signal. The twice-daily regimen improved a measure of cognitive impairment, and that effect did not appear to track simply with reduced psychosis. No drug is currently approved specifically for cognitive impairment in schizophrenia, despite cognition being a major determinant of functional outcome. If that observation replicates, MapLight may have a differentiator. For now, it is an exploratory finding from a mixed phase 2 study.

It may also support the ongoing trial in Alzheimer’s-disease psychosis, where avoiding constipation and other peripheral anticholinergic effects is especially important. Elderly patients are exquisitely unforgiving of drugs that worsen bowel function, urinary retention, confusion or orthostatic instability.

So MapLight still has a program. What it does not yet have is a clean commercial story.

Our second CNS item is Oak Hill Bio, which is going public through a SPAC merger and bringing an Angelman-syndrome antisense program with it.

A SPAC is essentially a publicly listed pool of cash created to merge with a private company. Instead of Oak Hill undertaking a conventional IPO roadshow, it merges into that existing shell and inherits its Nasdaq listing. The transaction is expected to provide $175 million: $75 million held by the SPAC and another $100 million from a private financing led by RA Capital. The combined company will trade as OAKH.

Its lead drug, rugonersen, tackles one of the more elegant mechanisms in neurogenetics. Angelman syndrome is generally caused by loss or mutation of the maternally inherited UBE3A allele. In many tissues, the paternal copy can still function. In neurons, however, the paternal allele is silenced by a long antisense transcript called UBE3A-ATS.

Rugonersen is an antisense oligonucleotide designed to degrade that silencing transcript. Remove the molecular brake, and the paternal UBE3A allele may resume producing protein.

UBE3A is an E3 ubiquitin ligase, helping label specific proteins for degradation and thereby regulating proteostasis, synaptic development and plasticity. The challenge is that Angelman syndrome begins during development. Restoring UBE3A later may improve neuronal function, but it may not reverse every circuit-level consequence of having lacked the protein during critical periods.

There is also a race underway. Ionis’ ION582 and Ultragenyx’s GTX-102 are already in phase 3. Oak Hill licensed rugonersen from Roche and has now dosed the first patient in its own phase 3 BEACON trial, with a readout and regulatory submission targeted for 2029.

For scientists considering this space, Angelman is becoming a real-world test of whether ASO-mediated unsilencing can move beyond molecular rescue to meaningful gains in communication, motor control, sleep and daily function. Biomarker evidence will not be enough. Families need changes they can see.

Next, Apnimed hopes to raise up to $160 million in an IPO as it prepares for a February FDA decision on Oxnimbi, or AD109, a daily pill for obstructive sleep apnea.

Oxnimbi combines aroxybutynin, an antimuscarinic, with atomoxetine, the norepinephrine-reuptake inhibitor already used for ADHD. During sleep—particularly REM sleep—the neural drive to upper-airway muscles falls. In susceptible patients, the pharyngeal airway collapses repeatedly, producing oxygen desaturation, brief arousals and cardiovascular stress.

The combination is intended to increase motor-neuron output to the muscles that hold the airway open. In one 660-patient phase 3 study, it reduced nightly apnea and hypopnea events by 46.8%.

That is substantial, although a percentage reduction is not the same as eliminating disease. We will want absolute apnea-hypopnea index values, the fraction of patients crossing into milder disease categories, improvements in daytime sleepiness and the adverse-event profile. Atomoxetine can affect heart rate and blood pressure; antimuscarinic pharmacology can produce dry mouth, constipation and urinary effects.

Commercially, the attraction is obvious. CPAP is effective when used consistently, but sleeping in a pressurized mask is not everyone’s idea of elegant human-centered design. Zepbound is approved for sleep apnea in adults with obesity, but Oxnimbi could become the first prescription drug specifically developed for obstructive sleep apnea regardless of obesity status.

The IPO money is therefore launch capital, not just laboratory capital. Apnimed is preparing sales, medical-affairs and payer-access infrastructure before it knows whether approval will arrive.

Now to the front of the eye, where Claris Biotherapeutics emerged with a $118 million Series B to develop CSB-001 for limbal stem-cell deficiency.

The limbus is the border region between cornea and sclera, and its stem cells continually replenish the corneal epithelium. When those cells are depleted by chemical injury, inflammation or chronic disease, conjunctival-like tissue and blood vessels can invade the corneal surface. Transparency deteriorates. Vision follows.

CSB-001 is an eye-drop formulation of oremepermin alfa, a recombinant form of hepatocyte growth factor. Intriguingly, it originally failed in neurotrophic keratitis. An investigator then noticed that a subgroup with limbal stem-cell deficiency appeared to regain vision. That clinical failure became a disease-repositioning clue.

Claris is now running a natural-history study before launching phase 3 in the first half of 2027. That preparatory work matters because rare ophthalmic diseases need reproducible definitions of progression and response. This is highly imaging-relevant: slit-lamp photography, corneal confocal microscopy, epithelial mapping and quantitative assessment of vascularization can reveal whether treatment is restoring a stable, transparent epithelial phenotype rather than merely changing a symptom score.

Claris spun out of Reza Dana’s corneal-disease laboratory at Harvard, has grown from five employees to more than 25 and is now establishing a physical headquarters. For the job market, this is a small translational ophthalmology company building both clinical and operational teams—not a mature retinal-drug machine with every function already fixed in place.

In autoimmunity, argenx is spending $2.2 billion to acquire Forte Biosciences and its anti-CD122 antibody FB102.

CD122 is the shared beta receptor chain used by IL-2 and IL-15 signaling. Those cytokines support several immune populations, including effector T cells and natural killer cells. Argenx believes FB102 can suppress pathogenic T- and NK-cell activity while preserving enough regulatory T-cell biology to maintain immune tolerance. That selectivity claim is central and will need careful human validation.

The acquisition follows a phase 1b vitiligo study in which FB102 produced a 29.6% improvement in a facial-vitiligo score. That is interesting. It is also extremely early evidence on which to spend $2.2 billion.

Argenx is buying optionality: vitiligo, celiac disease, alopecia areata and potentially other autoimmune disorders—a “pipeline in a product,” as industry likes to say when one molecule might justify many trials. The company has earned credibility through Vyvgart, but this price says it believes its indication-selection and antibody-development machinery can create considerably more value than Forte could alone.

Finally, ADCs offered a nice lesson in why the modality cannot be treated as interchangeable molecular Lego.

Hansoh reported that GSK-partnered risvutatug rezetecan, a B7-H3-directed ADC, improved progression-free survival over chemotherapy in heavily pretreated osteosarcoma. Overall survival also reportedly favored the ADC, though no actual numbers have been released. This follows a phase 3 win in small-cell lung cancer.

B7-H3 is expressed on multiple tumors and can be relatively limited in normal tissue, making it an attractive delivery address. But an ADC succeeds only if antigen density, internalization, linker stability, payload release and systemic pharmacokinetics all cooperate.

AstraZeneca demonstrated the other side of that equation by terminating two internally developed ADCs. An EGFR-cMET topoisomerase-1 inhibitor conjugate showed unimpressive pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, while a Claudin-18.2 conjugate was stopped for inadequate efficacy. AstraZeneca knows ADCs—it co-developed Enhertu and Datroway with Daiichi Sankyo—but success with one linker-payload architecture does not confer universal competence across every target.

And one career note worth flagging: Michael Ehlers, the former Duke neurobiology professor and Biogen R&D chief, is joining Formation Bio as chief scientific officer and head of R&D. For a synapse biologist, Ehlers is a familiar name from work on receptor trafficking and activity-dependent synaptic organization.
