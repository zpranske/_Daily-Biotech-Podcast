Let’s begin with Huntington’s disease, because uniQure’s four-year update is both scientifically important and statistically uncomfortable.

AMT-130 is an AAV gene therapy delivered directly into the brain. It carries a microRNA construct designed to reduce huntingtin messenger RNA and therefore lower production of huntingtin protein, including the mutant expanded form responsible for Huntington’s disease.

Huntington’s is caused by an expanded CAG repeat in the HTT gene. The resulting protein contains an abnormally long polyglutamine tract, misfolds, disrupts transcription and protein homeostasis, and progressively damages vulnerable neuronal populations—most famously medium spiny neurons in the striatum, although cortical and other circuits are also affected.

One year ago, uniQure reported that the high-dose therapy had slowed progression by 75% at month 36 on the composite Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale, or cUHDRS. That composite integrates motor function, cognition and the patient’s ability to perform daily activities.

At month 48, the estimated slowing fell to 44% and was no longer statistically significant. Investors cut uniQure’s share price by 41%.

That sounds like efficacy fading. It may be. But this study has only 12 treated patients in the relevant analysis, and it does not contain a randomized placebo arm extending for four years. Instead, uniQure compares treated patients with an external natural-history control assembled from people observed elsewhere.

External controls can be necessary in rare or devastating diseases, especially for invasive one-time procedures. They are also vulnerable to hidden differences in age, genotype, baseline function, clinical care and dropout.

Here, more than half of the month-48 external-control data were missing. According to uniQure, the patients disappearing from that dataset had been declining faster than the ones who remained. The comparator therefore became healthier over time, making AMT-130 look less effective. One outlier also contributed roughly one-third of the control group’s deterioration between years three and four.

Those are legitimate concerns. They are not a substitute for randomization.

A post hoc analysis using the company’s previous control dataset estimated 54% slowing. But changing the comparator after seeing how the analysis behaves is exactly the kind of flexibility that makes regulators nervous.

The encouraging part is Total Functional Capacity, or TFC. That measure asks whether someone can work, manage finances, perform household tasks and remain independent. AMT-130 showed 61% slowing on TFC at month 48, essentially unchanged from 60% at month 36.

That stability matters because TFC is also the planned primary endpoint of uniQure’s confirmatory trial. It is more clinically legible than a composite whose components may move in different directions. Families care whether a patient can still organize a day and live independently.

uniQure has already filed for accelerated approval using the month-36 dataset. The FDA must now decide whether the newer analysis weakens that package enough to demand stronger prospective evidence first. My read: the program is still alive, but the 75% slowing figure should be retired. The four-year data no longer support that level of confidence, and external controls are becoming the central liability in what was supposed to be a durability update.

Now to ai3Bio, a Massachusetts autoimmune startup emerging with $48 million and a memorable thesis: if a pathogenic immune cell is the gun and its cytokine is the bullet, stop blocking bullets and remove the gun.

The company is focused on pathogenic Th17 cells. These CD4 helper T cells can produce IL-17 and related inflammatory mediators, and they contribute to diseases including psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease and multiple sclerosis. Existing drugs such as Cosentyx neutralize IL-17A. ai3Bio instead wants to eliminate the disease-driving Th17 population selectively.

Its first approach, called StarX, uses lipid nanoparticles targeted toward CD161, a receptor enriched on certain Th17 and other immune-cell populations. The particles carry an mRNA payload intended to activate a cGAS-STING-associated cell-death program inside the targeted cells.

There is an important mechanistic gap in the public description. cGAS directly senses cytosolic DNA, not ordinary mRNA, so the payload presumably encodes something that activates or engages the pathway rather than acting as a direct RNA ligand. Until ai3Bio identifies that cargo, the phrase “activates cGAS-STING” is more destination than route map.

And STING activation is not a polite apoptosis switch. It can drive type-one-interferon production and substantial inflammation. Selective delivery therefore has to be excellent. A particle meant to delete pathogenic Th17 cells could become counterproductive if it broadly activates innate immunity in liver, spleen or myeloid cells.

The second platform, T Deplete, is more conventional: an antibody recognizes the same pathogenic population and recruits immune effectors to clear it.

CD161 is not exclusive to Th17 cells; it also appears on natural killer cells, mucosal-associated invariant T cells and other subsets. The company therefore needs high-dimensional single-cell profiling showing exactly which cells disappear, from which tissues and for how long. “Th17 depletion” measured only in peripheral blood would be inadequate if the pathogenic cells persist in gut, skin or lymphoid tissue.

Still, this is a timely idea. Autoimmune CAR-T is demonstrating that deep immune-cell deletion can generate durable remission, but recent deaths have exposed the hazards of powerful, self-expanding cell therapy. ai3Bio is attempting a more targeted and potentially controllable version. The $48 million financing is meaningful, though not generous for two delivery platforms. Expect rapid prioritization.

Obesity gives us today’s strongest example of the market splitting into distinct product profiles.

Lilly’s retatrutide produced 20.8% mean weight loss at 80 weeks in adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes, versus 4% on placebo. More than half of patients receiving the top dose lost at least 20% of body weight, and 34.9% lost at least 25%.

Retatrutide activates GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors—the “triple G.” GLP-1 and GIP support insulin secretion and appetite regulation. Glucagon can increase hepatic glucose output, but it may also increase energy expenditure and lipid mobilization. Retatrutide is trying to exploit the latter without allowing the former to overwhelm glycemic control.

That tradeoff appears in the data. Weight loss is extraordinary, but hemoglobin A1C fell only 1.4 to 1.6 percentage points, and there was no clear dose response. Around 40% of patients reached an A1C below 5.7%, compared with as many as 55.3% in an earlier tirzepatide study.

Cross-trial comparison is imperfect, but the product identity is emerging: retatrutide may be a stronger obesity drug than diabetes drug. Lilly plans to file early next year.

Roche and Zealand’s petrelintide occupies a different lane. This long-acting amylin analog produced its best week-28 weight loss at the middle, five-milligram dose: 9.8%, versus 1.7% on placebo. Higher doses did no better—9.3% and 9.4%.

Amylin is co-secreted with insulin and helps promote satiety, slow gastric emptying and suppress inappropriate glucagon release after meals. Petrelintide’s proposed advantage is tolerability. Nausea affected 20% of treated participants versus 6% on placebo, but vomiting and diarrhea were no more common than with placebo.
