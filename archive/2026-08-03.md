Let’s begin with Novo Nordisk, because this is almost a textbook experiment in the difference between changing a biomarker and changing a patient’s fate.

Novo’s antibody ziltivekimab binds the cytokine interleukin-6, or IL-6. The phase 3 Zeus trial enrolled more than 6,300 people with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease and evidence of systemic inflammation. These were patients at substantial risk of cardiovascular death, heart attack or stroke, and the biological hypothesis was quite reasonable: suppress IL-6-driven inflammation and perhaps stabilize atherosclerotic plaques enough to prevent those events.

The antibody successfully inhibited the IL-6 pathway.

It did not prevent the events.

The hazard ratio was 0.99. A hazard ratio of one means the treatment and placebo groups experienced events at essentially the same rate. Analysts had been looking for something closer to 0.85—a 15% relative reduction that might support broad use. Instead, ziltivekimab delivered approximately one percent, statistically indistinguishable from nothing.

That is not a near miss. It is difficult to blame this on a slightly underpowered trial or an unfortunate endpoint definition.

The result is especially interesting because inflammation really does contribute to atherosclerosis. IL-6 can drive hepatic production of C-reactive protein, influence leukocyte recruitment and amplify innate immune signaling within vascular lesions. Previous cardiovascular studies have also shown that anti-inflammatory intervention can reduce events. The CANTOS trial, for example, validated IL-1-beta inhibition as a way to lower cardiovascular risk, although infection risk, cost and modest absolute benefit limited commercial enthusiasm.

So why did direct IL-6-ligand blockade fail here? One cytokine can sit in the middle of a beautiful pathway diagram without controlling enough of the human phenotype to become a useful drug target.
