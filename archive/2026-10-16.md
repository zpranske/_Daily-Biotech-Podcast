The running thread is selective complexity: investors will finance sophisticated modalities, but increasingly around assets with a short route to decisive human evidence.

Let’s begin with BioNTech, because this is the most consequential clinical result today and a useful counterweight to the excitement around mRNA cancer vaccines.

BioNTech’s BNT113 regimen failed to improve progression-free survival over Keytruda alone in HPV16-positive metastatic head-and-neck squamous-cell carcinoma. The phase 3 portion of the Ahead-Merit trial tested Keytruda plus BNT113 against Keytruda by itself.

Progression-free survival, or PFS, measures how long patients remain alive without their cancer measurably worsening. It was one of two primary endpoints, and it missed.

Overall survival is the second primary endpoint. Those data remain immature, and the independent monitoring committee recommended that the study continue. Under the statistical plan, an overall-survival success could still make this a positive trial.

That outcome is possible. It is not the most likely conclusion after a PFS failure.

BNT113 differs importantly from Moderna and Merck’s intismeran, whose successful phase 3 melanoma trial we discussed in August. Intismeran is personalized: each patient’s tumor is sequenced, mutations are identified, and a bespoke mRNA vaccine encodes selected neoantigens unique to that cancer.

BNT113 is a fixed-antigen vaccine. It encodes the HPV16 oncoproteins E6 and E7.

Those are attractive immune targets because HPV-positive tumor cells depend on them. E6 and E7 disrupt tumor-suppressor pathways involving p53 and retinoblastoma protein, helping infected cells escape normal cell-cycle control. Because these are viral proteins rather than ordinary human proteins, the immune system should, in principle, recognize them as foreign.

The mRNA is intended to generate E6- and E7-derived antigens that antigen-presenting cells can process and display on MHC molecules. T cells recognizing those peptide-MHC complexes should then expand and attack tumor cells displaying the same viral peptides. Keytruda blocks PD-1, removing one inhibitory signal from those T cells.

It is an elegant combination: provide a recognizable antigen and release an immune brake.

But antigen recognition is only one step. T cells must be primed, traffic into the tumor, recognize sufficient antigen on malignant cells and remain functional in a microenvironment containing suppressive myeloid cells, regulatory T cells, inhibitory cytokines and metabolic stress. Tumors can also alter MHC expression or antigen processing. A viral target may be foreign, but it is not automatically accessible.

BioNTech argues that vaccine-induced immunity could deepen over time, eventually improving survival even without extending PFS. There is precedent. Keytruda itself missed PFS but improved overall survival in an earlier head-and-neck trial. Immunotherapies can produce delayed, durable responses that are imperfectly captured by early imaging.

Still, analysts are appropriately less confident now. Most trials that fail PFS do not later reveal a survival miracle.

This result also follows BioNTech and Genentech’s termination of a personalized colorectal-cancer vaccine study after more deaths occurred in the vaccine arm. Meanwhile, Moderna’s successful melanoma regimen combined a personalized vaccine with Keytruda in a particularly immunogenic cancer.

So the field’s current scorecard is not “mRNA cancer vaccines work.” It is narrower: one personalized vaccine-checkpoint combination has won phase 3 in resected melanoma, while other antigen designs, tumor types and treatment settings remain uncertain. Context keeps refusing to be optional.

Now to CNS, where Vanda Pharmaceuticals has failed phase 3 in social anxiety disorder.

The study enrolled 546 adults and tested one dose of VQW-765, an alpha-7 nicotinic acetylcholine-receptor partial agonist, against placebo. Participants then completed a psychosocial stress test involving an interview-style presentation before an intentionally unreassuring panel. A fairly efficient way to manufacture social anxiety in a conference room.

The drug did not reduce distress in women, the prespecified primary population. It also failed the secondary analysis including both women and men.

Alpha-7 nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels activated by acetylcholine. Unlike muscarinic receptors, which signal through G proteins, nicotinic receptors directly permit ions to cross the membrane. Alpha-7 receptors are highly permeable to calcium and can influence neurotransmitter release, neuronal excitability, attention and plasticity. They are also expressed on some immune cells, where their biology becomes considerably more complicated than a simple neuronal switch.

A partial agonist activates the receptor less strongly than a full agonist. That can theoretically provide enough signaling to improve function without causing excessive activation or rapid desensitization.

Novartis originally developed VQW-765 in schizophrenia before transferring it to Vanda in 2014. Vanda then moved it into anxiety. Phase 2 failed in the overall population but produced an apparently significant result in women. Phase 3 was designed around that subgroup.

It did not replicate.

This is why post hoc or subgroup findings need prospective confirmation. Sex differences in receptor biology, pharmacokinetics and anxiety phenotypes can be biologically plausible. But biological plausibility is cheap after a dataset has already shown you where to look. The confirmatory trial asks whether the effect survives when the hypothesis is stated in advance. Here, it did not.

Vanda has paused the program while examining the complete data. Social-anxiety drug development has become a small graveyard: Vistagen recently failed two phase 3 studies and is also leaning on a post hoc subgroup. The industry read should be skeptical. Another favorable slice of a negative trial would not meaningfully improve VQW-765’s position.

The more encouraging nucleic-acid story is financial rather than clinical. City Therapeutics has raised $200 million in an upsized Nasdaq IPO.

City was founded in 2024 with backing from several Alnylam veterans and operates in Cambridge, Massachusetts with 81 employees. The IPO follows a $99.5 million Series B in June and comes only weeks after ADARx raised $446 million publicly.

City is developing three GalNAc-conjugated siRNA drugs. GalNAc binds the asialoglycoprotein receptor on hepatocytes, making the liver the most mature destination in RNA interference. Once internalized and released from endosomes, the siRNA guide strand enters RISC, which recognizes and cleaves complementary messenger RNA.

The first program, CITY-FXI, silences coagulation factor 11 and will move from phase 1 into a study of thrombosis after knee surgery.

Factor XI contributes to clot propagation through the intrinsic coagulation pathway but appears less essential for ordinary hemostasis than factor 10 or thrombin. People with inherited factor XI deficiency often have comparatively modest bleeding, suggesting that lowering it might prevent pathological clots while preserving a larger safety margin than conventional anticoagulants.

That therapeutic window is promising, not guaranteed. An siRNA also cannot be switched off quickly if a patient bleeds or needs emergency surgery.

The second program, CITY-RBP4, has an especially interesting liver-to-eye mechanism. RBP4, or retinol-binding protein 4, carries vitamin A derivatives in the circulation. Silencing hepatic RBP4 should reduce retinol delivery to the eye and thereby decrease formation of toxic bisretinoid compounds such as A2E.

In Stargardt disease, pathogenic ABCA4 variants impair normal handling of retinaldehyde-related material in photoreceptors. Bisretinoids accumulate in retinal pigment epithelial cells as lipofuscin, contributing to dysfunction and cell death. Geographic atrophy also involves degeneration of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium, although its biology is much broader than ABCA4 dysfunction.

The strategy is elegant because the drug acts in hepatocytes while changing retinal substrate availability. The tradeoff is equally clear: vitamin A supports normal vision and other physiology. City must reduce pathological retinoid burden without creating night-vision problems or systemic deficiency.

CITY-TFR2, the third program, targets transferrin receptor 2 for anemia. TFR2 participates in iron sensing and regulation of hepcidin, the hormone that restrains iron absorption and release. Reducing TFR2 signaling may lower hepcidin and make more iron available for red-blood-cell production, potentially helping anemia associated with chronic kidney disease or inflammation.

That expansion is scientifically sensible, but chronic inflammatory anemia also involves inadequate erythropoietin signaling, iron sequestration and altered marrow biology. Releasing iron may address one bottleneck without solving the entire disease.

City’s IPO is a meaningful Boston hiring signal for oligonucleotide chemistry, bioanalysis, clinical pharmacology and hepatic delivery. But note where the money is going: named clinical programs, not an unconstrained RNA-discovery empire.

OMass Therapeutics has raised an even larger private round: a $310 million Series C.

The Oxford spinout uses native mass spectrometry and related structural approaches to study difficult membrane proteins while preserving more of their biologically relevant conformations and ligand interactions. Its lead drug, OMS1620, is an antagonist of the melanocortin-2 receptor, or MC2R, for congenital adrenal hyperplasia and ACTH-dependent Cushing’s syndrome.

ACTH is released by the pituitary and activates MC2R on adrenal cortical cells, stimulating cortisol synthesis. Cortisol then feeds back to the hypothalamus and pituitary to restrain further ACTH release.

In congenital adrenal hyperplasia, impaired cortisol synthesis removes that negative feedback. ACTH rises, the adrenal glands become hyperplastic and steroid precursors can be diverted into excess androgen production. In ACTH-dependent Cushing’s, excessive ACTH drives pathological cortisol production.

OMS1620 is intended to block ACTH at the adrenal receptor. OMass describes the molecule as unusually “sticky,” meaning it has a slow dissociation rate and may remain receptor-bound even during daily ACTH surges. Residence time can matter as much as equilibrium affinity when ligand concentration fluctuates sharply.

The company is also developing an SLC15A4 inhibitor for lupus. SLC15A4 is an endolysosomal transporter linked to TLR7-and-TLR9-driven interferon signaling in immune cells. Finally, Gilead is paying Vir Biotechnology $90 million upfront for three masked T-cell engagers targeting solid tumors. Another $1.74 billion is possible in milestones, so most of the $1.9 billion headline remains biobucks.

A T-cell engager binds a tumor antigen with one arm and CD3 on T cells with another, forcing an immune synapse. This can be potent, but if the tumor antigen is also present on healthy tissue, or if the engager activates T cells systemically, cytokine release and on-target injury become serious constraints.

Vir’s PRO-XTEN platform places masks over the engager’s binding regions. The masks are intended to remain attached in circulation and then be removed by proteases enriched in the tumor microenvironment. The molecule should therefore become more active near the tumor and less active elsewhere.

“Should” is the operative word. Tumor protease activity varies among lesions and patients, while inflamed healthy tissues can express some of the same enzymes. Incomplete unmasking could reduce efficacy; premature unmasking could erase the safety advantage.

The structure of the deal is sensible. Vir receives meaningful cash and retains focus on its own programs.
