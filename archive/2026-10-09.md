Cerevance’s Parkinson’s result is the place to begin, because this is a phase 3 neuroscience win from a mechanism that had already disappointed once.

The 341-patient trial tested solengepras as an add-on to levodopa in people experiencing motor fluctuations. At the effective 150-milligram dose, solengepras reduced average daily OFF time by 0.61 hours relative to placebo at week 12. It also increased well-controlled ON time by 0.60 hours.

Those are internally consistent results. They are also the same quantity viewed from opposite sides of the day: roughly 37 fewer minutes with medication wearing off, and roughly 36 more minutes with symptoms controlled.

That is useful, but not transformative. In Parkinson’s disease, however, a predictable extra half-hour of movement can mean getting dressed, preparing food or leaving the house without waiting for the next levodopa dose to take effect. The practical value depends on whether the added ON time comes without troublesome dyskinesia, something today’s report does not fully detail.

Solengepras also improved a daily-living scale by 1.91 points and daytime sleepiness by 0.98 points versus placebo. Those secondary findings are interesting because the company is pitching a mechanism that could affect both motor and non-motor function.

The biology takes us into basal-ganglia circuitry. Dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra normally modulate striatal medium spiny neurons through two broad pathways. The direct pathway facilitates selected movement. The indirect pathway suppresses competing movement and acts rather like a brake.

When dopamine is lost, D2-receptor-expressing neurons in the indirect pathway become excessively active. The movement brake clamps down too hard.

GPR6 is an orphan G-protein-coupled receptor enriched in those striatopallidal, indirect-pathway neurons. One correction to the source article: these are not themselves the substantia-nigra dopamine-producing neurons. They are downstream striatal neurons that normally receive dopaminergic input.

GPR6 has substantial constitutive activity, meaning it can signal even without a known ligand bound to it. Solengepras is an inverse agonist, so it reduces that baseline receptor signaling rather than merely blocking an external agonist. The intended result is lower cyclic-AMP-associated activity in the indirect pathway and partial release of the pathological brake.

That makes solengepras non-dopaminergic, but not independent of dopamine circuitry. It works within a network whose state has been altered by dopamine loss.

The context is important. Solengepras previously failed as monotherapy in untreated Parkinson’s patients. It now succeeds as an adjunct to levodopa. That suggests GPR6 modulation is not strong enough to replace dopamine restoration but may smooth the gaps between levodopa doses.

Cerevance plans to speak with the FDA about filing. Watch the full safety data, dyskinesia measurements and whether regulators require another pivotal study. A 37-minute benefit can support approval if it is robust and clean. It does not leave much room for hidden tolerability problems.

Our second CNS story is Atsena Therapeutics’ gene therapy for X-linked retinoschisis, or XLRS, and this one is particularly relevant to cellular imaging.

XLRS is usually caused by pathogenic variants in RS1, which encodes retinoschisin. Retinoschisin is a secreted protein that helps maintain retinal organization and adhesion among photoreceptors and bipolar cells. Without functional protein, layers of the retina can split and form cyst-like cavities, particularly around the macula. Patients—overwhelmingly boys and men because the gene is X-linked—can lose central visual acuity and retinal sensitivity.

Atsena administered ATSN-201 to one eye in 18 patients. Three additional patients remained untreated as controls. Across the treated population, nine of 18 met the response criterion on microperimetry, compared with one response in an untreated fellow eye.

At the dose selected for phase 3, six of nine patients responded on microperimetry, compared with none of the three untreated controls. That denominator is tiny, but the convergence across measurements is encouraging. Twelve of 18 treated patients met optical-coherence-tomography response criteria, and nine improved on visual acuity.

Microperimetry maps light sensitivity at specific retinal locations while tracking fixation. It therefore connects retinal geography with function rather than asking only how many letters someone can read. OCT supplies the structural counterpart, using reflected light to generate cross-sectional images fine enough to reveal retinal layers and schisis cavities.

This is where multimodal evidence becomes valuable. Better visual acuity alone can fluctuate. An OCT improvement alone may not matter to the patient. Improvement in sensitivity, acuity and retinal architecture suggests a more coherent biological effect.

Atsena selected the lowest tested dose for phase 3 because it combined consistent efficacy with better tolerability than higher doses. That is a healthy dose-selection signal. Gene therapy does not award bonus points for administering the most vector possible.

The pivotal portion should finish enrollment early next year, with data and a possible filing in 2028. The current control group is far too small for certainty, but ATSN-201 now has a credible functional-and-imaging package.

Moving to protein degradation, Ono Pharmaceutical is paying Biohaven $80 million upfront for rights across Japan and several other Asian markets to three extracellular IgG degraders.

The lead asset, BHV-1300, is already in phase 3 for Graves’ disease. It is designed to reduce IgG1, IgG2 and IgG4 while sparing IgG3.

Biohaven’s MoDE platform uses bifunctional molecules that bind a disease-relevant extracellular protein—or, here, selected antibody subclasses—and recruit the liver’s asialoglycoprotein receptor. That receptor normally recognizes particular glycan patterns and internalizes its cargo into hepatocytes for lysosomal degradation.

So this is targeted protein degradation without entering the target cell first. Instead of sending a cytosolic protein to the proteasome, the molecule captures circulating IgG and reroutes it into an endocytic disposal pathway.

In Graves’ disease, pathogenic IgG binds the TSH receptor and activates it, driving excess thyroid-hormone production. Biohaven previously reported complete suppression of TSH-receptor-stimulating antibodies in one patient. One patient is mechanistic encouragement, not phase 3 validation.

The subclass selectivity is potentially important. Sparing IgG3 may preserve part of the antibody repertoire, including potent complement-fixing and antiviral functions, while removing subclasses that contain much of the pathogenic activity. But autoantibody subclass composition differs among patients, and broadly reducing three IgG subclasses could still produce infections or hypogammaglobulinemia.

Ono will also receive regional rights to BHV-1310 and BHV-1320 and will owe Biohaven 20% of local profits. For glycobiology and ligand-receptor scientists, this is a nice example of converting a native hepatic clearance receptor into a programmable therapeutic waste-disposal system.

Celiac disease gives us a different form of immune intervention.

Argenx says FB102, the anti-CD122 antibody acquired through its $2.2 billion takeover of Forte Biosciences, significantly protected intestinal structure during a controlled gluten challenge. The 126-patient phase 2 study met its primary endpoint on villus-height-to-crypt-depth ratio, with a p-value of 0.0176.

In active celiac disease, gluten-derived peptides are modified by tissue transglutaminase and presented by HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8. Adaptive T-cell responses develop, but IL-15 is particularly important in converting that response into epithelial injury. It activates cytotoxic intraepithelial lymphocytes, which attack stressed enterocytes. Villi flatten and crypts become hyperplastic, reducing absorptive surface area.

CD122 is the shared beta chain used by IL-2 and IL-15 receptors. Blocking it can therefore suppress IL-15-driven epithelial destruction, but it also affects IL-2 biology. That creates a mechanistic tension because regulatory T cells depend on IL-2 for survival and function. Dose and tissue exposure will determine whether FB102 suppresses pathological cytotoxic activity without undermining beneficial immune regulation.

Argenx has not disclosed the actual histological effect size, symptom improvement or detailed safety data. A p-value tells us the groups separated. It does not tell us whether the villi were substantially protected.

Endeavor BioMedicines has produced today’s most provocative fibrosis claim.

If those results are substantial, taladegib could be differentiated from Ofev and Esbriet, which slow functional decline but are not generally described as reversing established fibrosis.

Hedgehog signaling is essential during embryonic development and tissue patterning. In adult injury, reactivation can help coordinate repair. Chronic activation, however, may sustain pathological fibroblast states, extracellular-matrix deposition and tissue remodeling.

Endeavor believes taladegib targets a disease-driving fibroblast population and allows fibrotic lung tissue to regress. That is a large claim.
