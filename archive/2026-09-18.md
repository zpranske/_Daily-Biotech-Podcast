The TL;DR: Novartis has stopped its TREM2-activating antibody in ALS after a 251-patient phase 2 failure, extending an uncomfortable sequence of clinical setbacks for the company and for attempts to therapeutically reprogram microglia. Yet Novartis is simultaneously paying $125 million for Sironax’s blood-brain-barrier delivery platform, suggesting that it still sees CNS exposure—not a retreat from neuroscience—as a strategic priority. SK Biopharmaceuticals is making a much smaller, mostly biobucks Parkinson’s bet on dual LRRK2 and c-Abl inhibition. Bristol Myers Squibb has killed Orum’s CD33-targeted degrader-antibody conjugate after seeing phase 1 data, following an earlier AbbVie failure in the same young modality. Roche is paying $36.5 million upfront for a huge functional screen of more than 300,000 bispecific combinations. Electra completed an upsized $350 million IPO around a drug for lethal hyperinflammation, while protein-engineering startup Goa is pitching something rather audacious: an enzyme therapy that rapidly removes alcohol from blood.

Let’s begin with Novartis, where two stories arriving together capture the tension in contemporary neuroscience rather neatly: the company has lost another mechanism but bought another way to reach mechanisms.

Novartis has discontinued lifonebart, also called VHB937, in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis after the 251-patient phase 2 Astrals trial missed both its primary and secondary endpoints. The antibody was designed to stabilize and activate TREM2, a receptor expressed prominently by microglia.

Microglia are not merely the brain’s resident macrophages, although that description is a useful starting point. They survey tissue, respond to cellular injury, clear debris and alter synapses and inflammatory signaling according to context. TREM2 helps these cells sense lipids and damaged-cell-associated material and supports survival, migration, phagocytosis and metabolic adaptation.

The therapeutic argument is that TREM2 activation might push microglia toward a more protective state: better clearance of degenerating-cell debris, less maladaptive inflammation and more support for stressed neurons. In ALS, the hoped-for result was preservation of motor neurons and slower functional decline.

It did not happen.

This is not TREM2’s first clinical disappointment. Alector and AbbVie’s activating antibody missed in Alzheimer’s disease in 2024, and Vigil Neuroscience’s antibody also failed. Novartis is continuing lifonebart in an Alzheimer’s phase 2 trial because human genetics provide stronger evidence there: loss-of-function TREM2 variants substantially increase Alzheimer’s risk.

But genetic validation still does not tell us whether an activating antibody recreates the correct microglial state after neurodegeneration is established. TREM2 signaling may matter at particular disease stages, in particular microglial subpopulations or only in concert with lipid handling and other pathways. “Activate the protective microglia” is a pleasingly directional story. Microglia, inconveniently, occupy a continuum of spatial and transcriptional states rather than a binary good-versus-bad switch.

The ALS failure also joins a bruising run for Novartis: autoimmune CAR-T trials paused after three inflammatory deaths, an Lp(a)-lowering drug that changed the biomarker but not cardiovascular outcomes, and the phase 3 failure of Avidity’s muscle-targeted oligonucleotide in myotonic dystrophy.

And yet Novartis is paying Sironax $125 million to acquire a platform for transporting large molecules across the blood-brain barrier.

That is real upfront money, not a billion-dollar milestone cloud. Novartis had spent 14 months evaluating the technology under an option agreement before deciding to exercise it. Sironax retains some rights to use the platform, but Novartis gains control of the core delivery technology.

The exact molecular shuttle has not been disclosed, so restraint is warranted. Broadly, these platforms usually attach a therapeutic cargo to a binder that engages a receptor on brain endothelial cells, exploiting receptor-mediated transcytosis to cross from blood into nervous tissue. The engineering problem is delicate. Bind too tightly or multivalently, and the construct may become trapped in endothelial cells and routed toward lysosomal degradation. Bind too weakly, and almost nothing crosses.

Even successful barrier passage is only the first gate. The molecule must then distribute through brain tissue, encounter the intended neuron or glial cell and maintain useful pharmacology without creating excessive peripheral exposure.

Novartis neuroscience head Bob Baloh has said there probably will not be one universal solution to the blood-brain barrier. This purchase adds another tool to the box. For scientists considering Novartis, the message is not that neuroscience is shrinking after the TREM2 failure. It is that delivery, biodistribution, translational biomarkers and target selection will face much harsher scrutiny.

Staying in neurodegeneration, SK Biopharmaceuticals has licensed a preclinical Parkinson’s candidate from fellow South Korean company 1ST Biotherapeutics.

The drug, 1ST-104, inhibits both LRRK2 and c-Abl. SK is paying just $1.8 million upfront, another $1.8 million in a near-term payment and $2.2 million as an equity investment. The advertised $314.8 million deal value is therefore overwhelmingly biobucks. This is an inexpensive preclinical option, not a $315 million endorsement of the molecule.

LRRK2 is a kinase whose pathogenic variants are among the most common genetic causes of familial Parkinson’s disease. Increased LRRK2 activity can disrupt vesicle trafficking, lysosomal biology, mitochondrial function and the formation of primary cilia.

Primary cilia are small, nonmotile projections that serve as cellular antennae. They concentrate receptors and signaling machinery, allowing neurons and glia to interpret extracellular cues. LRRK2 inhibition has been associated with restoration of cilia in experimental systems, but whether that translates into preserved dopaminergic neurons is unresolved.

Denali has already tested LRRK2 inhibition clinically, although Biogen walked away after a phase 2 miss. The class has also produced reversible lung-cell changes in animals. 1ST Bio calls its molecule a type 2 inhibitor, meaning it is designed to recognize a particular inactive kinase conformation, potentially changing selectivity and safety. That is a medicinal-chemistry distinction, not yet a clinical advantage.

The second target, c-Abl, is a stress-responsive kinase implicated in oxidative injury, autophagy and alpha-synuclein handling. Dual inhibition could conceivably address two connected degeneration pathways. It could also double the opportunities for off-target or chronic safety problems. In Parkinson’s, where treatment may continue for years, an exquisitely clean kinase profile matters.

SK already markets the epilepsy drug Xcopri and is building a broader CNS pipeline. But notice the contrast with its recent $350 million upfront license for Biohaven’s Kv7 activator, which almost immediately encountered an FDA partial hold. Here, SK has limited its near-term risk to a few million dollars. One expensive surprise appears to have sharpened the pencil.

Now to a different advanced modality, where Bristol Myers Squibb has abandoned the degrader-antibody conjugate it bought from Orum Therapeutics for $100 million.

ORM-6151, renamed BMS-986497, combined an antibody recognizing CD33 with a payload that degrades GSPT1. CD33 is frequently expressed by acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic-syndrome cells. GSPT1 is a housekeeping protein involved in translation termination and cell-cycle-associated biology; degrading enough of it can induce cell death.

The conjugate’s logic resembles an ADC, but instead of delivering a conventional DNA-damaging toxin or microtubule poison, it delivers a targeted protein degrader. In principle, degraders can act catalytically: one molecule recruits the cellular ubiquitin-proteasome machinery to destroy multiple target-protein molecules.

But the biological selectivity still begins with the antibody address. CD33 is not unique to malignant cells. It appears across normal myeloid populations and progenitors, creating risks of marrow suppression and infection. Pfizer’s Mylotarg, the original CD33 ADC approved in 2000, demonstrated both that the target can work and that its therapeutic window can be unforgiving.

BMS tested ORM-6151 alone and with azacitidine and venetoclax, then stopped after reviewing phase 1 data. It has not said whether the problem was toxicity, insufficient activity, poor exposure or some unattractive combination of all three.

That ambiguity does not help the modality. AbbVie previously terminated a CD33-directed conjugate carrying a BET degrader. Two failures do not invalidate every degrader-antibody conjugate, but they raise the possibility that the degrader payload does not magically escape the familiar ADC constraints of antigen distribution, internalization, linker behavior and intracellular release.

Orum received its $100 million sale price but loses up to $80 million in development milestones. The asset was preserved by the transaction. The scientific thesis was not.

Roche, meanwhile, is taking a far broader approach to bispecific discovery. It will pay Dualitas Therapeutics $36.5 million upfront to functionally screen more than 300,000 target combinations in immunology and inflammation. Milestones could bring the package to $1 billion, with royalties on resulting products.

This is not simply a computational exercise that pairs every fashionable receptor with every other fashionable receptor. Dualitas’ DualScreen platform tests combinations of cell-surface targets for functional effects, searching for pairings that may not have been obvious from known pathway diagrams.

That is attractive because immune diseases are network disorders. Blocking two cytokine or receptor pathways in one molecule might cover biologically distinct patient subsets, produce synergy in one cell population or concentrate activity only where both targets coexist.

The danger is that a large screen produces many statistically exciting combinations without explaining why they work or whether the relevant target geometry exists in human tissue. Bispecific activity depends not just on target names but on epitope, receptor density, valency, tissue distribution and the spatial arrangement of the cells involved.

Dualitas’ internal lead, DTX-102, engages CD28 in rheumatoid arthritis. CD28 supplies potent costimulation to T cells, so this biology deserves particular care. The infamous TGN1412 trial showed how systemic CD28 superagonism could trigger catastrophic cytokine release in healthy volunteers. A modern bispecific may use its other arm to localize or condition CD28 activity, which could be very clever. It must be demonstrated, not assumed.

For platform scientists, this is one of today’s more interesting job-market signals. Dualitas launched only last year with a $65 million Series A, and Roche’s upfront now helps finance its own clinical programs. High-throughput functional cell assays, receptor biology, primary immune-cell phenotyping and data analysis should all be central—not merely antibody production.

Electra Therapeutics has now completed the IPO we discussed as a proposal earlier this week. The final raise was even larger: $350 million, with another $52.5 million possible if underwriters exercise their option.

Electra sold 23.3 million shares at $15 and begins trading as ETRA. Add the company’s existing $97.7 million, and this becomes one of the year’s better-financed inflammation biotechs despite having only 46 full-time employees.

Most of the money—$220 million—is earmarked for ipsoprubart in secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, or sHLH. This is the overwhelming hyperinflammatory syndrome related to the fatal IEC-HS events that recently stopped autoimmune CAR-T studies. Macrophages, lymphocytes and inflammatory cytokines enter a positive-feedback loop, producing fevers, cytopenias, liver injury, coagulopathy and multiorgan failure.

Ipsoprubart targets signal regulatory proteins, or SIRPs, expressed on selected immune-cell populations and is intended to deplete pathological immune cells selectively. In an early malignancy-associated HLH study, all 12 frontline patients were alive at eight weeks.

That is encouraging and tiny. One hundred percent survival sounds definitive until the denominator walks into the room wearing a name tag that says twelve.

The upsized IPO reflects strong investor appetite for a severe disease, an identifiable registration path and a therapy that might control pathological immune cells without permanently engineering them. Electra also says it plans to hire. Expect that growth to favor global trial operations, immune biomarker work, safety and eventual commercial preparation.

Now, for the most entertainingly audacious claim of the day: Goa Therapeutics wants to create “Narcan for alcohol.”

Goa launched with $15.5 million and has already filed for an IPO that could add as much as $75 million. Its engineered protein therapy, GOA26, is intended to accelerate alcohol metabolism. The initial clinical use would be emergency departments, where intoxication can complicate anesthesia, trauma assessment and urgent surgery.

The company says an earlier protein construct cleared alcohol from rats’ blood within five minutes after an exposure likened to a 200-pound person consuming 13 vodka shots. That is a CEO’s description, not yet a peer-reviewed human result.

The chemistry needs careful attention. Alcohol dehydrogenase converts ethanol into acetaldehyde, which is itself toxic and contributes to flushing, nausea, cardiovascular stress and tissue injury. Aldehyde dehydrogenase then converts acetaldehyde into acetate. Any useful therapy must coordinate both steps or otherwise prevent acetaldehyde from accumulating. Rapidly turning ethanol into a more reactive toxin would be less “greatest therapeutic of all time” and more biochemical practical joke.

There is also an enormous behavioral hazard. Narcan reverses opioid overdose without making opioid use immediately safe. A widely available alcohol-clearing drug could save lives, but it could also encourage people to drink more, drive sooner or mistake lower blood ethanol for restored judgment and motor function. The brain’s behavioral recovery may lag behind the blood measurement.

The emergency-surgery indication is credible enough to test. The dream of putting the product in every bar, vending machine and glove box is several regulatory universes away.

Lisata Therapeutics has found a new escape route after its Kuva Labs merger collapsed and triggered 72% layoffs. It will combine with Marea Therapeutics in a reverse-merger-like transaction backed by $225 million. Lisata’s existing shareholders receive only about 2.4% of the resulting company; Marea investors and new financiers own essentially everything else. Marea’s severe-hypertriglyceridemia and acromegaly programs become the focus. Lisata’s public shell survives. Its original tumor-penetration identity largely does not.

And Sling Therapeutics raised a $123 million Series C to take oral IGF-1-receptor inhibitor linsitinib through phase 3 in thyroid eye disease. In an earlier 90-patient study, 52% of high-dose patients met the response criterion for reduced eye bulging.

IGF-1 receptor signaling interacts with TSH-receptor biology in orbital fibroblasts, promoting inflammatory expansion, fat remodeling and production of water-binding glycosaminoglycans such as hyaluronan. Inside the rigid orbit, that swollen matrix pushes the eye forward.

Tepezza and Lumvoa validate IGF-1R with infused antibodies. Sling is betting that an oral kinase inhibitor can offer comparable efficacy with easier dosing. Khartis, which we discussed last month, is pursuing the same oral concept but remains behind Sling clinically. For chemists and translational scientists, this is a repurposed oncology molecule becoming a late-stage endocrine-and-ophthalmology product—exactly the kind of asset where exposure, tissue penetration and chronic safety matter more than target novelty.

Today’s stories repeatedly separate platform promise from clinical proof. TREM2 activation had a persuasive microglial narrative and failed ALS. A degrader-antibody conjugate had a sophisticated payload and died after phase 1. A massive bispecific screen may uncover biology nobody thought to pair, but every hit still needs mechanistic validation.

The field is willing to finance ambition. It is becoming far less willing to finance ambiguity.
