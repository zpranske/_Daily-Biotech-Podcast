The company is nevertheless proceeding into phase 3 because safety was clean and the result was tantalizingly close—but this is a genuine replication gamble, not a disguised win. Let’s start with Acadia, because the phrase “near miss” is doing a great deal of emotional and financial work today.

Acadia tested remlifanserin, also called ACP-204, in people with psychosis associated with Alzheimer’s disease. At six weeks, the 60-milligram dose reduced hallucination-and-delusion scores by 12.6 points. Placebo improved by 10.4 points.

That is only a 2.2-point separation, corresponding to an effect size of 0.26. The primary endpoint missed, with a p-value of 0.0603.

Yes, that is close to the conventional significance threshold of 0.05. No, 0.0603 does not mean the drug almost certainly works and a bureaucratic decimal point spoiled the party. It means that, under the trial’s statistical assumptions, the observed separation was not strong enough to exclude chance at the prespecified level.

Analysts had expected an effect size around 0.35 to 0.4. Acadia delivered less.

Remlifanserin is an inverse agonist at the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor. An inverse agonist does more than prevent serotonin from activating the receptor; it can reduce the receptor’s baseline, ligand-independent signaling as well. Acadia’s approved drug Nuplazid, or pimavanserin, uses the same broad mechanism and is approved for hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson’s disease psychosis.

This is an attractive route because conventional antipsychotics often block dopamine D2 receptors. In older patients with neurodegenerative disease, D2 blockade can worsen movement, sedation, swallowing, falls and cognition. Remlifanserin is intended to suppress psychotic symptoms without extracting that motor price.

The safety data supported that proposition. Adverse events and discontinuations were similar to placebo. There was no apparent worsening of cognition or motor function, no deaths on drug and, importantly, no signal for QT prolongation—the cardiac electrical liability associated with Nuplazid.

A key secondary measure of symptom severity did nominally favor the drug, with a 1.3-point reduction versus 0.9 on placebo. But “nominally significant” generally means the result has not been fully protected against the multiple-comparison problem. Test enough secondary measures and one may cross 0.05 by chance.

Acadia is proceeding into phase 3. That is defensible given the unmet need, clean safety and near-threshold primary result. It is not de-risked. Alzheimer’s psychosis trials have large placebo responses, fluctuating symptoms and heavy caregiver influence on reporting. A slightly larger or better-enriched study could turn p equals 0.0603 into a win. It could just as easily reproduce an effect too small to matter.

The insider read is simple: phase 3 is absolutely worth pursuing if Acadia can afford the gamble. Investors should not price it as though phase 2 succeeded.

The autoimmune CAR-T story is more encouraging, especially after the three fatal inflammatory events that recently halted Novartis’ broad autoimmune program.

Kyverna reported one-year data for mivocabtagene autoleucel, or miv-cel, in stiff person syndrome. This is an autologous CD19-directed CAR-T: collect the patient’s T cells, engineer them to recognize CD19, administer lymphodepleting chemotherapy and reinfuse the cells to eliminate B cells deeply.

Stiff person syndrome is a rare neurological autoimmune disease marked by progressive rigidity and painful muscle spasms. Many patients have antibodies against GAD65, an enzyme involved in producing the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, while others have antibodies against amphiphysin or glycine-receptor-associated biology. The antibodies may not explain every feature directly, but they identify a B-cell-driven attack on inhibitory neurotransmission.

In Kyverna’s 26-patient registrational study, timed 25-foot walking improved by a median of 49% at 12 months compared with baseline. At week 16, the improvement had been 46%, so the benefit did not simply peak and evaporate. Eight of 12 patients who had needed a walking aid before treatment remained able to walk without one at one year. Ninety-two percent remained free of chronic immunotherapy.

Those are functionally legible outcomes. Needing less time to cross a room and no longer needing a cane are easier to interpret than a subtle change in a biomarker.

Safety is the especially important comparison. Kyverna reported no high-grade cytokine-release syndrome, no high-grade ICANS neurotoxicity and no IEC-HS, the HLH-like hyperinflammatory complication responsible for the recent Novartis deaths.

Still, this is 26 patients, not proof that miv-cel has uniquely solved autoimmune CAR-T safety. Different diseases, conditioning regimens, cell-manufacturing methods and CAR expansion kinetics can all change risk. Rare toxicities may also appear only after broader use.

But Kyverna now has a strategically valuable dataset: durable neurological benefit, deep treatment-free remission and no severe inflammatory signal so far. The company is adding the one-year results to its rolling FDA submission. In an autoimmune CAR-T field suddenly forced to explain why its risks are acceptable outside lethal cancer, that clean safety column may be as valuable as the efficacy.

Now to RNA financing. ADARx has completed the IPO we discussed earlier this month, and demand was much stronger than expected.

The company sold 26.3 million shares at $17, the top of its proposed range, raising $446.3 million in gross proceeds. Underwriters can add another $66.9 million, potentially bringing the offering above $513 million.

ADARx already began the year with roughly $427 million in cash. It could therefore emerge from this transaction with something approaching $900 million before expenses and operating burn. For a 100-person RNA company, that is an extraordinary balance sheet.

The money supports a broad siRNA pipeline: onvuzosiran against prekallikrein in phase 3 for hereditary angioedema; agazisiran against complement factor B across several kidney diseases, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and geographic atrophy; and ADX-626 against factor XI for stroke prevention.

What remains most interesting is extrahepatic delivery. ADX-077 is described as adipose-targeted RNAi for obesity, while ADX-199 is an Alzheimer’s program expected to enter the clinic next year.

ADARx still has not disclosed those targets or, in the Alzheimer’s program, the administration route and intended cell type. That matters enormously. Silencing a transcript after intrathecal dosing is a different platform claim from crossing the blood-brain barrier after systemic administration. Delivery to neurons is different from delivery to microglia or astrocytes.

The IPO validates investor appetite for ADARx, not those undisclosed biological claims. Still, this is one of the strongest current hiring signals for oligonucleotide chemistry, bioanalysis, translational biomarkers, clinical pharmacology and eventually commercial manufacturing. Public investors have financed multiple decisive experiments rather than one narrow runway extension.

In addiction neuroscience, Indivior has returned all GABA-B positive-allosteric-modulator assets to Addex Therapeutics ahead of Indivior’s merger with Supernus.

Positive allosteric modulators, or PAMs, bind a receptor at a site distinct from the neurotransmitter-binding site and increase the receptor’s response to its natural ligand. Here, the receptor is GABA-B, a metabotropic inhibitory receptor.

Unlike the ionotropic GABA-A receptor, which directly opens a chloride channel, GABA-B signals through G proteins. Presynaptically, it can reduce calcium entry and neurotransmitter release. Postsynaptically, it can promote potassium conductance and reduce excitability.

The orthosteric GABA-B agonist baclofen can reduce spasticity and sometimes alcohol craving, but sedation, dizziness and muscle weakness limit it. A PAM offers an appealing form of context dependence: amplify endogenous GABA only where and when it is released, rather than activating every accessible receptor continuously.

That is the theory. Addex’s partnered programs have not yet converted it into clinical success. Johnson & Johnson previously dropped an Addex PAM after poor phase 2 epilepsy data, and the original Indivior addiction program also failed to advance.

Addex now regains a substance-use-disorder candidate plus control of a broader GABA-B portfolio, including a chronic-cough program. Management calls that flexibility. Financially, it means Addex must find another partner or fund further development itself.

The timing suggests portfolio cleanup before Indivior combines with Supernus, not a newly discovered toxicity. Indivior also recently abandoned a sustained-release buprenorphine collaboration after its orexin-1 antagonist failed in opioid-use disorder. The merged company is narrowing its CNS portfolio around assets that can justify development capital now. Returned rights are valuable only if someone else wants them.

Novo’s new drug-delivery agreement with Sweden’s Nanexa is another attempt to make dosing frequency a competitive weapon.

The deal carries up to $1.33 billion in total value, although the upfront payment is undisclosed. Novo can apply Nanexa’s PharmaShell technology to as many as five peptide programs in obesity, diabetes and other cardiometabolic diseases.

PharmaShell places an inorganic coating around drug particles. After injection, those coated particles form a local depot. As the coating dissolves, active drug enters circulation over an extended period. Novo is targeting monthly and quarterly dosing.

This is not just a half-life-extension chemistry trick attached directly to the peptide. It is controlled release from a physical reservoir.

The challenge is avoiding an initial burst, maintaining predictable release and preventing local inflammation or variable depot dissolution. Peptides must also remain chemically and structurally stable while sitting under the skin for weeks or months.

And long duration cuts both ways. A once-monthly GLP-1 drug is convenient when it is well tolerated. If it causes severe nausea, vomiting or excessive appetite suppression, the patient cannot un-inject it. Novo’s own research chief emphasized that tolerability must be unusually attractive for monthly dosing.

The billion-dollar headline is mostly milestones, but the competitive pressure is real. Lilly licensed Camurus’ long-acting technology for up to $870 million, Pfizer has a monthly GLP-1 from its $10 billion Metsera acquisition, and Novo plans several first-in-human monthly candidates because it does not expect the first design to be the winner.

Now, two bispecific deals that illustrate where antibody engineering is heading.

Genentech is paying Earendil Labs $55 million upfront to discover oncology bispecifics, with milestones potentially exceeding $1.5 billion. Earendil will design the antibodies and take them through early clinical development before Genentech assumes global trials and commercialization.

Earendil describes an AI-enabled system that designs and optimizes antibody sequences at massive scale. The useful question is not how many sequences a model can generate. It is whether the resulting molecule has the correct affinity, geometry, stability, manufacturability, pharmacokinetics and tissue behavior. Bispecifics are three-dimensional machines, not two target names connected by an “X.”

Genentech’s agreement arrives one week after Roche paid Dualitas $36.5 million to functionally screen more than 300,000 bispecific combinations in immunology. Roche is simultaneously shopping for better design and better target pairing.

Amberstone Biosciences and China’s Henlius are adding an environmental switch. Their $440 million, mostly milestone-based collaboration will use Amberstone’s T-Mate platform to create T-cell engagers that become more active in acidic tumor conditions and less active at normal physiological pH.

Solid tumors often acidify their surroundings through glycolytic metabolism, poor perfusion and lactate accumulation. A pH-sensitive binder could theoretically form productive immune synapses in tumors while limiting systemic T-cell activation and cytokine-release syndrome.

But tumor acidity is heterogeneous, and inflamed or ischemic healthy tissues can also become acidic. The switch must behave across a fairly narrow physiological range; it cannot be a chemistry-class demonstration that works only in an idealized buffer. Amberstone’s first internal molecule enters human testing next year. That is when the clever pH curve meets actual tissue.

Finally, Merck’s $3 billion EyeBio acquisition has produced a pivotal win with an asterisk.

Remigromig, a trispecific agonist designed to mimic the natural Wnt-pathway ligand norrin, was noninferior to Lucentis on visual acuity at 52 weeks in diabetic macular edema. Norrin-Wnt signaling helps maintain retinal vascular integrity, so the drug aims to repair the blood-retinal barrier through a non-VEGF mechanism.

That is scientifically interesting because 30% to 40% of patients respond inadequately or lose response to VEGF inhibitors. Unfortunately, remigromig had higher rates of proliferative diabetic retinopathy, vitreous hemorrhage and discontinuation due to adverse events.

It also proved only noninferior to Lucentis, an older competitor increasingly overshadowed by Eylea and Vabysmo. Matching the weakest established comparator while producing more ocular complications is not yet a compelling commercial profile.

Merck needs the second pivotal trial, full safety analyses and anatomical imaging to clarify whether Wnt agonism meaningfully stabilizes retinal vessels or introduces a different form of vascular risk. A novel pathway has crossed the efficacy threshold. Whether it has crossed the usefulness threshold remains unsettled.

Across today’s stories, context-sensitive pharmacology keeps reappearing. Addex wants a GABA-B modulator that amplifies inhibition only when endogenous GABA is present. Amberstone wants T cells activated preferentially in acidic tumors. Nanexa wants peptides released slowly enough to improve convenience without trapping patients inside weeks of adverse effects. Kyverna wants B-cell depletion deep enough to reset neurological autoimmunity without tipping into lethal inflammation.

That is the current engineering frontier: not merely making a drug potent, but deciding where, when and for how long that potency is allowed to exist.
