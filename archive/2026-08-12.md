Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for August 12, 2026.

The TL;DR: Jazz Pharmaceuticals is paying $820 million upfront for Actio Biosciences and an early clinical drug targeting KCNT1, a potassium channel responsible for a devastating developmental epilepsy with dozens or even hundreds of seizures per day. It is a substantial vote of confidence in genetically defined ion-channel medicine, although “meaningful seizure reductions” is still frustratingly vague. Epicrispr raised $90 million for an epigenetic editor intended to silence toxic DUX4 expression in facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy, while Silence Therapeutics reported a strong phase 2 result for an siRNA that indirectly restricts iron availability in polycythemia vera. Elsewhere, Sionna lost 90% of its value after its cystic-fibrosis add-on barely changed sweat chloride, AbCellera produced unusually strong hot-flash data with an NK3-receptor antibody, and transcriptomics company Arpeggio shut down after it could not separate efficacy from an off-target liability. The recurring theme is mechanistic resolution: precise targets and enormous datasets help, but they do not excuse ambiguous human pharmacology.

Let’s begin with Jazz and Actio, because this is today’s most important neuroscience transaction.

Jazz Pharmaceuticals will acquire Actio Biosciences for $820 million upfront, with another $500 million tied to approval and sales milestones. The main asset is ABS-1230, an early clinical small molecule for KCNT1-positive epilepsy.

KCNT1 encodes a sodium-activated potassium channel, often called Slack. At first glance, a potassium channel associated with epilepsy can sound counterintuitive. Potassium currents usually help neurons repolarize and restrain firing, so why would excessive channel activity produce seizures?

Neural circuits are less considerate than a one-cell electrophysiology diagram. Many pathogenic KCNT1 variants are gain-of-function mutations, but their effect depends on which neurons express the channel, when during development it is active and how altered repolarization changes firing patterns. Excess potassium conductance in an inhibitory interneuron, for example, can disrupt its ability to sustain normal firing and disinhibit the broader circuit. Channel kinetics can also reshape bursting and action-potential timing rather than simply turning excitability up or down.

The clinical phenotype is brutal. Jazz estimates that roughly 2,500 people in the U.S. have KCNT1-positive developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. For about 80%, seizures begin during infancy. Many experience dozens to hundreds of drug-resistant seizures each day, fail to acquire speech or walking, and may not survive into adulthood.

Actio says ABS-1230 produced “meaningful seizure reductions” in an initial proof-of-concept study in children. That phrase helped support an $820 million upfront payment, but we still need the actual denominator, median reduction, responder distribution and duration. In ultra-high-frequency epilepsy, a percentage reduction can look dramatic while leaving a child with an enormous residual burden. Developmental outcomes matter too. Suppressing seizures after years of abnormal activity may improve attention, sleep and learning capacity, but it may not reverse every consequence of disrupted circuit formation.

The strategic fit is unusually clean. Jazz entered epilepsy through its $7.2 billion acquisition of GW Pharmaceuticals and now sells Epidiolex, which generated $292 million last quarter. It also licensed a Kv7.2 and Kv7.3 potassium-channel activator from Saniona last year. Jazz is assembling a channel-and-circuit franchise around rare epilepsies rather than treating Epidiolex as a one-off cannabinoid product.

Actio’s other assets will be spun into a new private company, including a TRPV4 inhibitor for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 2C. Jazz gets a minority stake, but it is paying primarily for KCNT1. That focus tells you the molecule has already separated itself internally from the broader platform.

Next, Epicrispr Biotechnologies raised a $90 million Series C for a very different form of genetic control: epigenetic repression without cutting the DNA sequence.

Its lead program, EPI-321, is in phase 1/2 for facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy, or FSHD. The disease typically weakens muscles of the face, shoulder blades and upper arms before progressing more broadly. Its genetics are wonderfully strange.

Most FSHD involves contraction of a repetitive region called D4Z4 on chromosome 4. Normally, that region is packaged into repressive chromatin. When the repeat array contracts in a permissive genetic background, chromatin repression becomes unstable and muscle cells aberrantly express DUX4, a transcription factor that should largely remain silent in adult skeletal muscle.

DUX4 then activates embryonic and germline-associated gene programs, triggers cellular stress and damages muscle. The therapeutic objective is therefore not to repair a missing structural protein. It is to put an inappropriately active gene back to sleep.

EPI-321 uses a viral vector to deliver genetic instructions for an epigenetic editor that represses DUX4 expression. Unlike conventional nuclease editing, the system is not intended to create a double-strand DNA break. It targets gene regulation—conceptually, recruiting a durable “do not transcribe” state to the problematic locus.

That may reduce risks associated with DNA cutting, but it creates different questions. How many muscle nuclei receive the vector? How durable is repression in long-lived, multinucleated muscle fibers? Does the editor affect other loci? And can one intravenous dose reach enough of the human musculature without unacceptable vector exposure?

Epicrispr reported that three patients gained between 0.5 and 1.3 pounds of lean muscle mass six months after treatment. Encouraging, yes. Persuasive, not yet. Three patients are far too few to distinguish treatment from measurement variability, changes in activity or natural fluctuation. Lean mass is also not the same as strength or function. We need timed motor tests, upper-limb performance and patient-level trajectories.

Competition is formidable. Novartis paid $12 billion for Avidity Biosciences and its phase 3 antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate delpacibart braxlosiran, which also suppresses DUX4 biology but uses an oligonucleotide delivery architecture. Sarepta has an siRNA in phase 1/2. FSHD is becoming a revealing comparison among transient RNA suppression, antibody-guided oligonucleotide delivery and potentially durable epigenetic silencing.

Speaking of RNA suppression, Silence Therapeutics has produced an impressively clean phase 2 result in polycythemia vera.

Its siRNA, divesiran, targets TMPRSS6 messenger RNA in hepatocytes. TMPRSS6 encodes matriptase-2, a negative regulator of hepcidin. Reduce TMPRSS6, hepcidin rises. Increased hepcidin then promotes degradation of ferroportin, the iron-export channel, reducing iron release into blood and limiting how much iron reaches the bone marrow.

That matters because polycythemia vera is a myeloproliferative blood cancer in which the marrow makes excessive red blood cells. Standard management often includes repeated phlebotomy—literally removing blood—to keep hematocrit below 45%. Divesiran tries to control erythrocyte production upstream by rationing iron.

Between weeks 18 and 36, 88% of divesiran-treated patients avoided phlebotomy while maintaining hematocrit below 45%, versus 19% on placebo. The response was 93.8% with dosing every six weeks and 81.3% every 12 weeks. Patients averaged 0.2 phlebotomies on divesiran versus 2.1 on placebo.

The commercial contest is with rusfertide, Takeda and Protagonist’s hepcidin-mimetic peptide. Rusfertide achieved a 76.9% phase 3 response rate and could be approved this quarter, giving it a substantial head start. But rusfertide is injected weekly. Silence plans to take the every-12-week divesiran regimen into phase 3 next year.

Cross-trial comparisons are treacherous because placebo rates and trial designs differ. Still, quarterly dosing versus weekly dosing is a real product distinction, not PowerPoint decoration. The watch item is whether prolonged iron restriction causes clinically important deficiency, fatigue or other consequences once larger numbers of patients are treated.

Now for a sharp reversal in cystic fibrosis. Sionna Therapeutics lost 90% of its market value after SION-719 failed as an add-on to Vertex’s Trikafta.

The common F508del mutation deletes a phenylalanine from CFTR and destabilizes the protein’s folding, especially within nucleotide-binding domain one, or NBD1. Misfolded CFTR is retained and degraded before enough channel reaches the epithelial surface. Trikafta combines correctors that improve folding and trafficking with a potentiator that helps channels open.

Sionna hoped that directly stabilizing NBD1 would restore additional CFTR function beyond Trikafta. In 15 patients homozygous for F508del, however, SION-719 produced a placebo-adjusted sweat-chloride reduction of only 1 millimole per liter.

Sionna had targeted 10. Independent experts were looking for at least 5 to 7. One is noise-level disappointment.

Sweat chloride is a direct pharmacodynamic readout of CFTR function, so this was not a trial where a subjective symptom scale obscured molecular activity. The drug simply did not add enough function on top of an already powerful standard of care.

Sionna is blaming some variability and differences in Trikafta exposure, but it has already terminated this add-on program and says it will preserve capital. Its separate combinations remain alive, including NBD1 stabilizer SION-451 paired with other correctors. Yet investors have learned something uncomfortable: NBD1 stabilization may be conceptually elegant without providing incremental value once Trikafta has rescued much of the recoverable biology. Vertex shares rose 6%. Its cystic-fibrosis moat just got wider.

AbCellera had a considerably better day. Its NK3-receptor antibody ABCL635 reduced moderate or severe menopausal hot flashes by 8.8 episodes per day after four weeks, compared with 3.5 on placebo. The placebo-adjusted reduction was 5.3 episodes per day.

That compares favorably, across separate trials, with reductions around 2.1 to 3.3 for the approved oral NK3-receptor antagonists Veozah and Lynkuet. Cross-trial comparisons can flatter a new drug, but the magnitude here is difficult to ignore.

The biology sits in hypothalamic thermoregulation. Menopause reduces estrogen feedback onto so-called KNDy neurons, named for kisspeptin, neurokinin B and dynorphin. Their dysregulated activity narrows the body’s thermoneutral zone, so small temperature changes trigger vasodilation, sweating and the familiar hot flash. Blocking neurokinin-B signaling through NK3 receptors can stabilize that circuit.

ABCL635 also reduced hot-flash severity and produced no liver-enzyme or bilirubin elevations in this 92-patient study. That is important because Veozah carries a boxed warning for liver injury. An antibody may permit stronger, sustained target engagement without the same small-molecule hepatic exposure, although phase 2 is too early to declare the program “highly de-risked,” as AbCellera’s CEO did. Highly promising would be safer language.

Finally, Arpeggio Biosciences has shut down after eight years, and this one deserves attention from anyone working in sequencing or high-dimensional phenotyping.

Arpeggio built what it described as the world’s largest nascent-RNA database. It ran more than 50 million experiments, screened over 150,000 compounds in dozens of reporter lines, sequenced more than 300,000 transcriptomes and synthesized over 2,000 proprietary molecules.

Its lead program was an Nrf2 degrader for lung cancer. Nrf2 controls antioxidant, detoxification and stress-response programs. In some tumors, persistent Nrf2 activity helps malignant cells tolerate oxidative stress and resist therapy, making degradation attractive.

But Arpeggio could not separate the molecule’s efficacy from an off-target liability. The program unraveled, and the company disbanded.

That is a sobering counterpoint to the current AI-and-omics enthusiasm. Transcriptomics can reveal that a compound changes thousands of genes. It can cluster phenotypes, identify response signatures and suggest mechanism. It cannot automatically prove which molecular interaction caused the desirable effect—or whether toxicity is inseparable from efficacy. Three hundred thousand transcriptomes still meet the same medicinal-chemistry question: can you make a molecule with the right activity and without the wrong one?

For the job market, there is expansion alongside the wreckage. Amgen is opening a research center in Hyderabad’s Genome Valley in 2027, bringing together discovery chemistry, drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics, precision medicine, data and technology. That follows a $600 million research investment in Thousand Oaks and a $125 million South San Francisco expansion. Global pharma research is not disappearing, but it is increasingly distributed across integrated hubs where experimental scientists are expected to work fluently with computational teams.

Meanwhile, Aura Biosciences is cutting 20% of its workforce to concentrate on ocular oncology, and aTyr Pharma is cutting 60% after its pulmonary-sarcoidosis drug failed phase 3. The hiring signal is therefore bifurcated. Well-capitalized programs with credible human data are building. Companies trying to finance a second interpretation of disappointing data are shrinking to the minimum team required for one more attempt.

Today’s strongest programs—KCNT1 inhibition, DUX4 repression, TMPRSS6 silencing and NK3-receptor blockade—are all unusually specific. But Sionna and Arpeggio supply the necessary warning. Precision tells you exactly what experiment you are running. It does not guarantee that the experiment will produce a useful medicine.

And that’s the roundup for today.
