Let’s begin with Bristol Myers Squibb’s zola-cel, because the new patient death arrives during an already sensitive reassessment of autoimmune CAR-T safety.

Zola-cel is an autologous CD19-directed CAR-T therapy. A patient’s own T cells are collected, genetically equipped with a receptor recognizing CD19 and reinfused after lymphodepleting chemotherapy. Those engineered T cells then eliminate B cells broadly.

In multiple sclerosis, B cells do more than produce antibodies. They present antigen, organize immune interactions and release cytokines that can sustain inflammation. Anti-CD20 antibodies such as Ocrevus already validate B-cell depletion in MS, but CAR-T aims for a deeper reset, potentially clearing B-cell populations from tissues that ordinary antibodies reach less completely.

BMS paused enrollment in autoimmune zola-cel trials in August after transient inflammatory events. The genuinely new information is that one MS patient subsequently died.

This patient developed late-onset motor symptoms that were not classified as ICANS—the familiar immune-effector-cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome—and did not resolve with intermittent steroids or intravenous immunoglobulin. The patient received high-dose cyclophosphamide and died from septic pneumonia the following day, 44 days after CAR-T infusion.

BMS emphasizes that this was distinct from the signal prompting the earlier pause. Fair enough. Distinct does not mean irrelevant.

CAR-T recipients face several overlapping risks. Lymphodepleting chemotherapy weakens host immunity before treatment. Deep B-cell depletion can reduce antibody-mediated protection. Steroids and cyclophosphamide used to control inflammatory or neurological complications add further immunosuppression. A fatal infection may therefore emerge from the entire treatment-and-rescue sequence even if no single component receives a neat causal label.

Among 66 patients in the phase 1 MS trial, 42.4% experienced cytokine-release syndrome, mostly grade 1, and one patient experienced grade 4 ICANS. Those numbers are not catastrophic by oncology standards. MS is not refractory leukemia, however. Many patients have effective disease-modifying therapies available, and progressive disease unfolds over years. Acceptable risk changes radically with clinical context.

BMS reported that all 60 patients receiving the second dose level remained off conventional disease-modifying therapy at the data cutoff, while 92% of progressive-MS patients were stable or improved on disability measures. Encouraging, certainly. But “stable or improved” in an uncontrolled early study is a permissive endpoint, particularly over a relatively short interval in a disease with variable trajectories.

The field now needs duration, a credible comparator and a clearer account of the unresolved neurological syndrome. After the recent Novartis autoimmune CAR-T deaths from an HLH-like inflammatory condition, companies cannot treat each serious event as an isolated exception. Regulators will ask whether cell expansion, conditioning, infection risk and rescue immunosuppression collectively produce a tolerable package.

The efficacy may be extraordinary. The safety burden is becoming equally extraordinary.

Now to a much more controllable modality: Roche and Ionis’ antisense oligonucleotide sefaxersen in IgA nephropathy.

We discussed the phase 3 win last month, when Roche disclosed only that proteinuria had improved. The company has now supplied the number: a 43.1% reduction compared with placebo at week 37.

IgA nephropathy begins with unusually glycosylated IgA1. Many patients produce IgA1 molecules whose hinge-region O-glycans are deficient in galactose. Autoantibodies recognize those abnormal glycoforms, creating immune complexes that accumulate in the glomerular mesangium. Complement activation then amplifies inflammation and damages the kidney’s filtration barrier.

Protein consequently leaks into urine. Proteinuria is therefore both a marker of glomerular injury and a predictor of future kidney decline.

Sefaxersen is a once-monthly subcutaneous antisense drug that reduces hepatic production of complement factor B. Factor B is required to form the alternative-pathway C3 convertase, an enzymatic complex that generates more activated C3 and creates a powerful amplification loop.

So the drug does not remove the initiating IgA immune complexes. It turns down the complement amplifier that makes their presence more destructive.

A 43% placebo-adjusted proteinuria reduction is clinically persuasive and potentially adequate for an accelerated-approval argument. The trial remains blinded through two years to determine whether estimated glomerular filtration rate is actually preserved. That longer result is essential. Less protein in urine predicts benefit; sustained kidney function demonstrates it.

The direct competitor is Novartis’ Fabhalta, an oral factor B inhibitor taken twice daily. This creates an interesting modality contest. Sefaxersen offers infrequent dosing and prolonged suppression, but an ASO effect cannot be quickly withdrawn if infection risk or another safety problem appears.

Lycia’s lead drug, LCA-0061, is a catalytic lysosomal-targeting chimera, or cataLYTAC, designed to remove IgE. The resulting complex is pulled into the cell and routed to lysosomes for degradation.

An ordinary neutralizing antibody mostly holds its target inactive. A LYTAC attempts to make the target disappear.

In food allergy, IgE binds high-affinity Fc-epsilon receptors on mast cells and basophils. When allergen crosslinks adjacent IgE-receptor complexes, those cells release histamine, proteases and lipid mediators, potentially causing hives, airway constriction or anaphylaxis.
