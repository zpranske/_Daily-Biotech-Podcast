Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for September 11, 2026.

The TL;DR: Encoded Therapeutics raised a massive $275 million Series F to move its one-time Dravet-syndrome gene therapy into pivotal development and build commercial manufacturing. ETX101 uses AAV9 and a cell-selective regulatory strategy to increase SCN1A expression in inhibitory neurons, addressing the interneuron dysfunction that destabilizes Dravet circuits. Earlier data showed a 78% seizure reduction, but durability, developmental benefit and gene-therapy safety remain decisive. In a less welcome epilepsy update, the FDA partially halted enrollment for Biohaven’s Kv7 activator opakalim over incompletely characterized rodent metabolites, just days after SK Biopharmaceuticals agreed to pay $350 million upfront for it. Elsewhere, a TLR7-and-8 inhibitor succeeded in phase 2 lupus, Lilly backed a menin-inhibitor spinout intended to restore pancreatic beta-cell capacity, and investors raised billions even as Massachusetts seed rounds shrank nearly 40%. Capital is abundant. Access to it is strikingly unequal.

Encoded Therapeutics is the obvious place to begin, because a $275 million financing for a neurological gene therapy is not a routine venture round—especially in a market that has become wary of AAV safety, manufacturing expense and the gulf between biomarker correction and functional improvement.

Encoded’s Series F was co-led by GV, formerly Google Ventures, and an undisclosed healthcare fund, with participation from ARCH, Janus Henderson, RTW and SoftBank’s Vision Fund. The money will support pivotal development of ETX101 in infants and young children with SCN1A-positive Dravet syndrome, while also expanding Encoded’s internal manufacturing capacity.

Dravet syndrome is most commonly caused by loss-of-function variants in one copy of SCN1A. That gene encodes Nav1.1, a voltage-gated sodium channel especially important for action-potential firing in inhibitory interneurons, including many parvalbumin- and somatostatin-expressing cells.

This is a useful circuit-level paradox. Losing a sodium channel sounds as though it should reduce neuronal activity. It does—but disproportionately in inhibitory neurons. If interneurons cannot sustain firing, their restraint on excitatory networks weakens. The result is disinhibition, seizures and developmental epileptic encephalopathy.

ETX101 uses an AAV9 vector to deliver a regulatory construct intended to increase expression of the patient’s own SCN1A gene selectively in inhibitory neurons. It is not simply adding a conventional full-length SCN1A replacement cassette. SCN1A is a large gene, and AAV has a limited carrying capacity. Encoded instead uses a synthetic transcriptional regulator designed to boost endogenous gene expression in the cell type where more Nav1.1 should be most useful.

That cell selectivity matters. Sodium-channel expression is exquisitely context dependent. Raising Nav1.1 indiscriminately across the brain could have very different consequences from restoring it in deficient interneurons. The vector is the delivery vehicle, but the regulatory DNA—the logic specifying where the therapy turns on—is central to the product.

Encoded began a phase 2 study in May. Its earlier trial reportedly showed a 78% reduction in seizures, with durable control and promising neurodevelopmental signals. Those words justify excitement, but we need the denominator, baseline seizure burden, duration and patient-level distribution. A mean reduction can conceal one spectacular responder and several modest ones. And seizure suppression, although enormously important, is not the whole Dravet phenotype. Families care about language, motor development, attention, sleep, behavior and the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy.

Timing may be decisive. Treating infants could prevent years of recurrent seizures and abnormal circuit activity, but it also exposes very young children to a one-time, effectively irreversible intervention. Regulators will scrutinize vector dose, liver toxicity, immune responses, dorsal-root-ganglion findings and long-term surveillance. AAV9 can distribute broadly after systemic administration; “neuron-selective expression” does not mean “the vector only visits neurons.”

Encoded is competing with Stoke Therapeutics and Biogen’s zorevunersen, an antisense oligonucleotide that increases productive SCN1A RNA from the functional gene copy. Stoke’s program is repeat-dosed rather than one-time and has a phase 3 readout expected in the third quarter of 2027.

That creates a fascinating modality comparison. An ASO offers adjustability: stop dosing if the risk-benefit balance changes. ETX101 offers the possibility of durable treatment after one administration, but with less reversibility. If both work, the competition may turn on age, efficacy, developmental outcomes, administration burden and safety—not merely whose molecular mechanism makes the prettier seminar slide.

Encoded’s history also says something about biotech employment. The company cut 29% of its staff in February 2025 to preserve the initial clinical trial. Now it has $275 million and is scaling manufacturing. That does not undo the layoffs, but it shifts hiring demand toward pivotal-trial operations, analytical development, vector manufacturing, quality systems, regulatory work and long-term safety. A Series F, in biotech-speak, means discovery is no longer the expensive part. Proving that every commercial batch is comparable—and that the treatment remains safe for years—is.

Unfortunately, the other major epilepsy story is a safety-related interruption.

The FDA has placed a partial clinical hold on Biohaven’s opakalim, or BHV-7000, after the company identified metabolites in rodent studies that have not yet been characterized well enough to determine their relevance to humans.

A metabolite is what appears after enzymes chemically transform a drug. Sometimes the metabolites are inactive and harmless. Sometimes they retain pharmacology, accumulate in one species or create reactive intermediates capable of binding proteins and damaging tissue. Rodents can also generate metabolites that humans barely produce, which is why this may ultimately be a species-specific distraction rather than a human risk.

For now, the FDA is allowing already enrolled patients to continue treatment but is blocking new recruitment. One pivotal focal-epilepsy study has completed enrollment and should still report this year. Enrollment in the second is paused while Biohaven generates additional nonclinical data over the coming weeks.

The timing is awful. Only days ago, SK Biopharmaceuticals agreed to pay Biohaven $350 million upfront and another $50 million next year for worldwide rights. SK is also taking on obligations to Knopp Biosciences, the molecule’s original developer. Biohaven says all metabolite findings were disclosed before the agreement, and both parties still intend to close the transaction.

That disclosure is important. This does not appear to be SK discovering an unpleasant rodent surprise by reading Fierce Biotech over breakfast. But it does mean SK knowingly accepted a regulatory uncertainty immediately before a pivotal readout.

Opakalim activates neuronal Kv7.2-and-Kv7.3 potassium channels, strengthening the non-inactivating M-current that restrains repetitive firing. We discussed the deal two weeks ago, when the main risk looked like competition from Xenon’s already successful Kv7 drug. The new question is more basic: can Biohaven clear the toxicology issue without delaying the program or narrowing the usable dose?

Watch whether the concerning metabolite appears in human plasma at meaningful exposure. If it is rodent-specific, the hold may be brief. If humans produce it too, Biohaven may need more toxicology, tighter exposure limits or altered monitoring. For SK, the $350 million check has suddenly acquired a footnote in bold type.

Moving from neurological excitability to innate immune sensing, Beeline Medicines says its oral TLR7-and-TLR8 inhibitor afimetoran met the primary endpoint in phase 2 systemic lupus erythematosus.

After 48 weeks, all three doses produced significantly higher SRI-4 response rates than placebo, with a reported p-value below 0.001. Secondary endpoints were directionally consistent, and safety broadly resembled earlier studies.

That sounds excellent. It is also an extremely incomplete data release. We do not have the actual response rates, placebo difference, dose response or adverse-event table. A low p-value says the observed separation is difficult to explain by chance under the statistical model. It does not tell us whether the benefit was 8 percentage points or 30.

TLR7 and TLR8 are endosomal pattern-recognition receptors that detect single-stranded RNA. In infection, that helps immune cells recognize viral material. In lupus, nucleic acids released from damaged cells can become packaged in immune complexes, enter endosomes and repeatedly trigger these receptors. That promotes inflammatory cytokines, B-cell activation and the type-one-interferon program characteristic of many patients.

The genetic evidence is unusually strong: gain-of-function TLR7 variants can cause lupus. Blocking TLR7 and TLR8 therefore attacks an upstream nucleic-acid-sensing mechanism rather than neutralizing one downstream cytokine.

The safety tension follows directly from the biology. These receptors contribute to antiviral defense. Chronic inhibition may increase infection risk, and the correct dose must suppress pathological self-RNA sensing without creating broad innate immune deafness.

Beeline emerged from stealth only in April with $300 million, five Bristol Myers Squibb assets and executives associated with SpringWorks. It then added a $126 million Series A extension. This is an asset-centric company assembled with enough money to move quickly, and afimetoran now appears ready for pivotal studies in systemic and cutaneous lupus. Merck KGaA already has a competing TLR7-and-8 inhibitor in phase 3, so “best in class” will require actual effect sizes and tolerability—not p-value calligraphy.

The day’s most intriguing regenerative-biology story comes from Kura Oncology, which has created Caspian Therapeutics with $50 million to test menin inhibition in diabetes.

Menin is best known in oncology as a scaffold supporting leukemogenic transcriptional programs involving mutant NPM1 or KMT2A rearrangements. Kura already markets the menin inhibitor Komzifti for acute myeloid leukemia.

In pancreatic beta cells, however, menin also restrains proliferation and functional mass. Caspian’s KO-7246 is designed for chronic metabolic disease, with the aim of increasing beta-cell capacity and insulin production in type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Kura says the effect has been consistent across animal models and human islet systems.

This would be a profound shift from managing glucose to restoring part of the insulin-producing cell population. But stimulating proliferation in a chronically treated patient raises an obvious question: can you expand beta cells selectively without encouraging unwanted growth elsewhere?

Type 1 diabetes adds another complication. New beta cells remain vulnerable if autoimmune destruction continues. Menin inhibition may ultimately need to be combined with immune modulation, cell protection or both. Regenerate the target without stopping the attack, and the immune system may simply destroy the replacement.

Lilly participated in the financing alongside BVF and the T1D Fund. That is a meaningful strategic endorsement, but not a license or acquisition. Frazier Life Sciences added more than $1.1 billion to its long-running public fund, bringing that strategy to $2.8 billion. Both things are true.

Massachusetts companies raised $3.45 billion in venture funding during the first half of 2026, up 25%, and completed eight IPOs—the strongest first-half listing count since 2021. But the average seed round fell from $7.6 million to $4.6 million, a decline of almost 40%.

Money is accumulating in large funds while investment committees favor later-stage programs, clinical evidence and companies that can reach a value-changing milestone without another financing. Frazier’s public fund specifically targets small- and mid-cap listed biotechs and crossover rounds—the late private financings that often precede an IPO. Its successful holdings included Verona Pharma before Merck’s $10 billion acquisition and Alpine Immune Sciences before Vertex bought it for $4.9 billion.

For academic scientists entering industry, this explains why enormous financing headlines can coexist with a poor discovery-job market. A billion-dollar fund does not distribute itself evenly among fifty embryonic platform companies. It may concentrate into ten de-risked clinical positions. The missing capital is often the first $5 million needed to transform interesting university work into reproducible pharmacology, protected intellectual property and a development candidate.

Finally, Takeda R&D chief Andy Plump will retire next June after more than a decade at the company. His tenure illustrates how a research leader is judged across an entire portfolio, not by one fashionable modality.

Takeda recently won approvals for the orexin agonist Orzeyful in narcolepsy and the blood-cancer drug Mimrylo, while its TYK2 inhibitor zasocitinib has positive phase 3 psoriasis data. Those successes matter as the company prepares for biosimilar pressure on Entyvio.

But Takeda also exited cell therapy, wrote down its GammaDelta Therapeutics acquisition and withdrew Crohn’s cell therapy Alofisel after phase 3 failure. Plump’s successor inherits both sides: a productive late-stage pipeline and the need to replace revenue from aging blockbusters without buying another modality that looks strategically mandatory until it suddenly does not.

For scientists, leadership transitions at this level can reshape portfolio reviews well before the new executive reorganizes reporting lines. Programs with approvals or decisive phase 3 evidence are protected. Early platforms should expect fresh questions about differentiation, cost and time to human proof of concept.

Today’s stories ultimately describe one capital market with several radically different climates. Encoded can raise $275 million around a genetically precise epilepsy therapy with human seizure data. Beeline can assemble more than $400 million around ex-BMS assets and experienced operators. Frazier can add $1.1 billion for public and crossover investments.

Meanwhile, a seed-stage Massachusetts company receives $4.6 million on average and is expected to turn a platform hypothesis into something that looks de-risked before the next check arrives.

The money exists. It is gathering around programs that already have a clinical signal, a clear regulatory path or a buyer waiting nearby. For early scientists and founders, crossing the distance to that first credible human experiment remains the most dangerous part of the journey.
