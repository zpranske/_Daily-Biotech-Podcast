The thread today is portfolio triage: mechanisms survive only when companies can afford the next decisive experiment.

Let’s begin with Ultragenyx, because this is the concrete organizational consequence of the Angelman syndrome failure we discussed in September.

Ultragenyx is cutting another 20% of its workforce and expects to employ roughly 1,000 people afterward. It began the year with 1,371 employees, including 916 in R&D, and had already eliminated about 130 roles in February. The company now plans to reduce annual R&D spending to around $350 million in 2027—approximately half its 2025 level—and pursue profitability.

The new scientific information is unfortunately limited. Ultragenyx still has not released the phase 3 Angelman data. CEO Emil Kakkis said variability within the control group was too large across multiple endpoints to distinguish a treatment effect.

GTX-102, also called apazunersen, is now being wound down completely.

Remember the molecular setup. In neurons, the paternal copy of UBE3A is normally silenced by a long antisense transcript called UBE3A-ATS. Most neuronal UBE3A therefore comes from the maternal allele. In Angelman syndrome, that maternal contribution is absent or dysfunctional, while the paternal gene remains present but transcriptionally suppressed.

GTX-102 was an antisense oligonucleotide designed to inhibit UBE3A-ATS and reactivate paternal UBE3A. It was one of those mechanisms that looks almost offensively elegant on a whiteboard: remove the silencing RNA and restore the missing protein.

The phase 3 failure did not tell us whether the drug failed to reactivate UBE3A adequately, whether restoration came too late to repair developmentally altered circuits, or whether the clinical instruments were simply too noisy. Those possibilities are scientifically very different. Investors and budget committees, however, eventually collapse them into one operational conclusion: the trial did not establish benefit.

Ultragenyx is also terminating UX701, an AAV gene therapy for Wilson disease. Too few patients produced the desired biochemical response and successfully withdrew from standard therapy. Management decided that optimizing dose or delivery would require more money than the program now deserved.

The company’s remaining R&D budget will be weighted toward approved products, required monitoring and regulatory filings rather than broad early discovery. It expects to maintain around five development programs at a time, supported by measured preclinical spending and small external deals.

For job seekers, this is the uncomfortable distinction between a commercial-stage rare-disease company and a discovery-driven employer. Ultragenyx still has six marketed products. It is not disappearing. But neuroscience, gene therapy and exploratory translational teams are being resized around what can support profitability in 2027. The science did not merely lose a trial. It lost organizational permission to remain expensive.

Agios Pharmaceuticals is making a related move across town in Cambridge.

Agios intends to eliminate more than 100 R&D roles by year-end, reducing its R&D workforce by more than 35% from 2025 levels. This is not a cash emergency either: the company held $907 million at the end of September. It is concentrating around mitapivat and preparing for a possible sickle-cell-disease launch.

Mitapivat activates pyruvate kinase in red blood cells. Mature erythrocytes lack mitochondria, so they depend heavily on glycolysis to make ATP. Increasing pyruvate-kinase activity can improve cellular energy balance, stabilize the red-cell membrane and reduce hemolysis in selected anemias.

In sickle cell disease, the underlying problem begins with mutant hemoglobin polymerizing when deoxygenated, distorting cells and damaging their membranes. Better red-cell metabolism cannot remove the sickle mutation, but it might improve cell survival and reduce anemia.

The regulatory package is not clean. Last year’s phase 3 study missed one of two primary endpoints, and the FDA has delayed its decision until February 1 while reviewing additional safety data. Agios calls sickle cell its largest commercial opportunity and says it is launch-ready. The workforce cuts suggest management is unwilling to maintain yesterday’s R&D organization while waiting to learn whether tomorrow’s franchise is approved.

Taken together, Ultragenyx and Agios show why Boston’s job market can remain difficult even when companies have products and hundreds of millions in cash. Profitability targets can eliminate discovery roles long before the balance sheet looks distressed.

Now to Rezera, formerly NodThera, which is trying to raise approximately $181 million in an IPO.

The Boston biotech has only $3.9 million currently available and 21 full-time employees. Most IPO proceeds would fund a phase 3 trial of ruvonoflast in peripheral artery disease, with a readout planned for 2028.

Ruvonoflast inhibits NLRP3, an intracellular sensor that helps assemble the inflammasome. When activated by cellular stress signals—including disturbed ion balance, mitochondrial injury and crystalline material—NLRP3 recruits adaptor proteins and caspase-1. Caspase-1 then processes inflammatory cytokines including IL-1 beta and IL-18 and can trigger pyroptosis, a highly inflammatory form of cell death.

In atherosclerosis, cholesterol crystals, oxidized lipids and damaged cells can sustain this pathway inside macrophages. Peripheral artery disease is atherosclerosis that compromises blood flow to the limbs. Patients may develop pain while walking, nonhealing wounds, tissue death and elevated cardiovascular risk even when cholesterol, blood pressure and diabetes are treated aggressively.

Rezera previously combined ruvonoflast with semaglutide in obesity and showed a statistically significant reduction in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein compared with semaglutide alone. CRP is produced by the liver in response to inflammatory signaling and is a useful cardiovascular-risk marker.

But lowering CRP is not the same as helping a patient walk farther, avoiding amputation or preventing a heart attack. We recently saw Novo’s IL-6 antibody ziltivekimab suppress inflammatory biology without improving cardiovascular outcomes. NLRP3 is upstream of IL-6 and may behave differently, but the failure raises the evidentiary standard. Rezera’s phase 3 needs a clinically meaningful endpoint, not merely a cleaner biomarker panel.

The CNS angle is trabzanoflast, a second NLRP3 inhibitor optimized specifically for central nervous system entry. It has completed phase 1, and Rezera wants to test it in neuroinflammatory disease. Yet the current IPO proceeds are not assumed to fund that phase 2 trial. The company will invest there only if the offering grows or underwriters purchase additional shares.

That is a useful read on priorities. Rezera is publicly associated with brain-penetrant inflammasome biology, but investors are being asked to finance peripheral vascular disease first. For neuroinflammation scientists, trabzanoflast remains strategically interesting—microglial NLRP3 signaling is implicated across neurodegenerative conditions—but it is not yet the funded center of gravity.

Next, molecular glue degradation is producing one new bet and one abrupt reversal.

Evommune is paying China’s Hinova Pharmaceuticals $30 million upfront for ex-China rights to EVO570, an oral molecular glue degrader targeting VAV1. The upfront consists of $20 million in cash and $10 million in Evommune warrants. Another $1.1 billion is possible through milestones, so most of that headline remains biobucks.

VAV1 is expressed predominantly in hematopoietic cells and helps transmit signals downstream of immune receptors. It can function as a guanine-nucleotide exchange factor, activating small GTPases that control cytoskeletal remodeling, immune-synapse formation and transcriptional responses. It also serves scaffold-like signaling roles in T and B cells.

Ordinary small-molecule inhibition has struggled with selectivity. A molecular glue takes a different route. It promotes a new interaction between the target protein and an E3 ubiquitin-ligase complex. The target is ubiquitinated and delivered to the proteasome for destruction. Instead of occupying VAV1’s active site continuously, the drug attempts to remove the protein.

The commercial pitch is JAK-like efficacy with better selectivity because VAV1 is more restricted to immune cells than JAK enzymes. That is plausible, but not guaranteed. JAK inhibitors suppress signaling from many cytokine receptors and can therefore produce broad efficacy along with infections, thrombosis and other safety liabilities. VAV1 degradation may affect fewer tissues, yet it could still disrupt several immune-cell functions simultaneously.

Novartis is already ahead with MRT-6160, licensed from Monte Rosa for $150 million upfront and now in phase 2 for psoriatic arthritis and Sjögren’s disease. EVO570 enters phase 1 imminently and is expected to move into rheumatoid arthritis in 2027. Evommune has therefore bought a differentiated scaffold in a mechanism that already has early human validation—but also an established competitor.

The cautionary comparison is Sanofi’s STAT6 degrader from Nurix. Sanofi started phase 1 only in August, triggering a $10 million milestone, then terminated the program two months later without explaining why.

STAT6 transmits signals downstream of IL-4 and IL-13, central cytokines in type 2 inflammation. Once activated, STAT6 moves into the nucleus and promotes transcriptional programs involved in allergic inflammation, mucus production and alternative macrophage states. Degrading STAT6 could theoretically suppress both IL-4 and IL-13 biology inside the cell.

Two months is so fast that the decision could reflect early pharmacokinetics, an emerging safety issue, insufficient degradation at tolerated exposure or simply Sanofi’s broader portfolio restructuring. We should not invent a mechanistic failure without data. But the episode punctures a common industry habit: declaring clinical entry to be platform validation. Starting phase 1 proves that manufacturing and toxicology cleared the starting gate. It does not prove useful human pharmacology.

Finally, T-Therapeutics raised a $133 million Series B from investors including Lilly and the venture arms of Johnson & Johnson, Merck KGaA and Sanofi.

The company is developing bispecific molecules built from T-cell receptors, or TCRs, coupled to CD3-binding domains. Its oncology candidate TTX-008 is entering IND-enabling work, while TTX-018 follows in inflammation.

Conventional antibodies recognize structures exposed on a cell surface. TCRs can recognize peptide fragments derived from proteins inside the cell, provided those fragments are presented by HLA molecules. This greatly expands the potential target space: transcription factors, mutated enzymes and other intracellular proteins can become immunological addresses.

The bispecific then uses CD3 to recruit a T cell toward the peptide-HLA-bearing target cell. In cancer, the objective is killing. In autoimmune disease, T-Therapeutics wants to eliminate selected pathogenic immune-cell populations rather than suppressing the entire compartment.

The tradeoffs are substantial. Peptide-HLA complexes may be present at very low density. Patient eligibility depends on HLA type. Tumors can escape by losing antigen presentation. And CD3 engagement can trigger cytokine release even when the targeting logic looks exquisitely selective in vitro.

T-Therapeutics says its architecture maintains potency while reducing cytokine release. Every T-cell engager would like that sentence in its press release. Human dose escalation will decide whether the molecule actually separates cytotoxicity from systemic inflammation.
