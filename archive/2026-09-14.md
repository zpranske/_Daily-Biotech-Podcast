Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for September 14, 2026.

The TL;DR: Definium Therapeutics has now won three phase 3 trials in twelve weeks with its single-session LSD therapy. In generalized anxiety disorder, 100 micrograms of DT120 produced a 5.1-point placebo-adjusted improvement on the Hamilton Anxiety scale at week 12. The replication is impressive, but the supposedly subtherapeutic 50-microgram active control also improved symptoms by 3.6 points over placebo, leaving functional unblinding only partly resolved. In advanced modalities, Cellectis has abruptly killed both clinical allogeneic CAR-T programs and joined the rush toward in vivo editing—a striking reversal after declaring that 2026 would be its year. Oncology dominates the rest of the news: GSK’s Chinese-originated B7-H3 ADC delivered 18.5-month survival in small-cell lung cancer, Roche’s rival reached 13.3 months with potentially cleaner lung safety, BioNTech’s CTLA-4 antibody nearly doubled survival over docetaxel in an initial phase 3 cohort, and Cullinan’s EGFR inhibitor added six months of progression-free survival. The theme is replication under pressure: one successful trial opens the door; differentiation determines who gets through it.

Definium is the obvious place to begin, because we now have a genuinely new result following the phase 3 anxiety data we discussed last month.

The 245-patient Panorama trial tested DT120 ODT, an orally disintegrating LSD formulation, in generalized anxiety disorder. A single 100-microgram dose reduced Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale scores by 9.8 points at week 12, compared with a 4.7-point reduction on placebo. That leaves a placebo-adjusted benefit of 5.1 points.

Last month’s Voyage study produced a strikingly similar 5.4-point placebo-adjusted effect. DT120 also succeeded in a phase 3 major-depression trial in June. Three pivotal wins in twelve weeks is not normal biotech weather. Definium plans to meet the FDA in the fourth quarter and discuss filing for approval later this year.

The product proposition is also radically different from a daily SSRI or SNRI. Patients receive LSD once during an eight-hour session, with a chaperone present to monitor and guide them through the experience. The benefit then apparently persists for at least twelve weeks.

Eight hours is operationally heavy, but one eight-hour visit every few months may still compare favorably with daily medication, particularly for patients who cycle through several drugs because of sexual dysfunction, gastrointestinal effects, insomnia or emotional blunting. Clinics, however, need dedicated rooms, trained personnel and procedures for managing panic, blood-pressure changes and prolonged psychological distress. Definium is not launching only a tablet. It is launching a tablet attached to a clinical-service model.

Panorama was supposed to answer a more scientific question: can a psychedelic trial remain meaningfully blinded?

In addition to placebo, the study included 52 patients who received 50 micrograms of LSD. That dose was intended to be psychoactive enough that participants might believe they had received full treatment, but too low to provide therapeutic benefit.

The results are awkwardly interesting. The 50-microgram dose improved HAM-A scores by 3.6 points over placebo. That arm was not powered for a formal efficacy comparison, so we should not pretend it definitively worked. But 3.6 points is not nothing, and it complicates Definium’s active-control argument.

There are two broad interpretations. One is that 50 micrograms successfully generated expectancy and therefore pulled the control response upward, yet 100 micrograms still produced a larger 5.1-point benefit. That would support a genuine dose-dependent pharmacological effect.

The other is that 50 micrograms itself has therapeutic activity. If so, it is not a clean subtherapeutic control at all. It becomes a lower-dose treatment group.

And neither interpretation fully proves successful blinding. We need to know what patients and raters guessed they received, how intense the subjective effects were, and whether those guesses correlated with clinical improvement. “Everyone felt something” is not equivalent to “nobody knew which group they were in.”

Still, repeated phase 3 efficacy deserves respect. A single study can be unusually fortunate. Three positive studies across anxiety and depression make simple statistical luck much less plausible. The remaining debate concerns how much benefit comes from receptor pharmacology, acute psychological experience, expectancy, subsequent behavioral change and the supervised environment surrounding treatment.

For a synapse biologist, 5-HT2A signaling and psychedelic-associated plasticity remain enticing mechanistic territory. Cortical 5-HT2A activation can alter pyramidal-neuron excitability, recurrent network communication and dendritic-spine dynamics. But a transient plasticity window does not explain what information is being encoded during that window. The drug may make the network more changeable; the experience and environment may help determine what it changes into.

Definium now has a credible regulatory package. The FDA discussion will reveal whether the agency agrees that efficacy replication outweighs residual concerns about blinding, session standardization and psychological support.

Now to Cellectis, where the mood is less celebratory.

The French gene-editing company has terminated both of its clinical allogeneic CAR-T programs: lasme-cel in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and eti-cel in non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Lasme-cel had been approaching a pivotal phase 2 readout, while eti-cel was supposed to produce a fuller phase 1 dataset around the same time.

This is a remarkably sharp reversal. In February, CEO André Choulika declared that “2026 is our year” and described huge momentum in allogeneic CAR-T. Seven months later, the clinical programs are gone.

Allogeneic CAR-T begins with donor cells rather than each patient’s own T cells. Developers edit those donor cells to remove or alter endogenous immune-recognition machinery, install a tumor-targeting receptor and manufacture standardized batches in advance. The appeal is an off-the-shelf cellular medicine: no waiting weeks while a sick patient’s own cells are collected and engineered.

The difficulty is biological and commercial. Donor cells can be rejected by the recipient, and they can attack recipient tissue unless graft-versus-host biology is controlled. Their persistence may be shorter than autologous CAR-T. Meanwhile, bispecific antibodies can redirect a patient’s existing T cells with an immediately available, repeat-dosed product.

Cellectis says improved frontline treatment is also reducing relapse rates, leaving fewer B-ALL and lymphoma patients eligible for late-line cellular therapy. That is the cruel arithmetic of drug development: better care can shrink the market before your program arrives.

The company will redirect resources toward preclinical in vivo programs, including HEAL-101, a base-editing therapy targeting APOC3 in severe hypertriglyceridemia, and an in vivo epigenetic-editing candidate.

This joins the pivots we recently saw at ArsenalBio and TScan. In vivo engineering is becoming a sort of promised land for companies bruised by ex vivo cell-therapy logistics. Instead of manufacturing engineered cells outside the patient, deliver editing or expression cargo directly to the relevant cells inside the body.

The attraction is real. The delivery problem is also enormous. A nanoparticle or viral vector must find the intended cell population, enter it, release cargo, achieve sufficient editing and avoid dangerous activity in every unintended cell it encounters. Moving “in vivo” does not remove manufacturing complexity so much as transfer part of the manufacturing process into the patient, where quality control becomes rather more difficult.

For prospective employees, Cellectis is now an early-platform editing company again, not a late-stage allogeneic CAR-T company. Clinical-cell-therapy roles will contract. Delivery, genome editing, biodistribution and preclinical translational work become the surviving priorities.

The next two stories are best considered together because small-cell lung cancer is rapidly becoming a crowded contest among B7-H3 ADCs.

GSK and Hansoh Pharma reported that risvutatug rezetecan, mercifully shortened to ris-rez, produced median overall survival of 18.5 months in a 461-patient Chinese phase 3 trial. Topotecan produced 10.3 months. That is an 8.2-month difference in a disease where relapse after platinum chemotherapy is aggressive and treatment options have historically been miserable.

Roche and MediLink also won phase 3 with their B7-H3 ADC, tambotatug pelitecan, or tam-peli. Its median overall survival was 13.3 months, versus 9.4 months for topotecan.

On a superficial scoreboard, GSK wins: 18.5 months versus Roche’s 13.3. But these were separate Chinese trials, with potentially different patients, sites, disease characteristics and subsequent treatments. Cross-trial comparison is useful for forming hypotheses and dangerous for declaring champions.

B7-H3 is a surface protein highly expressed in several tumors, including small-cell lung cancer, with more limited expression in many normal tissues. An ADC uses the antibody as a molecular delivery address, bringing a cytotoxic payload preferentially toward antigen-expressing cells. Success still depends on antigen density, internalization, linker stability, payload release and bystander killing of adjacent tumor cells.

Safety may become Roche’s differentiator. Grade 3 interstitial lung disease occurred in 0.9% of tam-peli patients, with no more severe cases. The corresponding rates were 3.9% for GSK and Hansoh’s ADC and 4.4% for Merck and Daiichi Sankyo’s ifinatamab deruxtecan. Two patients died from treatment-related lung injury in the Merck-Daiichi study.

Interstitial lung disease is especially treacherous in lung-cancer patients, whose respiratory reserve may already be compromised by tumor, smoking-associated damage, previous radiation and infection. A slightly less effective drug with meaningfully safer pulmonary pharmacology could still earn substantial use.

But Roche cannot simply point to safety and ignore efficacy. Tam-peli’s 13.3-month survival also sits only modestly above the 12 months reported with Merck and Daiichi’s ADC and below the 13.6 months achieved by Amgen’s DLL3-by-CD3 T-cell engager Imdelltra.

GSK already has a global phase 3 trial underway, reporting in 2027. Roche now plans to start one quickly. Those global studies will determine whether the striking Chinese results travel—and whether ris-rez’s 18.5 months was a reproducible therapeutic advantage or an unusually favorable trial.

BioNTech has produced another intriguing lung-cancer result, this time by revisiting one of immuno-oncology’s oldest and most toxic targets.

In the first stage of the phase 3 Preserve-003 trial, gotistobart produced median overall survival of 18.5 months in metastatic squamous non-small cell lung cancer after prior therapy. Docetaxel produced 10 months.

That comparison is impressive because multiple sophisticated drugs have failed to improve much on docetaxel here. Trodelvy reached 11.1 months, Datroway reached 12.9 months, and Cabometyx plus Tecentriq reached 10.7 months in separate studies.

Gotistobart blocks CTLA-4, an inhibitory receptor that restrains T-cell activation. CTLA-4 competes with the costimulatory receptor CD28 for ligands on antigen-presenting cells, limiting the strength of T-cell priming. Block CTLA-4, and antitumor T cells may expand or remain active more effectively.

Unfortunately, immune tolerance also uses CTLA-4 for good reasons. Serious treatment-related adverse events occurred in 44.4% of gotistobart patients, versus 29.3% on docetaxel. Treatment-related toxicity drove discontinuation in 15.6%, versus 4.9%, and severe colitis was the most common high-grade adverse event.

The efficacy may justify that risk in relapsed squamous lung cancer. But the trial’s second stage still needs to replicate the survival effect. An 8.5-month advantage would be a major result. Until confirmation, it remains an unusually strong initial phase 3 cohort, not an established new standard.

Cullinan and Taiho also have potentially practice-changing lung-cancer data. Adding the EGFR inhibitor zipalertinib to chemotherapy extended median progression-free survival from 8.5 to 14.5 months in previously untreated NSCLC carrying EGFR exon 20 insertions. Response rates rose from 40.3% to 65%, and the median response lasted 14.2 months rather than 9.9.

Exon 20 insertions alter the kinase domain in ways that activate EGFR while making many conventional EGFR inhibitors bind poorly. These tumors therefore require molecules shaped and dosed specifically for this resistant structural state.

The immature overall-survival hazard ratio was 0.72, suggesting a 28% reduction in the risk of death, but that analysis needs more events. Toxicity was substantial: 87.1% of combination patients experienced grade 3 or worse adverse events, versus 54.4% on chemotherapy alone. Much of that was hematologic toxicity associated with the chemotherapy backbone, although rash remained a visible EGFR-related issue.

Zipalertinib will compete with Johnson & Johnson’s Rybrevant-based approach. Six additional months without progression is clinically meaningful. Whether it is commercially differentiating will depend on overall survival, tolerability and the convenience of an oral TKI relative to antibody-containing regimens.

A brief regulatory note: the FDA has delayed its decision on Exelixis’ zanzalintinib plus Tecentriq in metastatic colorectal cancer from December to March 2027 while awaiting updated efficacy and safety data. The application was already complicated by a missed overall-survival endpoint in the subgroup without liver metastases, despite success in the full study population.

Analysts still expect approval, but an extension is not clerical trivia. It indicates the agency wants to see whether the favorable overall result remains persuasive as more events accumulate—and whether the subgroup inconsistency represents noise or a biologically meaningful limitation.
