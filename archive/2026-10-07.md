Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for October 7, 2026.

The TL;DR: Genentech is paying Alector $100 million upfront for a preclinical Parkinson’s enzyme replacement that combines an engineered glucocerebrosidase with blood-brain-barrier delivery. It is a substantial bet that restoring lysosomal enzyme activity can interrupt the feedback loop between GBA deficiency and alpha-synuclein accumulation, although neither brain delivery nor disease modification has been demonstrated in humans. In glioblastoma, MimiVax’s survivin vaccine extended median survival from 20.2 to 24.2 months but missed its phase 2 primary endpoint; a younger-patient subgroup looks intriguing, not registration-ready. Caribou Biosciences is stopping its entire allogeneic CAR-T pipeline despite claiming autologous-like durability, marking another retreat from donor-derived cell therapy as capital rushes toward in vivo engineering. ArriVent’s brain-penetrant EGFR inhibitor failed phase 3, Nuvig abandoned a slow-enrolling CIDP study without layoffs, and AstraZeneca opened a 570,000-square-foot Kendall Square research center intended to house nearly 2,000 scientists. Money exists. It is concentrating around infrastructure and programs that can reach decisive evidence quickly.

Genentech and Alector are the natural place to begin, because this is a serious preclinical neuroscience deal rather than a tiny option decorated with a billion dollars of milestones.

Genentech will pay Alector $100 million upfront for exclusive rights to AL050, with up to $1.17 billion more available in milestones. Genentech takes responsibility for clinical development, manufacturing, regulatory work and commercialization. Alector retains ownership of the underlying blood-brain-barrier delivery platform.

AL050 is an enzyme replacement therapy aimed at Parkinson’s disease associated with GBA mutations. GBA encodes glucocerebrosidase, usually shortened to GCase, a lysosomal enzyme that breaks down glucosylceramide into glucose and ceramide.

Biallelic GBA mutations cause Gaucher disease. Carrying one pathogenic copy is also among the strongest common genetic risk factors for Parkinson’s disease. Not every carrier develops Parkinson’s, and many Parkinson’s patients have no GBA mutation, but reduced GCase activity appears to create a cellular environment in which alpha-synuclein is more likely to accumulate.

The relationship may become a vicious cycle. Impaired lysosomal lipid metabolism disrupts the membrane and trafficking environment needed for efficient protein clearance. Alpha-synuclein accumulates and aggregates. Those aggregates can then further interfere with lysosomal function and GCase trafficking. Less enzyme, more alpha-synuclein; more alpha-synuclein, still less useful enzyme activity.

AL050 attempts to intervene by supplying an engineered GCase with increased activity and half-life, attached to a molecular shuttle intended to cross the blood-brain barrier.

That final component is the program. Ordinary enzyme replacement works well in some peripheral lysosomal-storage diseases because circulating enzyme can be taken up by cells and delivered to lysosomes. The brain is another matter. Large proteins barely cross intact brain endothelium without an active transport strategy.

Alector has not publicly identified the shuttle mechanism here, so we should not fill the blank with our favorite receptor. Broadly, these systems bind an endothelial transport receptor and exploit receptor-mediated transcytosis. Affinity and valency have to be tuned carefully: bind too avidly, and the construct may become trapped inside endothelial lysosomes; bind too weakly, and little reaches brain tissue.

Then AL050 must enter the relevant brain cells and reach their lysosomes in an active form. Dopaminergic neurons matter, but GBA-associated pathology may also involve microglia and other cell types. “Crossed the barrier” is not synonymous with “restored lysosomal activity in the cells driving disease.”

Genentech’s $100 million upfront indicates that its diligence found the preclinical package persuasive. It does not tell us whether restoring GCase after alpha-synuclein pathology is established will slow Parkinson’s progression. Alector had previously backed away from its 2027 clinical-entry target, so the partnership also transfers a capital-intensive program to a company capable of funding large biomarker-rich trials.

For scientists, this is a strong signal around enzyme engineering, lysosomal biology, lipidomics, alpha-synuclein assays and quantitative brain biodistribution. Alector’s cash runway now extends into 2029, while Genentech gains another delivery architecture alongside Roche’s internal transferrin-receptor work.

The second neuroscience story is a failed glioblastoma trial containing a signal that deserves investigation—but not rhetorical resuscitation.

MimiVax tested SurVaxM in 233 evaluable patients with newly diagnosed glioblastoma. Median overall survival was 24.2 months with the vaccine and 20.2 months with placebo. Four additional months in glioblastoma would matter enormously. The study nevertheless missed its primary endpoint.

We do not have the hazard ratio, confidence interval or p-value, so “clinically meaningful” is currently management’s interpretation of a statistically unsuccessful comparison. Median survival also captures only the point at which half the patients have died; it does not describe the full survival curve or prove that the apparent difference is stable.

SurVaxM is a peptide-mimic vaccine directed against survivin. Survivin is an inhibitor-of-apoptosis-family protein that is highly expressed in many cancers, including most glioblastomas, while being limited in many differentiated adult tissues. It supports mitosis and helps malignant cells resist cell death.

Because survivin is intracellular, “accessible to the immune system” needs a little translation. Antibodies are not simply swimming into tumor cells and grabbing it. Tumor cells process survivin-derived peptides and display some of them on MHC molecules. The vaccine is intended to generate T cells capable of recognizing those peptide-MHC complexes.

Glioblastoma makes that difficult. The tumor is heterogeneous, lymphocyte-poor and rich in suppressive myeloid cells. Patients often receive dexamethasone, which can further suppress immunity. Tumor cells may also reduce antigen presentation or lose the relevant antigenic state under pressure. A highly prevalent target is useful, but prevalence does not guarantee productive T-cell access.

MimiVax’s hopeful finding came in patients aged 65 or younger. In that group, 44% of vaccine recipients were progression-free at 12 months, versus 24% on placebo. About 42% were alive at 30 months or later, with no additional deaths reported through as long as 54 months.

That tail of long-term survivors is interesting. The problem is subgroup credibility. Was the age cutoff prespecified? Was the trial stratified by age? How many patients remained in each arm, and did molecular factors such as MGMT promoter methylation, IDH status, steroid exposure or extent of surgical resection remain balanced?

A subgroup selected after the primary analysis fails can generate a hypothesis. It usually cannot support approval without prospective confirmation. MimiVax should run an age-enriched randomized trial if the effect remains convincing after full analysis. Glioblastoma’s terrible prognosis raises the urgency of testing the signal; it does not lower the requirement to show that the signal is real.

That distinction is especially important after today’s other brain-penetrant oncology failure.

ArriVent BioPharma’s firmonertinib missed the primary endpoint in a 398-patient phase 3 trial of first-line EGFR exon 20 insertion-positive non-small cell lung cancer. The stock fell 57%.

Firmonertinib was designed as a brain-penetrant EGFR inhibitor that could potentially replace chemotherapy-based treatment. At the higher dose, median progression-free survival by blinded independent central review was 11 months, versus 9.5 months with platinum chemotherapy. The lower dose achieved only 8.4 months.

The high-dose response rate looked considerably better—60% versus 33%—but responses did not translate into a statistically successful delay in progression.

The disagreement between central and investigator review is notable. Investigators estimated 11.1 months versus 7.1 months, a much more attractive separation. But central review is included precisely because local investigators know more about treatment and may interpret borderline scans differently. When the prespecified independent analysis fails, the more flattering local analysis is supportive at best.

ArriVent may still look to immature overall-survival data or combination therapy. Yet Johnson & Johnson’s Rybrevant plus chemotherapy has already produced 11.4 months of median progression-free survival versus 6.7 months for chemotherapy. A chemotherapy-free oral option could be attractive, particularly with reliable intracranial control, but convenience cannot substitute for a successful pivotal endpoint.

Now to Caribou Biosciences, where an entire advanced-modality company has reached the strategic-alternatives stage.

Caribou is discontinuing all clinical development and imposing a substantial workforce reduction while its board considers an asset sale, acquisition, merger or sale of the company. It had $113.8 million at the end of June and 97 employees earlier this year.

This is not a biotech that reached zero cash. It is a biotech whose remaining cash was judged insufficient to finance the next value-creating experiment.

Caribou spun out of Jennifer Doudna’s laboratory in 2011 and used CRISPR editing to create allogeneic, donor-derived CAR-T cells. Healthy-donor cells can be edited, expanded and stored in batches, potentially eliminating the weeks required to manufacture an autologous product from each patient.

Its lymphoma therapy vispa-cel had reportedly achieved median progression-free survival comparable to Bristol Myers Squibb’s Breyanzi and Kite’s Yescarta in phase 1, and Caribou had reached agreement with the FDA on a pivotal-trial design. Its second program, CB-011, targeted multiple myeloma.

Why stop with apparently competitive data? Because “comparable” is commercially awkward when the established autologous drugs already possess physician familiarity, manufacturing networks and approved labels. An allogeneic challenger must demonstrate more than activity. It needs reliably faster access, scalable economics and enough persistence to prevent relapse, without graft-versus-host disease or rapid rejection by the recipient’s immune system.

A pivotal cell-therapy trial plus commercial manufacturing would consume far more than $113.8 million.

Caribou now joins Cellectis in terminating clinical allogeneic CAR-T work, while ArsenalBio and TScan have sharply contracted around in vivo engineering. Investors increasingly prefer the idea of delivering genetic cargo directly into a patient’s T cells, avoiding both individualized ex vivo manufacturing and donor-cell rejection.

The in vivo field has not yet proved that it can target enough T cells selectively and safely inside humans. Still, capital markets fund anticipated platform advantages, not only today’s clinical dataset. Caribou has been stranded by that shift.

For the job market, the immediate consequence is ugly: donor-cell engineering, clinical cell-therapy and manufacturing roles will disappear unless a buyer preserves the assets. A scientifically credible program can become financially unadvanceable when its modality loses investor sponsorship.

Nuvig Therapeutics offers a less catastrophic example of capital following enrollment speed.

Nuvig stopped a phase 2 study of NVG-2089 in chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy, or CIDP, after enrolling only 14 patients since April 2025. The company says the decision was not safety-related and does not involve layoffs. A separate study in immune thrombocytopenia is continuing.

CIDP is an autoimmune peripheral neuropathy in which immune attack damages myelin and sometimes axons, producing weakness, numbness and impaired reflexes. It is biologically compelling but operationally difficult: rare, heterogeneous, slowly assessed and already served by immunoglobulin products plus argenx’s Vyvgart Hytrulo.

For a small company, slow enrollment becomes a scientific risk because the answer arrives too late. Nuvig raised a $161 million Series B in 2024 from investors including Novo Holdings and the venture arms of Bristol Myers Squibb, Sanofi and Bayer. Even that backing does not make an inefficient indication attractive indefinitely.

ITP offers faster platelet-count readouts and a clearer near-term efficacy signal. It is also suddenly crowded with immune-reset approaches, including Novartis and Abogen’s mRNA-encoded CD19-by-CD3 engager. Nuvig has preserved the company, but its sole clinical asset now needs the ITP study to establish why this protein therapy deserves further indication expansion.

The capital picture is not uniformly bleak. European life-sciences investor Forbion has raised €2.3 billion, about $2.6 billion, across venture and growth funds intended to support roughly 30 companies. That averages to nearly $87 million per company if divided evenly—which it will not be.

“Dry powder” is venture jargon for committed money that has not yet been invested. Forbion now has plenty. The important caveat is selectivity: a record fund does not rescue every platform. It allows the investor to demand stronger data, better terms and a credible path to the next milestone. Forbion-backed Beacon recently won its retinal gene-therapy trial; Gate Bioscience expanded its Lilly partnership this week. Those are exactly the kinds of externally validated programs attracting capital.

And AstraZeneca has provided today’s clearest concrete hiring signal.

The company opened a 570,000-square-foot, 18-story R&D center in Kendall Square, with ten interconnected laboratory floors and plans to house nearly 2,000 researchers and scientists. The work spans oncology, obesity, metabolic and respiratory disease, rare disease, cell therapy and biologics, with robotics, laboratory automation and agentic AI built into the site.

This is part of a $1 billion Massachusetts commitment and a plan to increase AstraZeneca’s state workforce by more than 50% in the coming years. The location sits beside the Broad Institute and near Thermo Fisher’s gene-therapy manufacturing operation.

For scientists making the academia-to-industry transition, this is more meaningful than a generic promise to “expand the ecosystem.” A physical site with nearly 2,000 people creates roles in wet-lab discovery, translational biology, automation, imaging, multi-omics, protein science and cell therapy. It will also create intense competition for candidates who can bridge assay development with computational analysis and industrial reproducibility.

Today’s stories capture the capital market’s strange geometry. Genentech can spend $100 million upfront on a preclinical Parkinson’s enzyme because the genetics, lysosomal mechanism and brain-delivery platform create strategic value. Forbion can raise $2.6 billion, and AstraZeneca can open laboratories for 2,000 scientists.

At the same time, Caribou cannot finance a pivotal trial for a clinically active CAR-T platform, and Nuvig will abandon an indication because 14 patients in 18 months is simply too slow.

Capital has not disappeared. It has become impatient, modality-conscious and extremely sensitive to how quickly a program can produce evidence that changes its value.

And that’s the roundup for today.
