Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for September 16, 2026.

The TL;DR: Lilly is partnering with Toronto’s QurCan Therapeutics on polymer-lipid nanoparticles intended to carry genetic medicines across the blood-brain barrier. It is exactly the kind of extrahepatic nucleic-acid delivery problem the field desperately wants to solve—and exactly the kind of mostly biobucks platform deal that should not be mistaken for validation in humans. In clinical neuroscience, Johnson & Johnson’s M1 muscarinic antagonist failed a phase 2 depression trial after already missing in multiple sclerosis, while Axoltis is leaning heavily on functional and blood-brain-barrier secondary endpoints after its ALS peptide missed the primary neurofilament analysis. The FDA has launched a pilot intended to shorten U.S. phase 1 timelines by six months to a year, partly in response to China and Australia. Sionna is cutting 46% of its workforce after its cystic-fibrosis failure, and psychedelic medicine’s emerging regulatory problem is no longer simply whether these drugs work. It is who supervises an eight-hour treatment session—and who pays for it.

Let’s begin with Lilly and QurCan, because effective, repeat-dosable delivery of nucleic acids into specific cells of the nervous system remains one of biotechnology’s most valuable unsolved problems.

Lilly will work with QurCan Therapeutics on multiple CNS and peripheral-nervous-system programs using QurCan’s TERP platform. TERP stands for target-engineered responsive polymer, and the resulting delivery vehicles are described as polymer-lipid nanoparticles, or PLNPs.

The financial headline is up to $237 million per program, plus royalties. But the immediate payment and Lilly’s strategic equity investment were not disclosed. So do not mentally multiply $237 million by “multiple programs” and conclude that QurCan just received a small sovereign-wealth fund. Most of that money is contingent biobucks, payable only if programs advance through research, clinical, regulatory and commercial milestones.

The scientifically interesting part is the hybrid nanoparticle architecture. Ordinary lipid nanoparticles use ionizable lipids and other components to package RNA, protect it from degradation, support uptake and help cargo escape from endosomes. Adding responsive polymers could potentially alter particle stability, tissue targeting or cargo release in response to local conditions such as pH, enzymes or redox state.

The claim that matters is systemic delivery across the blood-brain barrier.

Crossing the barrier is only the first of several gates. A useful CNS nanoparticle must survive serum proteins and immune clearance, avoid simply disappearing into liver and spleen, traverse brain endothelium, move through the perivascular environment, reach the intended neuronal or glial population, enter those cells and escape the endosome before its cargo is degraded. Each step can reduce productive delivery by an order of magnitude. A lovely biodistribution image showing fluorescent signal in brain tissue does not prove that enough functional RNA reached the correct cytosolic compartment.

Cell type is crucial. Deliver an mRNA to astrocytes, an siRNA to microglia and a gene editor to neurons, and you have created three different medicines even if every particle crosses the same endothelial layer. For a developmental synapse program, interneuron versus pyramidal-neuron delivery could completely change both efficacy and risk.

QurCan says the platform is nonviral and repeat-dosable. That offers a potential advantage over AAV, where preexisting or treatment-induced antibodies can complicate redosing and where long-lived expression is difficult to reverse. A nanoparticle can theoretically be stopped, adjusted or reformulated.

Lilly brings a growing collection of genetic-medicine tools, including Verve’s editing programs and recombinase collaborations with Profluent and Seamless. What it does not yet have is a generally proven delivery system for putting arbitrary genetic cargo into arbitrary nervous-system cells. Neither does anyone else. This deal buys experiments against that problem.

Watch for three things: the actual cargo, the administration route and quantitative cell-level biodistribution in large animals. “CNS delivery” without those details is still platform vocabulary, not a therapeutic result.

The FDA, meanwhile, is trying to shorten the path from preclinical work to those first human experiments.

The agency has opened a pilot program pairing sponsors with “qualified research institutions,” or QRIs. These could include contract research organizations, academic medical centers, regulatory consultants and other groups with demonstrated expertise in toxicology, chemistry and manufacturing, clinical pharmacology, patient recruitment and safety monitoring.

About eight to ten sponsor-QRI pairs will be selected. Applications are due October 30.

The pairs will work with the FDA on an expedited, rolling investigational-new-drug submission. Instead of assembling every component of an IND and presenting the agency with one enormous finished package, sponsors may be able to submit modules as they become ready, identify deficiencies earlier and avoid discovering late in the process that a toxicology study or manufacturing assay does not answer the regulator’s question.

The target is ambitious: reduce phase 1 timelines by six months to a year.

This is partly a competitiveness initiative. Australia has long attracted first-in-human studies through relatively rapid ethics and regulatory pathways plus tax incentives. China can move programs into patients with extraordinary speed, including through investigator-initiated trials.

But speed cannot be the only metric. We recently discussed two deaths in Chinese gene-editing studies that became public only belatedly, including the death of a six-year-old with a nonfatal neurodevelopmental condition. The lesson is not that slow regulation is automatically safe. It is that acceleration needs rigorous preclinical evidence, product characterization, stopping rules and immediate adverse-event disclosure.

The FDA’s pilot is sensibly prioritizing novel products for severe diseases that already have enough preclinical data for meaningful review. In other words, this is intended to remove avoidable administrative latency—not wave scientifically immature products past the starting line.

For smaller biotechs, a good QRI could function like an experienced mountaineering guide: not carrying the drug to the summit, but preventing the team from spending six months climbing the wrong ridge. The danger is that access becomes limited to sponsors able to afford elite regulatory and CRO support. Eight to ten pairs will generate a useful experiment, not repair the entire U.S. phase 1 system.

Now to Johnson & Johnson’s latest CNS disappointment.

JNJ-5120, also known as PIPE-307, failed a 107-person phase 2 trial in major depressive disorder. Neither tested regimen beat placebo on the depression scale at day five.

J&J licensed the drug from Pipeline Therapeutics—now called Contineum—in 2023, paying $50 million upfront and offering up to $1 billion in milestones. The asset has already failed a phase 2 study in relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis. Two misses across two diseases make the remaining path very narrow.

JNJ-5120 antagonizes the M1 muscarinic acetylcholine receptor. That is an interesting counterpoint to MapLight’s M1-and-M4 agonist program and Bristol Myers Squibb’s Cobenfy, where stimulating muscarinic signaling is intended to improve psychosis.

This is not necessarily pharmacological contradiction. Receptor function depends on circuit, cell type, disease and timescale. M1 signaling influences neuronal excitability, synaptic plasticity and cognition. In multiple sclerosis, PIPE-307 was developed partly around evidence that blocking M1 on oligodendrocyte-lineage cells could promote differentiation and remyelination. In depression, the clinical hypothesis appears to have involved rapid changes in cholinergic and mood-related circuitry.

But a mechanistic rationale spanning remyelination and rapid antidepressant effects should raise the demand for clear human pharmacology. It did not deliver that at day five.

Depression trials certainly have high placebo responses and heterogeneous patients. William Blair had assigned only a 35% probability of success. Still, placebo volatility is a development risk, not an exemption from the endpoint. If the drug cannot separate from placebo in a randomized trial designed around its proposed rapid effect, the clean interpretation is that the experiment failed.

J&J is reviewing exploratory endpoints, but investors barely moved Contineum’s stock because most of the company’s value is attached to PIPE-791, an LPAR1 antagonist in pulmonary fibrosis. Sometimes a clinical failure produces a muted stock reaction not because the market is optimistic, but because it had already assigned the asset very little value.

Axoltis Pharma has supplied another failed neurological primary endpoint, this time in ALS.

Its phase 2 study enrolled 82 patients and tested NX210c, a synthetic peptide derived from sequences in SCO-spondin, a large extracellular glycoprotein associated with neural development and cell interactions. Patients received intravenous drug three times weekly for four weeks.

At week six, NX210c did not significantly change blood neurofilament light chain, or NfL, relative to baseline. NfL is a structural axonal protein released when neurons are injured. Elevated blood or cerebrospinal-fluid NfL can therefore serve as a marker of ongoing neuroaxonal damage.

The primary endpoint missed. Even after four months, the numerical NfL trend was not statistically significant.

Axoltis instead emphasized apparent slowing on an ALS functional scale and a reduction in plasma claudin-5 at the higher dose. Claudin-5 is a tight-junction protein highly important to brain endothelial barrier integrity. One correction to the source article: claudin-5 is not a transporter that carries molecules into the brain. It forms part of the paracellular seal between endothelial cells.

The company interprets lower circulating claudin-5 as evidence of blood-brain-barrier restoration. That is interesting, but indirect. Plasma concentrations can be influenced by protein release, turnover, clearance and assay behavior. Demonstrating barrier repair would ideally involve convergent evidence from permeability imaging, cerebrospinal-fluid-to-blood ratios, endothelial biomarkers and clinical effects.

The functional data also need exact values, confidence intervals and a clear explanation of the analysis. Axoltis says the lower dose produced a 64% reduction in functional decline while the higher dose produced 33%. A lower dose looking substantially better than a higher dose is possible, but it immediately raises questions about sample size, baseline imbalance and noise.

ALS is littered with small studies where a secondary functional signal looks hopeful after the biomarker primary fails. NX210c may deserve another experiment. The current study does not establish that the peptide slowed neurodegeneration, repaired the barrier or belongs in Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s and multiple sclerosis, despite the CEO already naming all three.

That is indication expansion by press release. Human replication should come first.

A more operational CNS story comes from the FDA’s public hearing on psychedelic medicines. This follows yesterday’s third phase 3 win for Definium’s LSD formulation and Compass Pathways’ rolling application for synthetic psilocybin.

The debate is shifting from “can psychedelics produce durable symptom improvement?” toward “what healthcare system must surround the drug?”

Developers have generally used dosing-session monitors rather than requiring formal psychotherapy as part of the labeled intervention. That helps isolate the molecule’s effect and could reduce cost. Psychologists at the hearing argued that the therapeutic relationship is not optional scenery and that psychedelics may work better when paired with structured therapy. Psychiatrists emphasized screening for bipolar disorder, psychosis risk, suicidality and medical complications.

This matters because an eight-hour LSD session consumes a room and staff for an entire working day. Require a psychiatrist and psychotherapist throughout, and access narrows while cost rises. Permit a trained monitor with psychiatric backup, and the model becomes more scalable—but potentially less capable of managing complex reactions.

The FDA may use a risk-management program to specify site certification, monitor training and post-dose observation. The reimbursement question then becomes unavoidable: is an insurer paying for a drug, a procedure, several hours of monitoring, psychotherapy—or some bundled combination?

Psychedelic efficacy has moved ahead of psychedelic infrastructure. Approval may be approaching, but the product cannot be separated cleanly from the room, the person in the room and what happens during the experience.

Sionna Therapeutics is confronting a much less abstract consequence of failed clinical translation: it is cutting 46% of its workforce.

We covered the underlying cystic-fibrosis failure last month. SION-719, an NBD1 stabilizer added to Vertex’s Trikafta, reduced sweat chloride by only about one millimole per liter—far below the five-to-ten-millimole change observers wanted.

The genuinely new development is that Sionna will proceed with a different combination, SION-451 plus the CFTR corrector SION-2222, while reducing a workforce that numbered 59 at the start of the year. It retains $268.3 million and expects runway into the second half of 2029.

Management says post hoc analysis identified factors that may have blunted the SION-719 result. Perhaps. But SION-451 now carries the burden of showing that NBD1 stabilization is valuable as part of a proprietary replacement combination, even though it contributed essentially nothing on top of Trikafta.

This is not a cash crisis. It is a thesis-concentration event. Sionna has enough money to preserve many jobs and has decided that doing so would reduce the probability of funding the next decisive trial. The retained roles will cluster around clinical development, regulatory work, technical operations and the surviving combination. Discovery breadth is paying for one more test.

Finally, GSK is licensing another trispecific T-cell engager from China’s Chimagen Biosciences, this time for multiple myeloma. The total package reaches $750 million, but the upfront was not disclosed.

The molecule binds two undisclosed tumor antigens plus a T-cell target, presumably CD3. Dual tumor recognition could improve coverage across heterogeneous myeloma cells or reduce escape when one antigen is downregulated. Existing bispecifics targeting BCMA or GPRC5D can produce major responses, but cytokine-release syndrome, infections and neurological toxicity remain important constraints.

GSK wants deeper responses with better tolerability. Naturally. The problem is that potency and inflammatory toxicity emerge from related biology: force enough productive T-cell synapses to eradicate tumor cells, and cytokines tend to notice.

AbbVie already paid $700 million upfront for a CD38-by-BCMA-by-CD3 trispecific, and J&J has a BCMA-by-GPRC5D-by-CD3 molecule in phase 3. Chimagen’s candidate does not enter phase 1 until next year. The field will soon learn whether trispecificity genuinely improves response durability or merely creates more complex manufacturing, pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity.

That connects with Electra Therapeutics’ proposed IPO of up to $342 million. Electra is spending most of the proceeds on ipsoprubart in secondary HLH, the life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome closely related to the fatal IEC-HS events that recently halted autoimmune CAR-T studies. Its early study reported 100% eight-week survival—but in only 12 frontline patients.

Electra’s financing ambition shows how valuable controllable immune modulation has become. After the recent CAR-T deaths, a therapy capable of selectively depleting pathological immune cells without permanently engineering them will attract attention. A 12-patient survival result earns a phase 2/3 trial. It does not yet earn certainty or a $342 million victory lap.

Today’s stories repeatedly return to control. Lilly wants repeat-dosable nanoparticles rather than irreversible CNS delivery. The FDA wants faster trials without importing weaker oversight. J&J and Axoltis are deciding whether exploratory findings justify another study after primary failures. Psychedelic developers must define who controls the treatment environment. And GSK wants T-cell engagement powerful enough to clear myeloma but controlled enough not to overwhelm the patient.

Speed and potency are easy ambitions to state. The useful medicine delivers both in the right place—and gives clinicians a way to stop when biology stops cooperating.
