Let’s begin with Latigo, because this is an update to a neuroscience financing story we discussed two weeks ago—and the scale is now clearer.

Latigo plans to sell 16 million shares at between $16 and $18 each. At the midpoint, that would generate about $247 million in net proceeds, potentially rising to $285 million if the underwriters exercise their option to buy additional shares.

That is a serious amount of capital for a company founded only in 2024. Latigo previously raised a $135 million Series A and a $150 million Series B, so it could soon have attracted well over half a billion dollars around one central idea: selectively inhibit Nav1.8 to suppress peripheral pain without the addiction, respiratory depression and central sedation associated with opioids.

Nav1.8 is a voltage-gated sodium channel enriched in nociceptive sensory neurons. When these neurons are depolarized, sodium channels generate the inward current needed to initiate and propagate action potentials. Blocking Nav1.8 should therefore make peripheral pain fibers less excitable while largely avoiding direct interference with brain circuits.

The target is no longer hypothetical. Vertex’s suzetrigine, sold as Journavx, became the first approved Nav1.8 inhibitor last year. Latigo’s lead candidate, LTG-001, is entering phase 3, including a study in patients undergoing bunion surgery. About $125 million of the IPO proceeds is earmarked for that program and for “commercial readiness”—industry language meaning Latigo is already thinking about manufacturing, reimbursement, medical affairs and a possible launch, not just clinical data.

Latigo claims Journavx is limited by efficacy, slow onset and contraindications. Fair enough, but competitors always describe the incumbent’s imperfections with remarkable fluency. LTG-001 needs direct evidence of faster analgesia, a larger effect, cleaner drug interactions or some other clinically noticeable advantage. Selectivity for Nav1.8 is now the entry ticket, not the differentiator.

The company is also developing an intravenous formulation for postoperative hospital use, potentially followed by oral dosing after discharge. For the job market, Latigo is moving into the expensive build-out phase: phase 3 operations, clinical pharmacology, safety, regulatory work and launch preparation. That is not merely legal theater. When a small biotech plans around a merger, it may stop financing an independent future. If the buyer then walks away, the target is suddenly left with its original burn rate, diminished leverage and no transaction proceeds. Hence the brutal head-count reduction.

The scientific connection between these companies was genuinely interesting. Lisata’s certepetide, also called CEND-1, is a cyclic tumor-penetrating peptide. It first interacts with alpha-v integrins enriched in parts of the tumor microenvironment. Proteolytic processing then exposes a motif that binds neuropilin-1, activating a tissue-penetration pathway that can increase movement of co-administered agents through tumor tissue.

Solid tumors are not simply loose bags of malignant cells. Dense extracellular matrix, abnormal vasculature, high interstitial pressure and heterogeneous perfusion can prevent both therapeutics and imaging probes from reaching the interior. Certepetide is intended to make that physical delivery barrier more permeable.

Kuva had licensed the peptide to combine it with NanoMark, its MRI-agent platform, potentially increasing contrast-agent access to solid tumors. That could have produced a useful theranostic pairing: imaging where the peptide-enabled probe distributes, then using related biology to improve drug delivery. It might also help identify tumors with a microenvironment permissive to certepetide activity.

For scientists, a 72% layoff after a failed merger is a reminder that transaction risk can abruptly override experimental progress. Amgen has discontinued phase 1 candidate AMG 513. The drug had previously experienced an FDA clinical hold, although Amgen said the decision to stop development was based on its internal standards rather than that hold. This follows the 2024 termination of another early obesity drug, AMG 786.

Amgen’s near-term obesity strategy is therefore concentrated almost entirely in MariTide, an antibody-peptide conjugate that activates GLP-1 receptors while inhibiting GIP receptors. Its most commercially striking feature is potential dosing only four to six times per year.

That is mechanistically and strategically distinct from Lilly’s tirzepatide, which activates both GLP-1 and GIP receptors. MariTide pairs GLP-1 agonism with GIP-receptor antagonism—the opposite directional choice at GIP. The fact that both strategies can plausibly promote weight loss tells you that incretin physiology is more complicated than a simple “GIP good” or “GIP bad” diagram. Timing, receptor desensitization, tissue distribution and the balance with GLP-1 signaling all matter.

Amgen is running phase 3 studies in obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular risk and obstructive sleep apnea, with major weight-loss data expected next year. Long-interval dosing could improve convenience, but it also means adverse effects may not be rapidly reversible. If a patient feels miserable after an injection, the molecule does not politely leave on Tuesday.

“Biased agonism” means favoring some signaling outputs downstream of a receptor over others—potentially preserving metabolic efficacy while reducing receptor internalization, desensitization or adverse effects. It is attractive pharmacology. It does not guarantee an attractive drug.

Pfizer is left with one oral GLP-1 candidate, licensed from China’s YaoPharma, while its obesity pipeline is led by monthly injectable berobenatide.
