Let’s start with ADARx, because this is both an RNA-therapeutics financing story and an unusually useful map of where extrahepatic siRNA is trying to go next.

San Diego-based ADARx Pharmaceuticals has filed to list on Nasdaq. It has not yet said how many shares it will sell or at what price, so there is no reliable IPO value to quote. But this is not a cash-starved startup using the public markets to keep the lights on. ADARx entered 2026 with $427.3 million in cash and employs about 100 people.

That war chest came partly from a $200 million Series C in 2023 and partly from AbbVie, which paid a substantial $335 million upfront last year for options on next-generation siRNA programs across multiple disease areas.

The proposed IPO tells you how expensive ADARx’s plan has become. It wants to support three clinical-stage drugs, potentially begin phase 3 trials in several complement diseases, prepare one product for commercialization and move two more candidates—including an Alzheimer’s drug—into human testing next year. Four hundred million dollars suddenly looks less infinite when several randomized trials begin consuming it simultaneously.

The most advanced asset is onvuzosiran, a phase 3 siRNA intended to prevent attacks of hereditary angioedema. It targets prekallikrein, the inactive precursor of plasma kallikrein.

In many hereditary-angioedema patients, deficient or dysfunctional C1 inhibitor allows the contact system to become overactive. Kallikrein cleaves high-molecular-weight kininogen and generates bradykinin, which increases vascular permeability. Fluid then escapes into tissue, producing severe swelling in the skin, gastrointestinal tract or, most dangerously, the airway.

Silencing prekallikrein in the liver reduces the amount of substrate available to become active kallikrein. The mechanism is already clinically validated by Takeda’s antibody Takhzyro and by approved RNAi therapy Dawnzera. ADARx therefore needs competitive attack prevention, durable dosing and tolerability, not merely proof that kallikrein matters. IPO proceeds will fund the pivotal study and “pre-commercial activities”—manufacturing inventory, medical affairs, reimbursement planning and the infrastructure required to launch if the data and FDA cooperate.

ADARx’s second major program, agazisiran, targets complement factor B. Factor B is central to the alternative complement pathway, where it associates with activated C3 fragments and helps form the C3 convertase. That convertase generates more activated C3, creating an amplification loop.

This is why factor B can influence diseases initiated through different routes. Even if an immune complex first engages the classical pathway, alternative-pathway amplification can greatly increase the eventual complement injury.

ADARx is testing agazisiran in IgA nephropathy, C3 glomerulopathy, immune-complex membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and geographic atrophy. That is an enormous indication spread. The biology connecting them is complement dysregulation; the relevant tissues, endpoints and competitive landscapes are very different.

Geographic atrophy is particularly challenging because approved complement inhibitors only modestly slow retinal-lesion expansion and require repeated intravitreal injections. A systemically administered siRNA would need to suppress complement enough to influence retinal injury without creating unacceptable infection risk. It would also need imaging evidence—typically fundus autofluorescence and optical coherence tomography—that lesion growth is actually slowed. Elegant complement pharmacodynamics will not compensate for another barely perceptible anatomical effect.

Then comes ADX-626, an siRNA against coagulation factor 11, or factor XI. ADARx is considering it for secondary stroke prevention and atrial fibrillation.

Factor XI sits in the intrinsic coagulation pathway and may contribute substantially to pathological thrombosis while being less essential for normal hemostasis than factor 10 or thrombin. People with congenital factor XI deficiency often have relatively modest bleeding compared with severe hemophilia, suggesting a possible therapeutic window: reduce clotting risk without paying the full bleeding penalty associated with conventional anticoagulation.

That idea is attractive but has already produced mixed clinical outcomes across the industry. ADARx will need to show that sustained RNA silencing can be titrated safely, because unlike a short-lived pill, an siRNA effect cannot be switched off immediately when a patient begins bleeding or requires emergency surgery.

The two preclinical programs may be even more interesting for platform scientists. ADX-077 is described as adipose-targeted RNAi for obesity. ADX-199 is aimed at Alzheimer’s disease.

Neither target is disclosed, which makes serious biological evaluation impossible. The delivery claims are therefore the story.

GalNAc-conjugated oligonucleotides have made hepatocyte delivery comparatively routine by exploiting the asialoglycoprotein receptor. Adipocytes and brain cells do not offer such a conveniently mature delivery route. An adipose-targeted construct must exit the vasculature, penetrate extracellular matrix, enter the appropriate adipocyte or stromal population and escape the endosome. A CNS siRNA must additionally confront the blood-brain barrier unless it is administered directly into cerebrospinal fluid.

For ADX-199, watch the administration route and cell-type distribution. “Alzheimer’s drug” could mean intrathecal silencing in neurons, uptake by glia or even suppression of a peripheral protein that influences brain disease. Those are profoundly different propositions.

ADARx was co-founded and is led by Zhen Li, an Arrowhead and Merck veteran. Now to the week’s most consequential RNA failure: pelacarsen.

Novartis and Ionis enrolled 8,323 high-risk patients in the phase 3 Lp(a)Horizon trial. Participants had elevated lipoprotein(a), pronounced “L-P-little-A,” and either established cardiovascular disease or a previous cardiovascular event. Pelacarsen lowered Lp(a), but it did not reduce the overall risk of heart attack, stroke or other major cardiovascular events.

This is a major disappointment because human genetics have strongly implicated Lp(a) in cardiovascular risk.

Lp(a) is essentially an LDL-like lipoprotein particle attached to apolipoprotein(a). Apo(a) contains repeated structural domains called kringles and can carry oxidized phospholipids. The particle may promote atherosclerosis through cholesterol deposition, vascular inflammation and potentially effects related to thrombosis. Lp(a) concentration is largely genetically determined and does not respond dramatically to lifestyle modification.

That combination—a common, inherited, relatively fixed risk factor—looked like ideal territory for RNA medicine.

Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces hepatic production of apo(a). Previous studies suggested average Lp(a) lowering of roughly 72%, and Ionis reportedly saw a similar reduction in this trial.

Seventy-two percent sounds enormous. Clinically, it was insufficient.

There are several possible interpretations. Lp(a) could be causally important over decades but difficult to modify late, after patients already have established vascular disease. The treatment period may have been too short relative to the biology. Benefit may be restricted to patients with the highest starting concentrations. Or there may be a nonlinear threshold: reducing Lp(a) by 72% might leave some patients above the level where risk meaningfully falls.

The harsher possibility is that circulating Lp(a) is a more useful risk marker than therapeutic bottleneck in this population. Genetics can tell us that lifelong exposure matters without guaranteeing that lowering the molecule later reverses accumulated disease.

Amgen’s olpasiran and Lilly’s lepodisiran now carry the burden of the class. Both are RNA-interference drugs that have produced greater than 90% lowering. Their defenders argue that deeper suppression may cross a biological threshold that pelacarsen missed.

This rhymes with Novo Nordisk’s recent failure of the IL-6 antibody ziltivekimab in cardiovascular disease. In both cases, a risk-associated biological signal was successfully reduced without changing cardiovascular outcomes. Biomarkers can be causally informative and still fail as practical intervention points in late-stage human disease.
