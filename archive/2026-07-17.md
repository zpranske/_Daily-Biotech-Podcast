The TL;DR: Biogen has produced the most credible clinical signal yet for lowering tau in Alzheimer’s disease. Its antisense drug diranersen reduced brain tau and appeared to slow cognitive decline by roughly 20% to 50%, depending on the scale—but the trial technically failed because the lowest dose worked best, wrecking the prespecified dose-response analysis. That is promising, not definitive. Johnson & Johnson, meanwhile, abandoned a complement-regulating retinal gene therapy after phase 2b. Outside CNS, GSK wrote off a $2 billion chronic-cough acquisition, and AdvanCell raised a striking $315 million to industrialize lead-212 alpha radiotherapy. The thread running through today is translation: elegant mechanisms are plentiful; reproducible clinical effects, workable administration and scalable manufacturing remain the scarce commodities.

Let’s begin with tau, because Biogen’s phase 2 Celia study is scientifically interesting and statistically awkward in almost equal measure.

Diranersen is an antisense oligonucleotide, originally developed with Ionis, that binds the messenger RNA encoding tau and recruits the cell’s RNA-degradation machinery. Less tau mRNA means less tau protein available to misfold, aggregate and potentially propagate through vulnerable neural circuits. The drug is delivered intrathecally, directly into cerebrospinal fluid, every six months. That is not trivial, but it is much less frequent than regular intravenous anti-amyloid antibody infusions.

At 18 months, the lowest tested dose—60 milligrams—produced 26% less decline than placebo on the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes. The apparent slowing was 42% on the cognitive portion of the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale and 50% on the Mini-Mental State Examination. Two composite measures showed 30% and 23% less decline.

Those are attractive numbers. They also come from only about 60 patients at that dose, and the drug did not beat placebo on a measure of daily functioning. That matters. Preserving a test score is useful, but preserving the ability to navigate a kitchen, manage medication or recognize what needs doing next is what patients and caregivers ultimately experience.

The trial’s primary endpoint required a dose-dependent response. It failed because the lowest dose looked better than the higher doses. Biogen argues that the study still established proof of concept and identified the dose for phase 3. That is defensible, but phase 3 design now carries an uncomfortable question: was 60 milligrams genuinely optimal, or was this a small-group statistical winner that will regress toward the mean?

There was also post-infusion confusion, mostly mild or moderate and generally resolving within roughly a week. Interestingly, that occurred least often at the 60-milligram dose. Unlike anti-amyloid antibodies, diranersen has not shown the same concern about ARIA—the edema and microhemorrhage visible on MRI that can accompany aggressive amyloid clearance.

For a synapse biologist, tau may also feel closer to the business end of neurodegeneration than extracellular amyloid does. Pathological tau tracks neuronal dysfunction and clinical stage more closely, and its disruption of microtubules, axonal transport and compartmentalized cellular organization can ultimately compromise synaptic maintenance. Still, lowering total tau is not automatically equivalent to selectively removing toxic tau species. Tau has normal neuronal functions. The phase 3 trial must establish that partial chronic suppression produces meaningful benefit without delayed liabilities.

The industry read is cautiously enthusiastic. This is probably the strongest combined biomarker-and-clinical signal yet for a tau-lowering therapy. It is not yet the clean validation that Biogen’s presentation makes it sound like.

Roche is watching closely. The company no longer has a clinical tau program after walking away from UCB’s bepranemab, but its neuroscience leadership says it remains “very interested” in tau. Roche’s nearer-term Alzheimer’s bet is trontinemab, an anti-amyloid antibody coupled to a transferrin-receptor-based Brainshuttle. The idea is to exploit receptor-mediated transcytosis across the blood-brain barrier, increasing antibody delivery into brain tissue rather than leaving most of a conventional antibody circulating in the periphery.

In a small open-label extension, Roche reported continued amyloid PET lowering and decreases in plasma GFAP, a marker associated with astrocyte reactivity and neuroinflammation. Only two cases of ARIA-related edema occurred among 126 patients. Rapid plaque clearance with unusually low ARIA would give trontinemab a plausible advantage over Leqembi and Kisunla, although an uncontrolled extension can establish pharmacology far more convincingly than clinical efficacy.

The exciting future here is not necessarily tau versus amyloid. It may be sequencing or combining them: clear amyloid relatively quickly, then suppress tau to protect downstream neuronal function. That sounds biologically satisfying. It will also be expensive, operationally cumbersome and difficult to test cleanly.

Staying in neuroscience, Eli Lilly is paying $2.8 billion upfront for AtaiBeckley, with another potential $1 billion tied to milestones. This is a serious commitment to psychedelic-adjacent psychiatry, not a small option deal dressed up with biobucks.

The lead asset, BPL-003, is an intranasal formulation of 5-MeO-DMT for treatment-resistant depression. It stimulates serotonergic signaling and produced statistically significant improvements beginning on day two in phase 2b, with effects reportedly maintained through day 57. Most patients were ready for discharge after 90 minutes.

That last number is commercially crucial. Johnson & Johnson’s esketamine product Spravato requires administration in a supervised setting, with two hours of monitoring because of dissociation, sedation and blood-pressure effects. BPL-003 is trying to fit into the clinics Spravato already created while shortening chair time. In interventional psychiatry, ninety minutes versus two hours affects how many patients a site can treat each day. Mechanism meets scheduling software.

The outstanding scientific question is how much efficacy comes from acute receptor pharmacology, how much from the intense subjective experience and how much from the therapeutic setting. Psychedelic trials are extremely difficult to blind. Patients generally know whether something dramatic happened. Phase 3 will therefore need more than a low p-value; it needs durable effects, convincing controls and a protocol that can work outside highly motivated specialty centers.

Lilly is diversifying beyond obesity and diabetes, and it has chosen a fast-acting psychiatric intervention rather than another conventional daily monoamine drug. That alone will get neuroscience investors’ attention.

A smaller but very relevant depression story comes from Draig Therapeutics, which raised a $65 million Series B. Its lead program, DT-101, is an AMPA receptor potentiator in multiple phase 2 studies for major depressive disorder.

AMPA receptors mediate most fast excitatory synaptic transmission in the brain. Positive modulation can enhance glutamatergic throughput and potentially engage plasticity-related signaling, including BDNF-associated pathways. The historical problem with AMPA potentiators is obvious: push excitation too hard and tolerability can deteriorate, with anxiety, insomnia or seizure risk hovering in the background.

Draig emphasizes DT-101’s “pulsatile” pharmacokinetic profile. In plain English, exposure rises and falls rather than maintaining continuous receptor potentiation. That may permit a plasticity-promoting signal without keeping neural networks stepped on the accelerator all day. It is a clever hypothesis. Phase 2 will tell us whether “pulsatile” is genuinely a therapeutic design feature or merely a flattering description of short drug exposure.

Now to the retina, where Johnson & Johnson has discontinued JNJ-1887 after seeing topline phase 2b data in geographic atrophy, the advanced dry form of age-related macular degeneration.

JNJ-1887 used AAV2 to express soluble CD59 after a single intravitreal injection. CD59 normally inhibits assembly of the complement membrane attack complex, so the gene therapy was intended to protect retinal cells from terminal complement-mediated injury. The 305-patient Parasol trial assessed whether it could slow enlargement of geographic-atrophy lesions over 18 months. Apparently it could not do so convincingly enough.

This is an imaging-heavy endpoint: fundus autofluorescence and related retinal imaging can map lesion area with impressive precision. But precise measurement does not rescue insufficient biology. Complement clearly contributes to geographic atrophy—approved drugs targeting C3 or C5 already slow lesion growth modestly—but it may require intervention at a different node, in a different cellular compartment or earlier in disease. A one-time gene therapy also creates a special risk-benefit problem: long-lived expression is wonderful if the construct works and harder to reverse if it does not.

GSK has suffered a different receptor-biology disappointment. It has stopped developing camlipixant for refractory chronic cough after mixed phase 3 results. GSK acquired the P2X3 antagonist through its $2 billion purchase of Bellus Health in 2023 and had forecast peak sales above $3.4 billion.

P2X3 is an ATP-gated ion channel expressed on sensory afferents, including vagal neurons involved in cough. Tissue irritation releases ATP, which can amplify sensory firing and produce a kind of peripheral hypersensitivity. The target is biologically compelling. Merck’s gefapixant, however, was hindered by taste disturbance because it also inhibited P2X2-containing receptors in taste pathways. Camlipixant was designed to be more selective and did appear much cleaner.

Unfortunately, cleaner did not mean sufficiently effective. The high dose reduced 24-hour cough frequency at week 12 in one trial, but it missed the week-24 primary endpoint in the second trial and failed multiple key secondary endpoints. GSK called the efficacy “limited” and unlikely to transform care. That is unusually direct corporate language—and probably the correct decision. A safe drug with inconsistent phase 3 efficacy is not rescued by a beautiful selectivity profile.

Finally, AdvanCell raised a huge $315 million Series D for ADVC001, a PSMA-targeted radiotherapy using lead-212 in metastatic prostate cancer. Lead-212 ultimately delivers alpha-particle radiation, which deposits dense energy over a short distance and produces difficult-to-repair double-strand DNA breaks. That contrasts with lutetium-177 therapies such as Pluvicto, whose beta particles generally travel farther and produce less densely ionizing damage.

The asset’s 10.6-hour half-life could limit prolonged radiation exposure, but it also creates a manufacturing and logistics race. AdvanCell’s answer is an automated generator, described as roughly the size of a wine crate, that produces lead-212 inside a shielded hot cell. This is where radiopharma becomes as much an operations business as a drug-discovery business. You cannot stockpile a rapidly decaying isotope and ship it whenever convenient.

That $315 million is therefore funding not just phase 3 development, but the physical system required to make the therapy reliably. Investors are betting on the molecule, the isotope and the factory simultaneously.

Taken together, today’s stories offer a useful correction to platform exuberance. Tau ASOs, serotonin psychedelics, AMPA potentiators, complement gene therapy, purinergic antagonists and alpha emitters all have coherent mechanistic stories. The winners will be the programs that translate those stories into effects patients can feel, with administration systems that clinics can actually deliver.
