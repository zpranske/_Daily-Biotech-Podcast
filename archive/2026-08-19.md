Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for August 19, 2026.

The TL;DR: Two previously undisclosed deaths in Chinese investigator-initiated gene-therapy trials have exposed a serious transparency problem around a fast route into first-in-human testing. Both involved viral-vector delivery, genome editing and severe immune reactions; one patient was a six-year-old with a nonfatal neurodevelopmental disorder. China has since tightened the rules, but delayed disclosure makes it impossible to know whether the system is learning reliably from its failures. Elsewhere in neuroscience, newly formed Slate Medicines is using Fulcrum Therapeutics’ Nasdaq listing and a $245 million financing to advance antibodies against the migraine peptides PACAP, VIP and CGRP. Evaxion is dropping a broad solid-tumor vaccine to focus on an endogenous-retrovirus vaccine for glioblastoma, while Lilly signed a frustratingly vague, mostly biobucks ion-channel discovery deal with OmniAb. Amylyx delivered today’s cleanest phase 3 result by using a GLP-1 antagonist—not an agonist—to cut dangerous post-bariatric hypoglycemia by 55%. BioMarin is making another skeletal-disease acquisition immediately after its last one failed, and AstraZeneca’s PD-1-by-CTLA-4 bispecific could not beat Keytruda in lung cancer.

We’ll begin with the two gene-therapy deaths in China, because the scientific risks are important, but the delayed disclosure is arguably even more consequential.

The patients were treated through investigator-initiated trials, or IITs. These are studies led primarily by an academic investigator or hospital rather than developed through the conventional sponsor-led investigational-new-drug pathway intended to support regulatory approval.

IITs are not inherently sloppy. They can be excellent engines for translational medicine, especially in rare diseases where a hospital may have the patients, genetic expertise and manufacturing collaborators required to test a therapeutic concept quickly. China has used this route particularly aggressively for cell and gene therapies. The attraction is speed: generate early human proof of concept without first completing every requirement associated with a registration-directed trial.

Speed, however, transfers responsibility rather than eliminating it.

In one case, a six-year-old girl with a rare but nonfatal neurodevelopmental disorder died several days after receiving an experimental base-editing therapy partly financed by her parents. In the second, a boy in a HuidaGene investigator-initiated study for Duchenne muscular dystrophy died of acute respiratory distress syndrome after receiving a CRISPR-based treatment.

Both programs reportedly involved high-dose viral-vector delivery and severe immune reactions. We should be careful not to flatten them into one mechanism because the detailed clinical records are not public. “Immune reaction” could encompass innate sensing of the vector, complement activation, inflammatory cytokine release, capsid-specific adaptive immunity, injury to heavily exposed organs such as the liver, or some combination of these.

The editing cargo creates additional layers of risk, but an editor can be molecularly precise while its delivery vehicle remains systemically inflammatory. AAV and related viral vectors are often called nonpathogenic or relatively non-immunogenic. Those adjectives are comparative, not magical. At the enormous particle numbers sometimes required to reach muscle or broadly distributed tissue, the capsid itself becomes a substantial biological exposure.

The first case raises a particularly difficult ethical issue. The disease was serious but reportedly not fatal. That shifts the acceptable risk-benefit balance. A desperate family may rationally accept enormous uncertainty, but consent is only meaningful when preclinical evidence, manufacturing quality and known risks are presented without therapeutic optimism overpowering them.

China implemented a new framework called Order 818 in May. It introduces tighter requirements for protocol design, preclinical safety, subject selection, product quality and risk management. That may improve future IITs. But the deaths occurred before the framework took effect and became public only much later.

That delay is not a cosmetic public-relations failure. Rapid disclosure allows unrelated investigators to modify doses, monitoring plans, vector designs and stopping rules. If severe adverse events remain hidden, other patients may enter trials under an obsolete understanding of risk. The industry can tolerate experimental failure. It cannot learn from failure that it is not allowed to see.

Now to migraine, where Slate Medicines is reaching Nasdaq only six months after emerging from stealth.

Slate will merge with Fulcrum Therapeutics and raise $245 million in a private placement led by Frazier Life Sciences. Fulcrum supplies the public listing after abandoning its sickle-cell drug pociredir. Slate supplies the programs that investors now want to finance.

This is a reverse merger: rather than conducting a conventional IPO, Slate moves into Fulcrum’s existing public corporate structure. Fulcrum investors receive a large cash dividend and retain about 5% of the combined company. The laboratory thesis changes. The stock-market shell survives.

Slate’s lead antibody, SLTE-1009, blocks both PACAP and VIP. PACAP stands for pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide; VIP is vasoactive intestinal peptide. They are related neuropeptides that signal through overlapping G-protein-coupled receptors and influence vasodilation, sensory transmission, autonomic activity and inflammatory signaling.

PACAP infusion can provoke migraine attacks in susceptible people, making the pathway more than a correlation. But PACAP-only antibodies have produced mixed results: Lilly discontinued one after failure, while Lundbeck recently reported encouraging phase 2b data.

Slate argues that dual blockade of PACAP and VIP will intercept more of the biology. Reasonable. Also unproven.

The migraine market already contains highly effective drugs targeting CGRP, including Aimovig, Ajovy, Emgality and Vyepti. Collectively, CGRP medicines generated about $5 billion in 2025. Yet many patients remain incomplete responders, so a new pathway could matter if it produces a visibly larger reduction in monthly migraine days.

Slate is not yet testing that question. SLTE-1009 is only preparing for phase 1 in healthy volunteers, with safety and pharmacokinetic data expected in mid-2027. A second molecule, SLTE-2100, goes even broader by combining CGRP blockade with PACAP-and-VIP blockade in one bispecific antibody.

The $245 million financing gives Slate runway into 2029. That is a remarkably well-funded antibody company for a thesis whose first internal human efficacy data remain more than a year away. Investors are paying for the team, the Chinese-licensed assets and external validation of PACAP—not yet for Slate-generated clinical proof.

Lilly’s new collaboration with OmniAb is earlier and considerably more opaque.

The deal covers an unidentified ion-channel program, with an undisclosed upfront payment and up to $370 million in research, development and commercial milestones. That means the impressive number is mostly biobucks. We do not know the target, disease, antibody format or how much cash OmniAb actually receives now.

OmniAb uses transgenic rats, mice and chickens engineered to generate antibodies with human sequences. Ion channels are difficult antibody targets because much of the protein sits inside the membrane, while the extracellular regions available for antibody binding can be small, conformationally sensitive and highly conserved among channel-family members. Purified protein may also lose the native membrane conformation that determines whether an antibody recognizes the functional channel on a cell.

This collaboration fits a broader ion-channel revival. Vertex validated Nav1.8 in pain with Journavx. Jazz just paid $820 million upfront for Actio and its KCNT1 inhibitor in developmental epilepsy. Latigo raised an enormous IPO to compete in Nav1.8 pain.

An antibody could provide exquisite channel or cell-type selectivity, but it brings a distribution constraint. Conventional antibodies generally do not cross the blood-brain barrier efficiently. Unless Lilly has a CNS delivery strategy, the most obvious opportunities are peripheral sensory neurons, immune cells or channels exposed in other accessible tissues. For now, this is an intriguing target-class signal attached to almost no program-level information.

Evaxion’s CNS-oncology program is more specific, although still preclinical.

The Danish company has discontinued EVX-03, a DNA vaccine once intended for broad solid tumors, and will concentrate resources on EVX-05, an off-the-shelf therapeutic vaccine for glioblastoma being developed with Duke University.

EVX-05 targets antigens derived from endogenous retroviruses, or ERVs. These are remnants of ancient viral integrations inherited through the germline and now embedded throughout the human genome. Most are normally silenced by DNA methylation and repressive chromatin. Cancer-associated epigenetic disruption can reactivate some ERV sequences, creating transcripts and proteins that are relatively absent from healthy adult tissues.

That potentially gives the immune system something unusual to recognize: a shared tumor-associated antigen with viral ancestry.

“Off-the-shelf” is important. A personalized neoantigen vaccine requires sequencing each patient’s tumor, predicting relevant epitopes and manufacturing an individualized product. EVX-05 instead aims to use shared ERV antigens identified across glioblastoma samples.

Glioblastoma will test every weakness in that idea. These tumors are profoundly heterogeneous, immunosuppressive and often treated with corticosteroids that further dampen T-cell responses. Antigen expression may vary among cells, allowing negative clones to escape. Activated T cells must also enter the brain tumor and remain functional within a hostile myeloid-rich microenvironment.

Duke investigator Mustafa Khasraw is expected to lead the eventual phase 1 study, while Evaxion optimizes the vaccine. The company says ending EVX-03 extends its cash runway into the second half of 2027. That tells you this is scientific prioritization under financial pressure, not a luxuriant expansion into neuro-oncology.

Amylyx, by contrast, has actual phase 3 efficacy—and a delightfully inverted use of GLP-1 pharmacology.

Avexitide is a GLP-1 receptor antagonist. Instead of mimicking GLP-1 like semaglutide or tirzepatide, it blocks the receptor.

In post-bariatric hypoglycemia, altered gastrointestinal anatomy can deliver nutrients rapidly into the intestine, producing an exaggerated incretin response and excessive insulin secretion. Blood glucose then crashes, sometimes with little warning. Patients may experience confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures or accidents while driving.

In the 78-patient Lucidity trial, once-daily avexitide reduced clinically serious hypoglycemic events by 55% relative to placebo through week 16. It met every secondary endpoint, produced no severe adverse-effect signal and did not change body weight. Diarrhea and injection-site reactions were the most common side effects.

That is a clean result in a disease with no approved treatment. Amylyx bought the asset for $35 million, and its shares rose about 45% after the readout. The company plans to file for FDA approval by year-end.

It is worth lingering on the conceptual elegance: the same hormonal pathway being amplified across obesity medicine becomes pathological after bariatric surgery and needs to be suppressed. Pathways are not good or bad. Context and direction matter.

BioMarin is spending $275 million upfront, plus up to $215 million in milestones, to acquire Alesta Therapeutics and its oral hypophosphatasia drug ALE1.

Hypophosphatasia is caused by deficient tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase. One consequence is accumulation of inorganic pyrophosphate, or PPi, which inhibits hydroxyapatite crystal growth and impairs bone mineralization. ALE1 targets an undisclosed regulator of PPi and is intended to reduce that excess.

This is the mirror image of ENPP1 deficiency, where insufficient PPi contributes to pathological calcification. BioMarin recently terminated an ENPP1 enzyme replacement acquired in its $270 million purchase of Inozyme. Now it is paying almost the same upfront amount for a disease in which PPi moves in the opposite pathological direction.

ALE1 could compete with AstraZeneca’s Strensiq, an enzyme replacement injected three or six times per week. An oral small molecule would be much more convenient, particularly for underserved adults. But ALE1 is only in phase 1/2, and BioMarin is buying the company before knowing whether chronic PPi modulation translates into stronger bone, fewer fractures or improved physical function.

No Alesta employees will transfer to BioMarin; the non-ALE1 assets and existing staff are being spun into a separate company. For job-market purposes, this is an asset acquisition, not a  team acquisition.

Finally, AstraZeneca has stopped an 895-patient phase 3 lung-cancer trial after its PD-1-by-CTLA-4 bispecific volrustomig was judged unlikely to beat Keytruda plus chemotherapy on either progression-free or overall survival.

The trial specifically enrolled patients with low or absent PD-L1 expression, where checkpoint therapy often works less well. Volrustomig was designed to concentrate CTLA-4 inhibition on PD-1-positive T cells, potentially retaining dual-checkpoint efficacy while reducing the toxicity of administering separate antibodies.

Safety apparently behaved as expected. Efficacy did not.

That is a useful warning for the entire bispecific field. Physically joining two checkpoint binders can alter avidity and cellular localization, but it cannot guarantee that the chosen immune population is the limiting factor in a resistant tumor. AstraZeneca continues phase 3 studies in cervical, head-and-neck and mesothelioma cancers, where the immunobiology may differ.

Evaxion drops one vaccine to preserve another. EMD Serono shrinks internal research while shopping outside.
