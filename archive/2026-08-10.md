The TL;DR: Ensoma is laying off employees for the second time in nine months and pausing its preclinical pipeline to preserve one clinical gene therapy for X-linked chronic granulomatous disease. Scientifically, EN-374 is an ambitious attempt to repair the antimicrobial machinery of blood-forming stem cells. Financially, it is now a one-asset company that needs more money. The healthier side of the market is visible in two IPO updates: neuroscience company Latigo and oncology biotech BlossomHill both raised at least 30% more than expected, with BlossomHill securing $150 million. BioMarin, however, has killed the enzyme replacement therapy acquired through its $270 million purchase of Inozyme less than a year ago. The market is funding differentiated clinical programs. Platforms without near-term data are still being squeezed.

Let’s start with Ensoma, because it combines advanced gene delivery, immunology and the increasingly familiar sight of a biotech compressing itself around one surviving clinical asset.

The Boston company has initiated another round of layoffs after already cutting 37 employees—about half its workforce—last November. Ensoma is also temporarily pausing its preclinical programs, including a solid-tumor immuno-oncology candidate. The remaining organization will concentrate on EN-374, now in a phase 1/2 trial for X-linked chronic granulomatous disease, or X-CGD.

X-CGD is caused by pathogenic variants in CYBB, an X-chromosome gene encoding gp91-phox, also called NOX2. That protein is the catalytic core of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase complex.

When neutrophils and macrophages engulf a bacterium or fungus, they do not rely only on lysosomal enzymes. They also assemble NADPH oxidase across the phagosomal membrane, transfer electrons to oxygen and generate reactive oxygen species in what is called the respiratory burst. In X-CGD, that burst is defective. Phagocytes can ingest pathogens but struggle to kill particular catalase-positive bacteria and fungi, leaving patients vulnerable to recurrent, sometimes life-threatening infections and inflammatory granulomas.

EN-374 is designed to deliver a functional CYBB copy into hematopoietic stem cells using Ensoma’s virus-like vector. The attraction of targeting blood-forming stem cells is durable lineage correction: repair an appropriate fraction of the long-lived progenitors, and their descendants could continually produce functional myeloid cells.

That is a more ambitious proposition than temporarily supplying an antimicrobial protein. It is an attempt to rebuild a cellular function across the blood system.

The crucial details will be the percentage of corrected stem cells, the durability of CYBB expression and whether newly generated neutrophils recover a meaningful oxidative burst. Safety matters just as much. Gene delivery into self-renewing hematopoietic cells carries a high bar because any problematic genomic interaction or aberrant expression could persist for years.

Ensoma debuted in 2021 with $70 million and a Takeda collaboration covering as many as five rare-disease targets. It then raised another $53 million shortly before last year’s layoffs. That history gives today’s restructuring its subtext: this is no longer a broad-platform growth story. The company needs EN-374 to clinically validate both the product and the delivery system.

Management says it intends to restart the solid-tumor program after raising more capital. Treat that as an aspiration rather than a funded plan. Investors rarely finance a paused preclinical oncology portfolio simply because the slides remain attractive. Convincing human data from EN-374 would help enormously.

For scientists assessing jobs there, the risk is concentrated. Clinical development, biomarker assays, hematopoietic-cell biology and gene-therapy manufacturing remain essential. Broad exploratory discovery has just been declared nonessential for now.

At the opposite end of the financing mood, Latigo Biotherapeutics and BlossomHill Therapeutics both exceeded their IPO expectations by at least 30%.

Latigo is the neuroscience name to remember. We discussed its proposed IPO last week, when it was targeting roughly $247 million to advance LTG-001, a Nav1.8 inhibitor entering phase 3 for acute pain. The completed offering was substantially oversubscribed.

That demand is notable because Latigo is challenging an approved incumbent, Vertex’s Journavx. Nav1.8 is a voltage-gated sodium channel enriched in peripheral nociceptors, and inhibiting it can reduce pain-fiber excitability without directly engaging opioid receptors or broadly suppressing the brain.

The biology is validated. Differentiation is not.

Latigo has suggested LTG-001 could offer stronger analgesia, faster onset or fewer prescribing limitations than Journavx. Investors have now given it enough capital to test that claim in large studies and prepare for commercialization. In biotech-speak, “commercial readiness” means building the unglamorous machinery needed to sell a drug: reliable manufacturing, regulatory systems, medical affairs, payer negotiations and eventually a sales organization.

For the job market, this makes Latigo one of the better-capitalized neuroscience companies currently transitioning from development shop to late-stage operating company. Expect the hiring center of gravity to favor clinical operations, safety, regulatory science and launch preparation rather than a huge expansion in ion-channel discovery.

BlossomHill’s final numbers are more concrete. The oncology biotech raised $150 million by selling 9.375 million shares at $16 each, versus an earlier plan to raise about $111.7 million. Underwriters can purchase another 1.4 million shares, potentially adding $22.5 million.

About $70 million will fund BH-30643, its macrocyclic EGFR inhibitor for non-small cell lung cancer. Another $20 million goes toward a CLK inhibitor in acute myeloid leukemia, while $5 million supports a preclinical pan-KRAS inhibitor.

We covered the underlying EGFR strategy last week, so the new information is investor appetite. The offering was not merely completed; it was enlarged. BlossomHill is betting that a structurally distinct macrocycle can inhibit tumors carrying EGFR C797S, a resistance mutation that disrupts the covalent binding used by third-generation inhibitors such as AstraZeneca’s Tagrisso.

Founder Jean Cui has acknowledged that 35 to 40 EGFR programs were already in the clinic when the project began. Her answer is that many are incremental analogs, whereas BlossomHill started from a different chemical architecture. Fair enough. Medicinal chemistry can absolutely escape a resistance mechanism through a new binding mode. But “novel structure” earns the experiment, not the market. BH-30643 still needs strong response rates, durability, tolerability and preferably brain exposure in patients with CNS metastases.

The IPO results from Latigo and BlossomHill continue the reopening we have seen with Scribe, Apnimed, Braveheart and Attovia. Public investors are buying focused clinical stories again. They are not indiscriminately funding every platform with a large target list and a poetic diagram.

BioMarin has provided the harsher counterexample by terminating BMN 401 in every indication.

BMN 401 was the central asset in BioMarin’s $270 million acquisition of Inozyme Pharma, completed in July 2025. Less than a year later, the therapy missed one of two co-primary endpoints in a phase 3 study of ENPP1 deficiency. BioMarin reviewed the data for several months and has now stopped development, including work in ABCC6 deficiency and end-stage kidney disease.

ENPP1 is an extracellular enzyme involved in regulating pyrophosphate and phosphate balance. Pyrophosphate normally inhibits inappropriate mineral deposition. In ENPP1 deficiency, low pyrophosphate can cause pathological calcification, including severe vascular and soft-tissue mineralization, while later disturbances in phosphate handling can contribute to skeletal disease.

BMN 401 was a subcutaneously administered enzyme replacement intended to restore that missing extracellular activity. It is a coherent mechanism in a genetically defined disease. But a late-stage therapy has to improve the prespecified clinical outcomes, not simply normalize part of the biochemical pathway.

The acquisition accounting is particularly painful because BMN 401 represented substantially all the value of the assets BioMarin bought. This is what people mean when they say an acquisition was “written off”: the company did not literally set $270 million on fire in a laboratory sink, but the purchased asset is no longer expected to generate the value used to justify the deal.

BioMarin can absorb the hit better than a small biotech because its commercial revenue grew 20% in the second quarter. Still, the episode shows how quickly a seemingly strategic rare-disease acquisition can collapse when pivotal data arrive. Genetic clarity lowers biological ambiguity. It does not guarantee that the chosen replacement molecule, dose or endpoint will work.

Novo Nordisk is dealing with a less terminal but more commercially uncomfortable result. CagriSema, its combination of semaglutide and the amylin analog cagrilintide, missed one phase 3 endpoint in people with type 2 diabetes.

Amylin is co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic beta cells and contributes to satiety, slower gastric emptying and suppression of glucagon after meals. Pairing an amylin analog with GLP-1 agonism is therefore a rational way to attack appetite and glucose regulation through overlapping but nonidentical pathways.

The problem is that obesity has become an arms race. “Really good” is now investor language for “possibly not best in class.” Novo still believes CagriSema can be a competitive diabetes product, while oral Wegovy is reportedly launching strongly across Europe and has helped the company raise its full-year guidance. Oral delivery could matter enormously for patients who dislike injections, provided efficacy, gastrointestinal tolerability, dosing instructions and manufacturing economics remain competitive.
