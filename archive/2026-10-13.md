Good morning. Here’s your Fierce Biotech update for October 13, 2026.

The TL;DR: Tortugas Neuroscience has licensed its way into a surprisingly strong schizophrenia dataset. In a 363-patient Chinese phase 2b trial, the dopamine-and-serotonin modulator obuprazine improved PANSS scores by as much as 16.7 points over placebo—far larger than the modest effects we have recently seen from several novel antipsychotic mechanisms. The result is impressive, although the dose response is not tidy and the active comparison with aripiprazole remains underexplained. In advanced modalities, Roche is paying up to $100 million near term for an adipose-targeted ALK7 RNAi program, making extrahepatic delivery the centerpiece of another major obesity deal. Aspen Neuroscience is preparing an IPO around patient-specific dopamine-neuron replacement for Parkinson’s disease. Pfizer has joined the emerging SEC61 protein-biogenesis field, while Nurix’s decision to kill one clinically active BTK degrader shows that target degradation is not automatically therapeutic differentiation. And in ophthalmology, Roche’s IL-6 antibody is heading toward an FDA decision while Merck’s expensive retinal-barrier drug now looks commercially troubled.

Let’s begin with Tortugas, because a 16.7-point placebo-adjusted PANSS effect in schizophrenia deserves attention.

Tortugas Neuroscience launched only this spring with $106 million and a pipeline assembled from Chinese and Japanese licensors. The Framingham, Massachusetts-based company was founded by Sage Therapeutics veterans, so this is an asset-centric CNS startup rather than a discovery platform patiently growing molecules from first principles.

Its lead drug, obuprazine, or TRTL-107, came from China’s Hansoh Pharma. In a phase 2b study, 363 adults hospitalized recently for acute schizophrenia received one of five obuprazine dose regimens, aripiprazole or placebo for six weeks.

Every obuprazine dose significantly improved the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, or PANSS, relative to placebo. PANSS is a clinician-rated instrument covering positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, negative symptoms such as social withdrawal and reduced emotional expression, and broader psychopathology.

The largest placebo-adjusted reduction was 16.7 points in the 1.5-to-3-milligram group. The higher 4.5-to-6 and 6-to-9-milligram groups produced reductions of 13.2 and 14.7 points.

Those are large effects. For comparison, MapLight’s muscarinic agonist recently beat placebo by 4.5 PANSS points at its successful twice-daily dose. Cross-trial comparisons are treacherous—placebo behavior, baseline severity, hospitalization and geographic setting can all change the apparent drug effect—but 16.7 points is not a subtle signal.

Obuprazine is a partial agonist at dopamine D2 and D3 receptors, with additional serotonergic activity including potent 5-HT2A antagonism and reported modulation of 5-HT1A receptors. A partial agonist activates a receptor, but less strongly than a full agonist. In a dopamine-rich circuit, it can compete with dopamine and reduce net signaling. In a dopamine-poor circuit, it can preserve some receptor activity rather than shutting the pathway off completely.

That is broadly the logic behind aripiprazole. Blocking 5-HT2A can further influence cortical dopamine release and may reduce some movement-related adverse effects. D3 receptors are concentrated partly in limbic circuitry and have attracted interest around motivation, cognition and negative symptoms, although clinical claims about D3 selectivity routinely outrun what trials actually demonstrate.

So obuprazine is not a new mechanistic class like muscarinic agonism. It is a potentially better-tuned implementation of established dopamine-serotonin pharmacology.

Several questions remain. The best result occurred at the lowest reported range, so the dose response is not monotonic. That can happen if higher exposure adds off-target effects or if the useful receptor-occupancy window is narrow, but it can also arise through sampling variation. Tortugas also emphasized higher responder rates than both placebo and aripiprazole at day 14, suggesting rapid onset, yet it did not provide the full aripiprazole comparison. If you include an active comparator, people will reasonably ask whether the drug actually beat it and by how much.

The 2.1% discontinuation rate was reassuringly low, even slightly below placebo. We still need weight, metabolic measurements, prolactin, movement symptoms, sedation and akathisia. A favorable overall discontinuation rate can conceal a tolerability profile that becomes important during years of maintenance treatment.

Tortugas now wants FDA authorization for a U.S. trial. The insider interpretation is that the company has obtained unusually strong proof of concept cheaply and quickly through a Chinese-originated asset. The next study must show that the magnitude travels across healthcare settings and populations. If it does, an apparently conventional antipsychotic may become more commercially important than several pharmacologically fashionable competitors.

Staying in CNS, Aspen Neuroscience is preparing an IPO around a much more radical proposition: replacing the dopaminergic neurons lost in Parkinson’s disease.

Aspen’s sasineprocel begins with a patient’s own skin cells. Those cells are reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells and differentiated into dopaminergic-neuron precursors, which are then implanted into the brain.

Parkinson’s motor symptoms emerge partly because dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra degenerate, reducing dopamine delivery to the striatum. Levodopa temporarily restores dopamine synthesis, but it does not replace the neurons or recreate physiological release patterns. A transplanted cell could, in principle, survive, mature, extend processes and provide more continuous dopamine.

Aspen reported improvements in motor function, symptom control and quality of life one year after low- or high-dose treatment in phase 1/2a. Some patients reduced their levodopa use.

That is encouraging, not yet definitive evidence that transplanted cells are functionally integrated. Early cell-therapy studies are small and often lack a credible sham-surgery control. Expectations, changes in medication and selection of relatively healthy surgical candidates can influence outcomes. Reduced levodopa use is supportive, but imaging of graft survival and dopamine synthesis, standardized OFF-medication testing and durable controlled outcomes will be much more persuasive.

Autologous manufacturing avoids donor-cell immune incompatibility, but each patient becomes a separate production campaign. Skin-cell collection, reprogramming, differentiation, quality control and release testing must all remain reproducible. Residual pluripotent cells are a tumor risk, while incompletely specified neural populations could behave unpredictably.

Aspen had $72.4 million in June and 120 employees. IPO proceeds would support the existing study and a phase 3 trial that the company intends to begin before phase 1/2a is finished. That is aggressive. It may preserve speed, but it risks locking the pivotal design before the early dataset has fully matured.

Aspen is also developing autologous microglial replacement for leukodystrophies. That is scientifically fascinating: replace dysfunctional CNS-resident immune cells with patient-derived, genetically corrected or otherwise functional cells. It is also earlier and should not be mistaken for a second clinically validated platform.

For the job market, Aspen is relevant to stem-cell differentiation, single-cell identity profiling, microscopy, surgical delivery and cell-manufacturing analytics. An IPO would mainly finance clinical and manufacturing execution, not unlimited exploratory neurobiology.

Now to Roche’s new obesity deal with China’s Rona Therapeutics.

Roche is committing up to $100 million in upfront and near-term payments for an undisclosed RNAi candidate against ALK7, with another $1.06 billion in longer-term milestones. Unlike deals where even the initial payment is hidden, the near-term commitment here is meaningful. The billion-dollar remainder is still contingent biobucks.

ALK7 is a TGF-beta-family receptor encoded by ACVR1C and expressed preferentially in adipose tissue. Its signaling helps regulate adipocyte metabolism, including lipid storage and responsiveness to lipolytic signals. Human genetic variants that reduce ALK7 function have been associated with lower waist-to-hip ratios, while disrupting the pathway makes mice resistant to obesity.

The therapeutic idea is not primarily to suppress appetite. It is to make adipose tissue less inclined to retain pathological lipid stores, particularly visceral fat, while potentially preserving lean tissue.

That could complement GLP-1 drugs. Incretins reduce food intake very effectively, but weight loss includes both fat and lean mass. An adipose-directed RNAi drug might improve where weight is lost and how durable that loss becomes after appetite-directed therapy.

Arrowhead reported a 14.1% placebo-adjusted reduction in visceral fat eight weeks after one dose of its ALK7 candidate. Alnylam, GSK and Novo are also entering the race.

For an oligonucleotide scientist, however, the most important phrase is “extrahepatic delivery platform.” Liver delivery is comparatively mature because GalNAc conjugates can exploit the hepatocyte asialoglycoprotein receptor. Adipocytes do not offer an equally established portal.

Rona must get its RNAi construct out of circulation, across adipose vasculature and extracellular matrix, into the relevant adipocyte population, and then out of endosomes so the guide strand can enter the RNA-induced silencing complex. It also needs to avoid excessive liver and immune-cell exposure.

Roche has not disclosed the candidate, chemistry, ligand, stage or quantitative adipocyte distribution. So this is a substantial bet on delivery, not public validation that delivery has been solved. With Roche’s muscle-preservation antibody recently discontinued and questions hanging over other obesity assets, ALK7 gives the company another way to pursue body composition rather than competing only on total kilograms lost.

Protein control is also heating up at the opposite end of molecular biology: before a protein has fully formed.

Pfizer has signed a research collaboration with Enodia Therapeutics to discover selective SEC61 inhibitors. The total package could exceed $500 million, but the upfront is undisclosed. This is therefore a platform endorsement with indeterminate immediate financial weight.

SEC61 is the channel through which many secreted and membrane proteins enter the endoplasmic reticulum while they are being translated. Once inside that pathway, proteins can fold, acquire glycans, form disulfide bonds and travel toward the cell surface or extracellular space.

Enodia wants small molecules that selectively block a particular nascent protein from passing through SEC61, causing it to be degraded before it reaches its destination. It is like intercepting a receptor or cytokine on the factory line rather than neutralizing it after delivery.

This follows Lilly’s expanded Gate Bioscience collaboration last week. Enodia’s scientific roots include work on mycolactone, a natural SEC61 inhibitor produced by the bacterium responsible for Buruli ulcer. Mycolactone demonstrates the power—and danger—of inhibiting this channel broadly. Safe drugs must discriminate among protein substrates while leaving essential secretory traffic intact.

One Enodia program selectively blocks PD-1 expression, while the company has also discussed inflammatory disease and AL amyloidosis. In AL amyloidosis, abnormal plasma cells produce immunoglobulin light chains that misfold and damage organs. Selectively preventing those chains from entering the secretory pathway could attack production upstream, although suppressing normal immunoglobulin secretion would be an obvious risk.

The emerging competition suggests SEC61 has become a genuine industry target class. Human selectivity data are still sparse.

Nurix supplied a useful warning from a different protein-degradation architecture. It has discontinued zelebrudomide, an oral degrader of BTK and the cereblon-dependent substrates IKZF1 and IKZF3, after reviewing completed phase 1 enrollment.

Earlier studies showed robust, sustained target degradation. The drug still did not earn another trial.

That distinction is essential. Degrading the intended protein proves pharmacology. It does not prove that the clinical effect is strong enough, safe enough or differentiated from ordinary inhibition.

Nurix is concentrating instead on bexobrutideg, the BTK degrader Roche licensed for $700 million upfront and has already moved into phase 3 against Lilly’s Jaypirca. Bexobrutideg may offer cleaner or deeper BTK degradation without the additional IKZF effects. The portfolio decision also reflects competitive economics: once Roche supplied $700 million and pivotal momentum behind one molecule, a weaker wholly owned cousin became difficult to justify.

Finally, retinal imaging is doing real interpretive work in two ophthalmology programs moving in opposite directions.

Roche’s intravitreal IL-6 inhibitor vamikibart is under FDA review for uveitic macular edema, with a July 2027 decision date. Uveitis is inflammation inside the eye. When inflammatory signaling makes retinal vessels leaky, fluid accumulates around the macula and disrupts central vision.

One of Roche’s two nominally identical phase 3 trials hit its primary endpoint and the other missed. At week 52, both showed sustained numerical improvements in vision and macular edema, and roughly two-thirds of eligible patients needed no retreatment after week 16.

Optical coherence tomography is especially useful here because it resolves retinal layers and fluid-filled spaces at near-cellular anatomical scale. The clinically persuasive package is not merely a thinner retina or fewer cystic spaces. It is OCT evidence of edema resolution aligned with improved visual acuity and reduced injection burden.

Merck’s remigromig has the opposite problem. The trispecific norrin-Wnt agonist produced mean visual gains of 8.7 to 9.1 letters in diabetic macular edema, versus 11.8 letters with Lucentis. It was statistically noninferior, but no secondary endpoint showed superiority.

More concerningly, proliferative diabetic retinopathy occurred in approximately 6% to 7% of remigromig patients, versus 0.9% on Lucentis. Merck argues that its drug repairs the blood-retinal barrier without directly suppressing VEGF-driven pathological blood-vessel growth. Mechanistically, that explanation may be correct. Clinically, it may be the problem.

Retina specialists already have anti-VEGF drugs that improve edema while restraining neovascularization. A new therapy that restores barrier biology but allows more proliferative disease needs a dramatic advantage elsewhere. Remigromig has not shown one, despite Merck paying $1.3 billion upfront for EyeBio.

This week’s Nobel Prize for optogenetics offers a more uplifting retinal coda. Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel were recognized for turning light-sensitive microbial proteins into tools for controlling electrically active cells. In neuroscience laboratories, optogenetics lets investigators activate defined neuronal populations and connect cell identity to circuit function.

The retina may become its most direct therapeutic application. When photoreceptors have degenerated but downstream retinal neurons survive, a gene therapy can install light-sensitive channels in those remaining cells. The resulting vision is unlikely to resemble normal photoreception; microbial opsins differ in sensitivity, kinetics and signaling architecture. But converting a previously blind retinal circuit into one that can detect visual information would be a remarkable translation from algae to human neuroprosthesis.

Today’s CNS stories span that entire translational distance. Tortugas has a conventional small molecule with unusually strong patient data. Aspen is attempting to manufacture new dopamine neurons one patient at a time. Optogenetic therapies propose giving surviving retinal neurons an entirely new sensory capability.

Elegant biology opens the possibility. Controlled trials, cell identity, delivery and functional measurements decide whether the possibility becomes medicine.

And that’s the roundup for today.
