The TL;DR: Orexin pharmacology is attempting its first serious move beyond sleep medicine. Alkermes’ orexin receptor agonist produced large two-week improvements in a 50-person adult ADHD study, but the trial was not powered for efficacy and—crucially—the company has not disclosed the placebo response. That is an intriguing signal, not yet proof that orexin is the “GLP-1 of neuroscience.” Meanwhile, Xenon has paused enrollment in phase 3 depression studies of its Kv7 potassium-channel opener after confusion, ataxia and a small number of psychosis events, although existing patients remain on treatment and the company still plans an early readout. Beacon Therapeutics scored a pivotal retinal gene-therapy win by choosing a concrete low-light visual endpoint after larger competitors failed. And Telix is spending $1.65 billion upfront to buy ITM, isotope manufacturing and a radioligand therapy recently rejected over manufacturing deficiencies.

We’ll start with orexin, because today’s ADHD data provide the first clinical hint that this system may do more than repair the unstable sleep-wake boundary of narcolepsy.

Alkermes tested ALKS 7290 in 50 adults with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Patients received placebo, 20 milligrams or 50 milligrams for two weeks. On a 54-point investigator-rated ADHD symptom scale, scores fell by an average of 14 points at the lower dose and 19 points at the higher dose.

On the seven-point Clinical Global Impressions severity scale, the treatment groups moved from baseline averages of four or five—roughly moderately to markedly ill—to averages of one or two, meaning normal to minimally ill.

Those are large changes. They are also incomplete data.

We do not have the corresponding placebo reductions. The study was small, lasted only two weeks and was explicitly not designed to statistically establish efficacy. Alkermes has therefore shown a dose-ordered signal that looks exciting in isolation, but it has not yet shown us the comparison required to interpret it. Adult ADHD trials can generate meaningful placebo improvement through expectancy, increased structure and repeated investigator contact. If placebo fell by two points, the result is spectacular. If placebo fell by ten, the interpretation changes considerably.

The biology is nevertheless compelling. Orexin—also called hypocretin—is produced by a relatively small population of hypothalamic neurons. Those neurons project widely and recruit arousal systems using norepinephrine, histamine, serotonin, dopamine and acetylcholine. Orexin is not merely caffeine generated by the brain. It helps stabilize wakefulness and aligns alertness with motivation, metabolic state and environmental salience.

Takeda’s recently approved orexin-2-receptor agonist Orzeyful validates the approach in narcolepsy type 1, where orexin-producing neurons have largely been lost. That is close to replacement pharmacology: restore signaling downstream of a missing neuronal population.

ADHD is a tougher conceptual test. People with ADHD do not generally lack orexin neurons. Alkermes is instead asking whether increasing orexin signaling can improve sustained attention and executive control in a brain whose arousal system is intact but perhaps insufficiently regulated for particular tasks.

That distinction matters. Replacing a missing signal in narcolepsy is biologically cleaner than amplifying an existing one in ADHD. Too little arousal impairs attention, but too much can produce anxiety, insomnia, behavioral rigidity or autonomic effects. Like many neuromodulatory systems, the useful range may resemble an inverted U rather than a simple “more is better” line.

The reported adverse events included dizziness, constipation, changes in sustained attention and more frequent or urgent urination. Urinary frequency is emerging as an orexin-agonist class effect. The phrase “changes in sustained attention” also deserves clarification when sustained attention is one of the functions the drug is supposed to improve.

Alkermes is already enrolling a four-week phase 2 study testing three dosing strategies, followed eventually by pediatric trials if adult safety remains acceptable. ADHD could indeed be a company-changing market. But this is exactly when commercial excitement tends to outrun the dataset. The next trial needs placebo-adjusted effect sizes, sleep measurements, cardiovascular monitoring and evidence that improved investigator scores translate into better daily function—not merely greater wakefulness.

There is also a useful contrast with Xenon Pharmaceuticals, where a different excitability-modulating mechanism has encountered neuropsychiatric trouble.

Xenon voluntarily paused new enrollment in two phase 3 studies of azetukalner: X-Nova2 in major depressive disorder and X-Ceed in bipolar depression. Existing participants may continue treatment, so this is not a complete clinical hold. The company is examining dosing changes after seeing confusion, aphasia, ataxia and, in a very small number of patients, adverse events categorized under the broad term “psychosis.”

Azetukalner opens Kv7 potassium channels, particularly neuronal Kv7.2 and Kv7.3 channels. These channels generate the M-current, a slowly activating potassium conductance that restrains repetitive neuronal firing. Think of it as a stabilizing electrical brake: as a neuron depolarizes, potassium exits through Kv7 channels, making additional action potentials harder to generate.

That is useful in epilepsy, and Xenon has just filed for FDA approval in focal seizures. The company has accumulated more than 1,500 patient-years of exposure and says the neuropsychiatric events have been brief, reversible and without lasting consequences.

But using the same electrical brake in depression is more speculative. Neural-network stability is not automatically mood improvement. Excessive channel opening in the wrong circuits, or overly high peak exposure, could plausibly interfere with language, coordination and cognition. Whether it explains psychosis is much less clear. Psychosis is not a single mechanism, and the reported category may include heterogeneous events.

The major-depression study had enrolled around 360 patients—about 80% of target—when Xenon stopped recruitment. The company believes that is enough to detect a clinically meaningful effect and plans topline data in the first quarter of 2027. That creates an awkward situation: the trial may read out before the company fully redesigns dosing.

Investors cut Xenon’s shares by 26%, and analysts lowered the estimated probability of success in depression from 60% to 50%. That reaction reflects both safety and an already mixed phase 2 efficacy package. The epilepsy filing remains intact, but prescribers and regulators will now scrutinize whether psychiatric adverse events are dose-related, indication-specific or intrinsic to the molecule.

So orexin agonism is moving from sleep into attention, while Kv7 activation is trying to move from seizures into mood. Both are examples of validated neurobiology being stretched into broader populations. That is where the commercial opportunity grows—and where the mechanistic certainty begins to thin.

Beacon Therapeutics has delivered a cleaner success in X-linked retinitis pigmentosa.

Its AAV gene therapy, laruparetigene zovaparvovec, or laru-zova, met the primary endpoint in the 85-patient phase 2/3 Vista trial. Twelve months after treatment, 24.1% of low-dose recipients and 31% of high-dose recipients could read at least 15 additional letters on an eye chart under low-light conditions. None of the control patients met that responder definition.

Fifteen letters corresponds to roughly three lines on a standard eye chart. That is not a subtle molecular biomarker. It is a noticeable improvement in low-light visual acuity.

X-linked retinitis pigmentosa is commonly caused by pathogenic variants in RPGR, a gene important to photoreceptor cilia and trafficking. Photoreceptors continually transport proteins and membrane components between their inner and outer segments. Disrupt that system, and rods and cones progressively deteriorate, often beginning with night blindness and constricted peripheral vision before advancing toward severe visual loss.

Beacon now plans a rolling FDA submission before year-end.

The company’s strategic insight was partly about measurement. Earlier Biogen and Johnson & Johnson programs failed trials using retinal sensitivity or navigation-based endpoints. Beacon selected a low-light acuity measure that appears better aligned with how this particular disease affects patients and how much function the therapy could realistically restore.

That does not mean the earlier therapies were biologically inactive. It means a clinical endpoint can determine whether useful biology becomes an approvable product.

For imaging and retinal-biomarker scientists, the full dataset should be especially interesting.
