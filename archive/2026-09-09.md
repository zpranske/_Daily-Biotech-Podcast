Good morning. Here is your Fierce Biotech update for September 9, 2026.

The TL;DR: Novartis’ $12 billion acquisition of Avidity Biosciences has suffered a major blow. Its antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate for myotonic dystrophy type 1 failed phase 3, despite encouraging activity on secondary measures. That does not invalidate targeted RNA delivery into muscle, but it badly weakens the commercial centerpiece of the deal. In pediatric neuro-oncology, Seattle Children’s spinout BrainChild Bio raised a substantial $116 million Series A to fund a pivotal study of locally delivered CAR-T cells in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma. Evommune, meanwhile, is taking an MRGPRX2 antagonist into migraine after failures in both eczema and urticaria—a scientifically intriguing but increasingly speculative neuroimmune pivot. Outside CNS, Bristol Myers Squibb’s post-BCMA myeloma CAR-T hit its pivotal endpoint, Inhibrx reported unusually strong OX40-agonist data in HPV-positive head-and-neck cancer, and Pharvaris achieved an 83% reduction in hereditary-angioedema attacks with an oral bradykinin antagonist. The recurring question is whether elegant delivery platforms and mechanistic biomarkers translate into outcomes patients can actually perceive.

Novartis and Avidity are the obvious place to begin, because this is a clinically important neuromuscular failure attached to a very large acquisition.

The phase 3 Harbor trial tested del-desiran, an antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate, in 159 people with myotonic dystrophy type 1, or DM1. After seven infusions given every eight weeks, the drug was statistically no better than placebo on video hand-opening time.

Video hand-opening time measures myotonia—the delayed relaxation that occurs after a voluntary muscle contraction. A patient may grip an object or close a hand normally, then be unable to release promptly. It is an imperfect summary of a multisystem disease, but it is concrete, quantifiable and directly connected to a symptom patients experience.

The trial missed. Novartis says secondary endpoints and exploratory analyses showed clinical activity, and it will discuss the complete dataset with regulators. That is worth examining, but the hierarchy matters: a plausible secondary measure does not erase a failed primary endpoint.

The molecular biology remains compelling. DM1 is usually caused by expansion of CTG repeats in the DMPK gene. The resulting RNA contains abnormally long CUG-repeat tracts that accumulate in nuclear foci and sequester muscleblind-like, or MBNL, RNA-binding proteins. MBNL proteins normally regulate alternative splicing. Trap them on mutant RNA, and adult muscle cells revert toward inappropriate fetal splicing patterns across many transcripts.

So this is not primarily a toxic-protein disease. The expanded RNA itself acts as a molecular sponge, deranging RNA processing across the cell.

Del-desiran uses an antibody-based delivery component to improve oligonucleotide uptake into muscle and an RNA-silencing cargo intended to reduce DMPK transcripts. That architecture was central to Avidity’s appeal: antibodies can provide a cell-surface address, while an oligonucleotide performs sequence-specific silencing after internalization and endosomal escape.

The failed trial does not prove that antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates cannot deliver RNA drugs into muscle. It could reflect insufficient silencing in the most relevant fibers, incomplete endosomal escape, disease irreversibility, the selected dose or simply a clinical endpoint that did not capture the drug’s strongest effects.

But Novartis paid $12 billion for Avidity, and outside forecasts had placed peak del-desiran sales as high as $6 billion. This was not a decorative pipeline option. It was the largest commercial opportunity used to justify the acquisition.

Novartis still has Avidity-originated programs in Duchenne muscular dystrophy and facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy. The FSHD program targets DUX4 biology and recently produced positive early-stage data. Yet that program now carries more pressure, especially after Ultragenyx’s Angelman ASO failure last week reminded everyone that restoring or suppressing the correct RNA does not guarantee functional recovery.

For job seekers, muscle delivery, oligonucleotide pharmacology and translational biomarker work remain strategically relevant at Novartis. Broad platform enthusiasm, however, will become more conditional. Expect every program to be interrogated for evidence that molecular correction is producing functional change—not merely attractive biopsy data.

Our second neuroscience story is considerably more hopeful. BrainChild Bio has raised a $116 million Series A to develop CAR-T therapies for pediatric brain tumors.

The company was spun out of Seattle Children’s Hospital in 2023 by pediatric oncologist Michael Jensen. The financing comes from Seattle Children’s itself and WRF Capital, the Washington Research Foundation’s investment arm. That is a concentrated and mission-driven syndicate, which reflects a difficult reality: pediatric brain cancers are scientifically urgent but often commercially neglected because the patient populations are small.

BrainChild’s lead program, BCB-276, is entering a pivotal phase 2 study in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma, or DIPG, now commonly classified within diffuse midline glioma.

DIPG grows within the pons, a brainstem structure containing pathways essential for breathing, movement, swallowing and arousal. Its location makes surgical resection effectively impossible. Radiation may temporarily slow progression, but typically adds only months. There are roughly 250 to 300 new U.S. cases per year, and no approved drug specifically for the disease.

BrainChild removes a patient’s T cells, genetically engineers them to recognize tumor-associated targets and then administers them directly into the central nervous system. The local route is important. Systemically infused cells must cross vascular barriers, find a relatively small brain lesion and avoid becoming trapped or exhausted elsewhere. Locoregional delivery places the therapy closer to the tumor and may permit repeat dosing.

That last feature distinguishes BrainChild’s strategy from conventional blood-cancer CAR-T, which is usually administered once. BrainChild envisions multiple doses, potentially allowing clinicians to adjust treatment as the tumor and immune response evolve.

This is still an exceptionally difficult environment. Brain tumors can vary target expression from cell to cell. Activated T cells face immunosuppressive myeloid cells, inhibitory ligands and metabolic stress. In the brainstem, inflammatory swelling also has very little room to be benign. Even a productive immune response can become dangerous when it occurs beside circuitry required for ventilation.

The $116 million should carry the pivotal DIPG study to completion and support a phase 1 glioblastoma program beginning next year. For scientists, BrainChild is directly relevant to cell engineering, neuro-oncology, flow cytometry, spatial tissue analysis and longitudinal immune monitoring. It is also a rare case where a large Series A is funding a pivotal trial rather than a collection of preclinical aspirations.

Evommune has a much less comfortable neuroimmune story. Its MRGPRX2 antagonist EVO756 failed phase 2 in atopic dermatitis after already failing in chronic urticaria.

In 121 adults with moderate-to-severe eczema, the drug missed its primary endpoint at week 12 and every secondary endpoint. Evommune will stop developing it in atopic dermatitis but continue a phase 2b migraine-prevention study.

MRGPRX2 is a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed prominently on mast cells, especially in skin and connective tissues. It can respond to various cationic peptides and drugs, triggering mast-cell degranulation without conventional IgE-mediated allergen recognition. That releases histamine, proteases and other inflammatory mediators capable of activating sensory endings and producing itch.

The hypothesis was biologically coherent. The problem is that it has now failed in two itch-associated diseases.

This rhymes with Celldex’s recent prurigo-nodularis failure, where profound mast-cell depletion did not improve itching. In both cases, pharmacology aimed at mast-cell biology failed to move the clinical phenotype. That does not mean mast cells are irrelevant in every itchy disease. It suggests they may not be the controlling bottleneck in these specific settings, where epithelial barrier dysfunction, type 2 cytokines and sensitized sensory neurons can sustain disease independently.

Migraine is still conceivable because meningeal mast cells sit near trigeminal sensory fibers, and neuropeptides released during migraine can participate in reciprocal mast-cell and neuronal activation. But after two clinical misses, migraine should be viewed as a new high-risk hypothesis, not a casual third indication for a validated mechanism.

Evommune’s more credible priority is now EVO301, an IL-18-binding-protein fusion that succeeded in a phase 2 eczema study and should enter phase 2b in mid-2027. The company has cash into 2029. It also has one program whose biological story just became much harder to defend.

In multiple myeloma, Bristol Myers Squibb says its GPRC5D-directed CAR-T therapy arlo-cel hit the primary endpoint in a pivotal trial of patients previously treated with a BCMA-targeted therapy.

No actual response rate has been disclosed, so celebration should wait for the medical-meeting presentation. The trial met its overall-response endpoint and a key secondary endpoint measuring complete responses.

GPRC5D provides an alternative address after myeloma escapes BCMA-targeted CAR-T, bispecific antibodies or ADCs. It is highly expressed on malignant plasma cells, but also appears in some normal keratinized tissues. That explains class-associated problems involving taste, skin and nails: the immune system is attacking the target it was given, just not exclusively on the tumor.

Johnson & Johnson’s GPRC5D-by-CD3 bispecific Talvey produced a 72% response rate in a broadly similar setting. Arlo-cel’s possible advantage is a one-time cellular treatment rather than continuous bispecific dosing, potentially reducing the duration of on-target toxicity. Its disadvantage is personalized manufacturing and the acute risks of CAR-T.

This is oncology, so the risk tolerance differs sharply from the autoimmune CAR-T programs paused after recent inflammatory deaths. In heavily pretreated myeloma, a high response rate can justify substantial acute toxicity. BMS still needs controlled evidence of superiority, and it has a phase 3 trial reporting in 2028. Until then, a pivotal “win” without numbers is more regulatory signal than clinical conclusion.

Inhibrx has supplied much richer numbers for its OX40 agonist INBRX-106 in head-and-neck cancer.

Among 68 patients with high PD-L1 expression, INBRX-106 plus Keytruda produced a 48.3% objective response rate, versus 26.5% for Keytruda alone. Median progression-free survival was 9.6 months versus 4.9 months, and six-month progression-free survival was 72.4% versus 42.8%.

OX40 is a costimulatory receptor expressed after T-cell activation. Engaging it can promote T-cell survival, proliferation and effector function. INBRX-106 is hexavalent, meaning it presents six binding sites, encouraging the receptor clustering required for productive signaling. It is essentially trying to press the accelerator while Keytruda releases the PD-1 brake.

The most dramatic result came from HPV-positive tumors: an 80% response rate with the combination versus 33.3% with Keytruda. HPV-positive cancers express virally derived antigens, making them potentially more visible to T cells. OX40 stimulation may therefore amplify an immune response that already has recognizable targets.

That subgroup is also small, and small subgroups are where percentages acquire theatrical confidence. Inhibrx is adding 50 HPV-positive patients and discussing accelerated approval. Randomization against Keytruda makes the evidence stronger than an uncontrolled response-rate study, but the FDA may still want a larger dataset and clear durability. The signal is excellent. The CEO calling it “profound” does not complete the filing.

Pharvaris, meanwhile, has produced one of today’s cleanest clinical results. Once-daily oral deucrictibant reduced hereditary-angioedema attacks by 83% versus placebo over 24 weeks. Among patients with the classical type 1 or type 2 forms, the reduction was 87%.

Deucrictibant blocks the bradykinin B2 receptor. In hereditary angioedema, inadequate C1-inhibitor function allows excessive kallikrein activity and bradykinin production. Bradykinin then acts on vascular endothelium, making vessels leaky and producing swelling that can affect the limbs, gut, face or airway.

Most effective prophylactic therapies are injected. Pharvaris is trying to build both immediate-release rescue and extended-release preventive formulations around the same oral molecule. If approved, that could give patients one mechanistic franchise for daily prevention and acute attacks.

This result matters in the context of ADARx’s prekallikrein siRNA and Intellia’s in vivo gene-editing program. Genetic and RNA approaches may offer extremely long duration. Deucrictibant offers something different: strong oral efficacy with reversibility. If adverse effects or life circumstances change, a pill can be stopped. Permanent editing cannot.

Roivant also has an unusually strong phase 2 result from a cheaply licensed asset. The company paid Bayer just $14 million upfront for mosliciguat, an inhaled soluble-guanylate-cyclase activator.

In pulmonary hypertension associated with interstitial lung disease, mosliciguat reduced pulmonary vascular resistance by a placebo-adjusted 56.3%, improved six-minute walking distance by 35.2 meters and reduced NT-proBNP, a marker of cardiac strain, by 53.2%.

Those are internally consistent hemodynamic, functional and biomarker effects. One daily inhalation could also be easier than current inhaled prostacyclin regimens. Bayer retained potential milestones, but $14 million upfront for this phase 2 package now looks like excellent asset shopping. Phase 3 still needs clinical outcomes and durable safety; for the moment, Roivant appears to have bought genuine pharmacology before the market recognized it.

Finally, Novo Nordisk has terminated two more phase 3 trials of the IL-6 antibody ziltivekimab.

We covered the first collapse in August: in more than 6,300 high-risk cardiovascular patients, ziltivekimab produced a hazard ratio of 0.99—no reduction in cardiovascular events. Now independent monitors have concluded that two heart-failure trials enrolling more than 5,500 patients are unlikely to produce a different answer.

One post-heart-attack trial continues, but the former $3 billion opportunity is close to disappearing.

This is more than one antibody having a bad quarter. IL-6 and C-reactive protein identify inflammatory cardiovascular risk, yet suppressing that pathway has repeatedly failed to change outcomes here. It also casts a shadow over upstream NLRP3-inflammasome programs. Not every NLRP3 drug will reproduce ziltivekimab’s biology, but developers can no longer assume that lowering IL-6-associated inflammation will protect the heart.

Today’s sharpest contrast is between molecular activity and functional consequence. Del-desiran can embody sophisticated muscle-targeted RNA delivery and still fail hand opening. An MRGPRX2 antagonist can be tolerable and still leave eczema unchanged. Ziltivekimab can suppress inflammatory signaling without preventing hospitalization or death.

Then there are the programs where several layers line up: deucrictibant reduced attacks by 83%, mosliciguat improved hemodynamics and walking distance, and INBRX-106 nearly doubled both response rate and median progression-free survival.

Mechanistic elegance earns attention. Convergent clinical outcomes earn the next trial.

And that’s the roundup for today.
